Negative Declaratory Action on Negotiable Instruments in Türkiye: Promissory Notes, Cheques, Bills of Exchange

Short answer: Under Turkish law, the negative declaratory action on a negotiable instrument is the action by which the debtor asks the court to establish that they are not indebted under a cheque, promissory note or bill of exchange, and under Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law Art. 72 it may be brought before enforcement, during enforcement or after the enforcement has become final. The action does not automatically stay enforcement: if it is brought before enforcement, the court may issue an interim injunction staying enforcement against security of at least 15% of the claim, whereas if it is brought after enforcement, the debtor may only ask that the money held by the enforcement office not be paid to the creditor. As a rule, the debtor who alleges want of consideration on a promissory note must prove it, and claims that the instrument was given as security or filled in contrary to the agreement must be proven by written evidence under the rule of proof by deed, not by witnesses. Forgery may be raised against anyone, but personal defences such as want of consideration cannot be raised against a good-faith holder unless the holder knowingly acted to the debtor’s detriment when acquiring the instrument (TCC Art. 687). The action is heard by the commercial court, applying to a mediator before suing is a precondition to suit (TCC Art. 5/A), and if the enforcement proves unjustified and in bad faith, the creditor is ordered, at the debtor’s request, to pay bad-faith compensation of at least 20% of the claim.

Negotiable instruments such as cheques, promissory notes and bills of exchange give the creditor a fast and strict means of collection (enforcement proceedings special to negotiable instruments). However, where the instrument is not in fact based on a debt, where the debt has been paid, where the instrument was given as security, or where it was obtained by fraud, duress or forgery, the debtor’s principal legal weapon is the negative declaratory action. This action allows the debtor to have a court establish that “I am not indebted under this instrument”. Below we examine when and where the action is brought, the conditions for staying enforcement, the abstract nature of the instrument, who bears the burden of proof, the defences available, and the settled criteria of the Court of Cassation.

What Is a Negative Declaratory Action on a Negotiable Instrument?

A negative declaratory action is not an action for performance but for a declaration that a legal relationship (a debt) which the defendant alleges to exist does not exist. As to a negotiable instrument, it establishes that the debtor is not indebted under it. The action is regulated in Article 72 of the Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law and may be brought before enforcement, during enforcement or after enforcement. Even where the proceedings have become final, the debtor may assert through this action that, as a matter of substantive law, it is not indebted.

Relevant provision — Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law No. 2004, Art. 72 (Negative declaratory and recovery action) (unofficial translation)

“The debtor may bring a negative declaratory action, before or during enforcement, to prove that it is not indebted. … The court hearing a negative declaratory action brought before enforcement may, upon request, against security of not less than fifteen percent of the claim, grant an interim injunction staying the enforcement. In a negative declaratory action brought after enforcement, the enforcement may not be stayed by way of interim injunction. However, against security of not less than fifteen percent of the claim and to cover damages arising from delay, the debtor may request the court, by way of interim injunction, that the money in the enforcement office not be paid to the creditor. … If the debtor has not obtained an injunction in the negative declaratory action and the debt has been paid, the action continues as an action for recovery. … Negative declaratory and recovery actions may be brought in the court of the place of the enforcement office conducting the proceedings, or in the court of the defendant’s domicile.”

Staying Enforcement: Security and Interim Injunction

Bringing the action does not by itself stay enforcement. What is decisive is whether the action was filed before or after enforcement began:

If filed before enforcement: the court may, on the debtor’s request, grant an interim injunction staying the enforcement against security of at least 15% of the claim.

If filed after enforcement: the enforcement may not be stayed by injunction. The debtor may only request — again against at least 15% security and to cover delay damages — that the money in the enforcement office not be paid to the creditor. Enforcement continues and assets may be sold, but the proceeds are held until the action concludes. In practice this distinction is crucial; a debtor who sees the instrument is well advised to act, where possible, before enforcement begins.

The Abstract Nature of the Instrument and the Promissory Note

Negotiable instruments contain an abstract (mücerret) acknowledgment of debt. This means that the debt under the instrument is independent of the underlying relationship (sale, lease, loan, etc.) that gave rise to it. Even if the underlying relationship is void or extinguished, the instrument retains its validity under the law of negotiable instruments; this gives the debtor the possibility of raising a defence based on the defect in the underlying relationship and of relying on unjust enrichment. A promissory note (bono) is a negotiable instrument in the nature of a promise to pay and, to be valid, must bear the mandatory elements set out in the law.

Relevant provision — Turkish Commercial Code No. 6102, Art. 776 (Elements of the promissory note) (unofficial translation)

“A promissory note (bono) or note to order shall contain: a) the word ‘bono’ or ‘note to order’ in the text of the instrument (or its equivalent if written in another language); b) an unconditional promise to pay a definite sum; c) the maturity; d) the place of payment; e) the name of the person to whom or to whose order payment is to be made; f) the date and place of issue; g) the signature of the issuer.”

Many provisions on bills of exchange apply directly to promissory notes unless contrary to their nature. In particular, Article 687 on defences, falling within Articles 681–690 on the endorsement of bills of exchange, also applies to promissory notes.

Relevant provision — Turkish Commercial Code No. 6102, Art. 778 (Applicable provisions) (unofficial translation)

“Unless contrary to the nature of the promissory note, the provisions on: a) endorsement of bills of exchange, Articles 681 to 690, … also apply to promissory notes.”

Burden of Proof, Want of Consideration and Proof by Deed

The burden of proof in a negative declaratory action depends on the type of the claimant’s assertion. If the debtor merely says “no debt ever arose”, the burden, as a rule, is on the creditor, because it is the creditor who asserts the existence of the relationship. But if the debtor accepts that the relationship arose and asserts that the instrument is without consideration, or that the debt was paid, released or set off, the burden of proving these assertions shifts to the debtor (claimant). Owing to the abstract nature of the instrument, in a promissory note the party alleging want of consideration bears the burden, as a rule. If the instrument states that value was received “in goods” or “in cash” and one party asserts the contrary (the talil of the instrument), the burden shifts to the party asserting the contrary of that statement.

Another critical rule: assertions against the instrument — want of consideration, that it is a security note, that it was completed contrary to the agreement — are, as a rule, proven by deed (written evidence); proof by witness is not possible. It is therefore decisive for the debtor to assemble payment receipts, reconciliations, correspondence and similar written documents.

Which Defences May Be Raised? Forgery, Defect of Will, Good-Faith Holder

In the law of negotiable instruments defences are of two kinds. Absolute defences (assertable against everyone) — for example forgery of the instrument, that the signature is not the debtor’s, or the absence of mandatory elements — may be raised against anyone, regardless of who holds the instrument. Personal defences (want of consideration, payment, security, fraud/duress) may, as a rule, be raised only between the parties to the instrument.

Relevant provision — Turkish Commercial Code No. 6102, Art. 687 (Defences) (unofficial translation)

“A person against whom a claim is made on a bill of exchange may not set up against the holder defences founded on the relations existing directly between that person and the drawer or a previous holder, unless the holder, in acquiring the bill, has knowingly acted to the detriment of the debtor.”

The practical consequence is this: if the instrument has been endorsed by the payee to a good-faith third party, the debtor may not, as a rule, raise against that holder the personal defences (e.g. want of consideration) existing between itself and the payee. But if the holder acquired the instrument knowingly to the debtor’s detriment, personal defences may be raised against it too. Allegations of defect of will — that the instrument was signed under fraud or duress — may also be raised in the action; but such allegations must be proven by concrete, definite and sufficient evidence.

Action for Recovery and Bad-Faith Compensation

If the debtor has brought a negative declaratory action but obtained no injunction and has meanwhile been compelled (under threat of enforcement) to pay, the action converts of itself into an action for recovery of the sum paid. A person compelled to pay a sum not owed — because no objection was raised to the proceedings or the objection was set aside — may also bring an action for recovery in the general courts within one year of the date of payment.

If the negative declaratory or recovery action concludes in the debtor’s favour and the enforcement is found to have been unjust and in bad faith, the creditor is, on the debtor’s request, ordered to pay bad-faith compensation of at least 20% of the claim. Conversely, if the action concludes in the creditor’s favour, a debtor found to be in bad faith may likewise be ordered to pay at least 20%; the seriousness of the allegations and the evidence must therefore be assessed carefully before filing.

Competent Court and Venue

Negative declaratory and recovery actions arising from a negotiable instrument (promissory note, cheque, bill of exchange) are commercial actions — even if the parties are not merchants — and are heard in the commercial court of first instance. In commercial cases, negative declaratory and recovery actions require, as a precondition to suit, an application to a mediator before filing (TCC Art. 5/A(1); these actions were added to the provision by Law No. 7445). The 11th Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation upheld a regional court of appeal decision dismissing on procedural grounds, for lack of this precondition, a negative declaratory action brought without mediation against enforcement based on a promissory note (11th Civil Chamber, E. 2026/6092, K. 2026/4300, 07.09.2026). As to venue, under the last paragraph of Art. 72 the action may be brought in the court of the place of the enforcement office conducting the proceedings or of the defendant’s (creditor’s) domicile. Determining the correct court and venue at the outset prevents dismissal on procedural grounds and loss of time.

Decisions of the Court of Cassation

The decisions below concern the allocation of the burden of proof in a negative declaratory action, want of consideration and talil in a promissory note, the nature of the want-of-consideration defence as a personal defence under TCC Art. 687, proof by deed (the bar on proof by witness), the assertability of forgery against everyone, the proof of defect-of-will allegations, and bad-faith compensation under Art. 72/5. Negative declaratory actions on negotiable instruments are heard, in the Court’s current division of work, by the 11th Civil Chamber and, on questions of principle, by the General Assembly of Civil Chambers. All the decisions were verified in full text from the official case-law database.

Allocation of the burden of proof; want of consideration and talil in a promissory note

Court of Cassation
General Assembly of Civil Chambers, 2024/345 E., 2025/665 K., 22.10.2025

“In a negative declaratory action the burden of proof, as a rule, falls on the defendant creditor. If the claimant (debtor) merely denies the legal relationship (e.g. the debt) whose existence the defendant (creditor) asserts — that is, asserts that the relationship (the debt) never arose — the burden is on the defendant. … But if the claimant debtor, rather than asserting that the relationship never arose, states that it did arise but is void or has been extinguished for another reason, the burden of proving this falls on the claimant. For example, the burden of proving that the instrument relied on by the creditor is without consideration is on the claimant debtor. … Where want of consideration is alleged in a promissory note, the burden of proof, as a rule, is on the party alleging that the instrument is without consideration. However, if the cause of the debt is stated in the instrument as ‘goods’ or ‘cash’, the claimant being entitled to rely on the written cause of the debt, the burden falls on the party asserting the contrary.” (unofficial translation)

Burden on the debtor; where payment is proven by admission the action is allowed, and bad faith triggers Art. 72/5 compensation (determination upheld)

Court of Cassation (upheld regional appeal court reasoning)
11th Civil Chamber, 2025/4662 E., 2026/1392 K., 09.03.2026

“… in negative declaratory actions arising from a negotiable instrument the burden of proof is on the claimant debtor; … pursuant to Art. 188/1 of the Code of Civil Procedure No. 6100, the claimants proved, by the defendant’s admission, that the payments in question related to the instruments at issue; … since, despite the payments, the defendant initiated proceedings as if no payment had been made and was therefore in bad faith, it was also erroneous not to award bad-faith compensation against the defendant under Art. 72/5 of the Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law No. 2004.” (unofficial translation)

Forgery is assertable against everyone; an allegation of a security note or wrongful completion must be proven by deed, not by witness (determination upheld)

Court of Cassation (first-instance reasoning kept on appeal; upheld)
11th Civil Chamber, 2025/3619 E., 2026/1194 K., 02.03.2026

“… since forgery is among the objections that may be raised against everyone, this claimant’s negative declaratory claim was made out on the record; … although it was alleged that the note was a security note, this could be proven by written evidence, there was no such notation on the note and no written evidence was produced; … the allegation that it was completed contrary to the agreement also required proof by deed; … these matters could not be proven by witness under the rule of proof by deed; … the burden of proof was on the claimant.” (unofficial translation)

An allegation of fraud and duress (defect of will) must be proven by concrete, definite and sufficient evidence (determination upheld)

Court of Cassation (upheld regional appeal court reasoning)
11th Civil Chamber, 2025/4255 E., 2026/1438 K., 11.03.2026

“… in the present case the assertion that the note was given under economic distress or by fraud and duress could not be proven by concrete, definite and sufficient evidence, and there was no impropriety in the first-instance court’s assessment of the evidence and characterisation of the legal relationship.” (unofficial translation)

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a negative declaratory action on a negotiable instrument, and when is it brought?

It is the action by which the debtor asks the court to establish that it is not indebted under a cheque/promissory note/bill of exchange. Under Art. 72 of the Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law it may be brought before enforcement, during enforcement, or after the proceedings have become final.

Does bringing the action stay the enforcement?

Not automatically. If the action was filed before enforcement, an injunction staying it may be granted against security of at least 15% of the claim. If filed after enforcement, it cannot be stayed; the debtor may only request, against at least 15% security, that the money in the enforcement office not be paid to the creditor.

Who proves want of consideration in a promissory note?

As a rule, the party alleging it — the debtor. If the instrument states value received “in goods” or “in cash” and one party asserts the contrary (talil), the burden shifts to the party asserting the contrary of that statement.

Can I prove want of consideration by witness?

As a rule, no. Want of consideration, that the note was given as security, or that it was completed contrary to the agreement must, under the rule of proof by deed, be proven by written evidence; a witness alone is insufficient. Payment receipts, reconciliations and correspondence are critical.

I signed the instrument under fraud or duress; what can I do?

An allegation of fraud or duress (defect of will) may be raised in the action, but it must be proven by concrete, definite and sufficient evidence. Forgery of the instrument, or that the signature is not yours, is an absolute defence assertable against everyone.

Can I sue after paying the debt? Is there bad-faith compensation?

Yes. If you were compelled to pay without an injunction, the action converts into a recovery action; you may also bring a recovery action within one year of payment. If the enforcement is found to be unjust and in bad faith, the creditor is ordered to pay bad-faith compensation of at least 20% of the claim.
