Trademark Infringement in Türkiye (IPC Art. 29): Infringing Acts, Defences, Injunction, Removal, Damages

Short answer: Under Turkish law, the following acts constitute trademark infringement (Industrial Property Code art. 29). These acts are: using a trademark without the proprietor’s permission in the ways set out in Industrial Property Code art. 7 (such as using an identical sign, or a similar sign giving rise to a likelihood of confusion), imitating the trademark, selling, distributing or importing goods while knowing or being expected to know that they bear an imitated mark, and extending licensed rights without permission. The proprietor may seek a declaration of infringement, prevention, cessation and removal of the infringement, compensation for material and moral damage, seizure of the products and publication of the final judgment (art. 149). In an infringement action brought by the holder of an earlier right, the defendant cannot rely on its own later registration as a defence (art. 155), but it may raise the non-use plea and acquiescence. The actions are heard by the intellectual and industrial property civil court or, where none has been set up, by the civil court of first instance designated by the Council of Judges and Prosecutors (art. 156/1); infringement by copying or by creating confusion is also an offence prosecuted on complaint (art. 30).

This article explains Turkish law. It is an English translation of the Turkish original; in case of any discrepancy, the Turkish text prevails.

In brief

Trademark infringement under the Turkish Industrial Property Code (Law No. 6769): the scope of trademark rights (art. 7), infringing acts (art. 29), likelihood of confusion and use as a trademark, trade names and online use, why a later registration is no defence (art. 155), the non-use plea, acquiescence (art. 25/6 and Civil Code art. 2), the relationship with unfair competition, claims for declaration, injunction, removal, seizure and publication (art. 149), damages and the calculation of lost profit (arts. 150-151), jurisdiction, mediation, limitation, interim measures (art. 159), criminal liability (art. 30), proof and verified Court of Cassation decisions.

A guide to trademark infringement under the Industrial Property Code No. 6769 (IPC): scope of the right, infringing acts (Industrial Property Code art. 29), likelihood of confusion, defences, unfair competition, claims and damages (Industrial Property Code art. 149-151), jurisdiction, interim measures, criminal liability and verified Court of Cassation decisions.

Trademark Rights and Their Scope (IPC art. 7)

A trademark is any sign capable of distinguishing the goods or services of one undertaking from those of others and of being represented on the register (Industrial Property Code art. 4). Protection is acquired through registration (Industrial Property Code art. 7/1) and covers identical use, similar uses causing a likelihood of confusion, and uses that exploit or harm the reputation of a well-known mark.

Statutory Provision — Industrial Property Code (Law No. 6769) art. 7/2-3 (Scope of and limitations on the rights conferred by registration) (unofficial translation)

“(2) The rights arising from trademark registration belong exclusively to the trademark proprietor. The proprietor is entitled to request the prevention of the following acts where they are carried out without permission: a) Use of any sign identical with the registered trademark in relation to goods or services covered by the registration. b) Use of any sign which is identical with or similar to the registered trademark and covers goods or services identical with or similar to those covered by the registered trademark, and which therefore gives rise to a likelihood of confusion on the part of the public, including the likelihood of association with the registered trademark. c) Use, without due cause, of any sign identical with or similar to the registered trademark, irrespective of whether it is used for identical, similar or different goods or services, which, owing to the level of recognition the trademark has attained in Türkiye, would take unfair advantage of the reputation of the trademark, harm its reputation or impair its distinctive character. (3) The following may be prohibited under paragraph two where the sign is used in the course of trade: a) Affixing the sign to the goods or their packaging. … ç) Using the sign on business papers and in advertising. d) Using an identical or similar sign on the internet, in a manner creating a commercial effect, as a domain name, routing code, keyword or in a similar form, provided that the user has no right or legitimate connection relating to the use of the sign. e) Using the sign as a trade name or business name. f) Using the sign in comparative advertising in an unlawful manner.”

The rights take effect against third parties from publication of the registration; damages may be claimed for acts after the application is published in the Bulletin, but the court may not decide before the registration is published (Industrial Property Code art. 7/4). Honest use in line with ordinary commercial practice cannot be prevented where natural persons indicate their own name or address, where descriptive indications are given, or where it is necessary to indicate the intended purpose, particularly of accessories, spare parts or equivalent parts (Industrial Property Code art. 7/5).

Acts Deemed to Infringe Trademark Rights (IPC art. 29)

Industrial Property Code art. 29/1-a refers to the uses listed in Industrial Property Code art. 7, so the two provisions are applied together.

Statutory Provision — Industrial Property Code (Law No. 6769) art. 29 (Acts deemed to infringe trademark rights) (unofficial translation)

“(1) The following acts constitute infringement of trademark rights: a) Using the trademark, without the proprietor’s permission, in the forms set out in article 7. b) Imitating the trademark, without the proprietor’s permission, by using the trademark or a sign so similar as to be indistinguishable from it. c) Selling, distributing, otherwise placing on the market, subjecting to import procedures, exporting, holding for commercial purposes or offering to enter into contracts concerning products bearing a trademark used through infringement, while knowing or being in a position to know that the trademark has been imitated by use of the trademark or a sign so similar as to be indistinguishable from it. ç) Extending without permission the rights granted by the proprietor under a licence, or transferring those rights to third parties. (2) The provision of paragraph two of article 19 may be raised as a plea in infringement actions. In that case, the date of the action is taken as the basis for determining the five-year period relating to use.”

Sellers, distributors and importers are liable if they knew or should have known of the imitation (Industrial Property Code art. 29/1-c). Liability may reach a non-user: the first-instance court found infringement by a defendant that had transferred the right to use its later marks to another firm, as the third-party production rested on those marks, and the regional court judgment accepting the claim was upheld (Court of Cassation 11th Civil Chamber, E. 2024/4566, K. 2025/2608).

Exhaustion: Acts concerning goods put on the market by the right holder or with its consent fall outside the right, but the proprietor may prevent commercial use of goods that have been altered or impaired (Industrial Property Code art. 152). No action or complaint lies against a person holding or using infringing goods for personal needs (Industrial Property Code art. 153/1).

Likelihood of Confusion and Use as a Trademark

Most disputes turn on the likelihood of confusion under Industrial Property Code art. 7/2-b. For the Civil General Assembly, it is both a bar to registration and an act of infringement and requires identity or similarity of both the signs and the goods or services; the test is the average consumer, and an assumed economic link between the undertakings suffices (Court of Cassation Civil General Assembly, E. 2024/376, K. 2025/798).

Marks are compared as a whole; a regional court judgment upholding a dismissal was reversed by majority where phonetic, visual and aural similarity created a risk of confusion (Court of Cassation 11th Civil Chamber, E. 2024/2460, K. 2025/1301). Even with a weak dominant element, where similarity is high and the added word contributes no distinctiveness, consumers may assume a line extension or a link between the undertakings; on that basis the first-instance court invalidated the later mark and found infringement through online product promotion, and the regional court judgment dismissing the appeal was upheld (Court of Cassation 11th Civil Chamber, E. 2025/27, K. 2025/4521).

Use as a trademark depends on the average consumer’s perception and may also arise from advertisements, catalogues and business documents (Court of Cassation Civil General Assembly, E. 2024/376, K. 2025/798). Use as a trade name or business name may be prohibited (Industrial Property Code art. 7/3-e); according to the Civil General Assembly, what matters is whether trademark value is attributed to the name (Court of Cassation Civil General Assembly, E. 2024/376, K. 2025/798). In an upheld regional court judgment, using the registered trade name and a matching domain name was lawful, while highlighting the core element of the name in larger letters and another colour and font on business papers and the website infringed (Court of Cassation 11th Civil Chamber, E. 2025/1561, K. 2025/5659).

Defences: Earlier Rights, Non-Use and Acquiescence

A later registration is no defence (IPC art. 155)

The defendant’s own registered mark does not protect it against the holder of an earlier right:

Statutory Provision — Industrial Property Code (Law No. 6769) art. 155 (Effect of earlier rights) (unofficial translation)

“(1) The holder of a trademark, patent or design right may not invoke its industrial property right as a defence in an infringement action brought by right holders whose priority or application date is earlier than that of its own right.”

In view of this provision, the 11th Civil Chamber has stated that it can no longer maintain its earlier case law that, absent a bad-faith registration, “use of a registered trademark during the term of registration does not constitute infringement” (Court of Cassation 11th Civil Chamber, E. 2021/2797, K. 2022/7133). The earlier right holder may also seek invalidation of the later mark (Industrial Property Code art. 25/1, Industrial Property Code art. 6/1) against the registered proprietor; the Turkish Patent and Trademark Office is not a party (Industrial Property Code art. 25/3).

The non-use plea (IPC art. 29/2)

The defendant may raise the proof-of-use rule of Industrial Property Code art. 19/2 as a plea, the five-year period being determined by the date of the action (Industrial Property Code art. 29/2). The plea requires that the mark relied on has been registered for at least five years; the proprietor must then prove genuine use in Türkiye for the goods or services relied on, or proper reasons for non-use (Industrial Property Code art. 19/2). Use in a form not altering the distinctive character and use with consent count as use (Industrial Property Code art. 9/2-3); a mark not genuinely used for five years without proper reason may also be revoked by the Office on request (Industrial Property Code art. 9/1, Industrial Property Code art. 26/1-a).

Loss of rights through acquiescence (IPC art. 25/6, Civil Code art. 2)

The Code expressly regulates acquiescence only for invalidity:

Statutory Provision — Industrial Property Code (Law No. 6769) art. 25/6 (Grounds for invalidity and request for invalidation) (unofficial translation)

“(6) Where the trademark proprietor, while knowing or being in a position to know that a later trademark is being used, has acquiesced in this situation for a period of five successive years, it may not rely on its trademark as a ground for invalidity unless the registration of the later trademark was made in bad faith.”

For infringement claims, the Court of Cassation bases acquiescence on the good-faith rule in Civil Code art. 2. According to the Civil General Assembly, the earlier holder must have knowingly stayed silent for an acceptable period and the later user must be in good faith; a justified cause such as force majeure excludes the loss. In that Decree-Law No. 556 case, as no statutory period existed, the Assembly held that the period must be determined on the facts; the defence is an objection, not a plea, and is considered ex officio where it appears from the file (Court of Cassation Civil General Assembly, E. 2024/376, K. 2025/798).

The defendant bears the burden: trade-name use on a tax certificate, invoices and reports did not prove use as a trademark (Court of Cassation Civil General Assembly, E. 2024/376, K. 2025/798), and acquiescence failed where it could not be established when the claimant knew or should have known of the use (Court of Cassation 11th Civil Chamber, E. 2025/1561, K. 2025/5659).

Relationship with Unfair Competition

Under the repealed Commercial Code No. 6762, infringement was also protected cumulatively as unfair competition. Noting that Commercial Code art. 55/1-a-4 deliberately omits “name, trade name and trademark”, the 11th Civil Chamber abandoned that case law and reversed a judgment treating use of a similar mark on goods within the registration as additional unfair competition (Court of Cassation 11th Civil Chamber, E. 2019/5189, K. 2022/1852). A regional court judgment to the same effect was upheld (Court of Cassation 11th Civil Chamber, E. 2025/1561, K. 2025/5659).

The Civil General Assembly, however, stated in the general part of a decision concerning the Decree-Law No. 556 period that the infringing act also constitutes unfair competition because it causes confusion (Court of Cassation Civil General Assembly, E. 2024/376, K. 2025/798). An unfair competition claim should therefore rest on facts going beyond the registration and falling within Commercial Code art. 55.

Infringement Actions and Claims (IPC art. 149)

The claims are declaration, injunction, cessation, removal, damages, seizure, ownership, destruction and publication of the judgment, usually combined in one action.

Statutory Provision — Industrial Property Code (Law No. 6769) art. 149/1 (Claims of the holder of an infringed industrial property right) (unofficial translation)

“(1) A right holder whose industrial property right has been infringed may request the following from the court: a) A determination of whether the act constitutes infringement. b) Prevention of a likely infringement. c) Cessation of the infringing acts. ç) Removal of the infringement and compensation for pecuniary and non-pecuniary damage. d) Seizure of the products constituting infringement or requiring a penalty, and of the means such as devices and machines used exclusively in their production, in a manner that does not prevent the production of products other than the infringing products. … g) Where there is a justified reason or interest, publication of the final judgment in full or in summary in a daily newspaper or similar media, or its notification to those concerned, at the expense of the other party.”

The shortened sub-paragraphs (e) and (f) cover ownership of seized goods and means and, at the infringer’s expense, alteration, removal of the marks or, if unavoidable, destruction; the value of goods awarded is deducted from damages (Industrial Property Code art. 149/2). The publication right lapses unless requested within three months after the judgment becomes final (Industrial Property Code art. 149/3).

Unless otherwise agreed, an exclusive licensee may bring the right holder’s actions in its own name; a non-exclusive licensee whose right to sue is not contractually restricted must first ask the right holder and may sue itself if refused or if no action is brought within three months (Industrial Property Code art. 158/1-2). Any interested person not already sued for infringement may seek a declaration of non-infringement (Industrial Property Code art. 154/1).

Damages and Loss of Profit (IPC art. 150-151)

The infringer must compensate the loss (Industrial Property Code art. 150/1). If the reputation of the right suffers from misuse, poor production or inappropriate marketing of the goods, additional compensation for loss of reputation may be claimed (Industrial Property Code art. 150/2), and the court may be asked to order production of documents on the use of the right (Industrial Property Code art. 150/3). Non-pecuniary damages are also available (Industrial Property Code art. 149/1).

Statutory Provision — Industrial Property Code (Law No. 6769) art. 151/1-4 (Loss of profit) (unofficial translation)

“(1) The damage suffered by the right holder covers the actual loss and the loss of profit. (2) The loss of profit is calculated, at the choice of the injured right holder, by one of the following methods: a) The probable income the right holder could have earned had there been no competition from the infringer. b) The net profit earned by the infringer. c) The licence fee the infringer would have had to pay had it used the right lawfully under a licence agreement. (3) In calculating the loss of profit, factors such as, in particular, the economic importance of the industrial property right, or the number, duration and type of licences relating to the right at the time of the infringement, and the nature and extent of the infringement are taken into account. (4) Where one of the methods in sub-paragraphs (a) or (b) of paragraph two has been chosen, if the court finds that the industrial property right was a determining factor in creating demand for the product, it shall decide to add an equitable share in calculating the profit.”

The right holder chooses the method, but the court may adjust the amount whatever the method by the factors in Industrial Property Code art. 151/3, including the reputation of the mark, the degree of similarity, the form and duration of use and whether the mark used by the defendant is registered in its name (Court of Cassation 11th Civil Chamber, E. 2021/2797, K. 2022/7133).

Under the licence-fee method the basis is the turnover from the infringing mark alone. A judgment was reversed that applied discretionary assessment under Code of Obligations art. 50-51 before the infringer’s books for the infringement period had been obtained with due warning and examined by experts (Code of Civil Procedure art. 219 et seq., Industrial Property Code art. 150/3) (Court of Cassation 11th Civil Chamber, E. 2023/6730, K. 2024/8662). Where the loss cannot be fully proven, the judge sets it equitably (Code of Obligations art. 50/2); in one case the first-instance court did so because pecuniary loss could not be calculated and added non-pecuniary damages for harm to the mark’s reputation; the regional court judgment accepting the claim was upheld (Court of Cassation 11th Civil Chamber, E. 2024/4566, K. 2025/2608).

Competent Court, Mediation and Limitation

Jurisdiction: Intellectual and industrial property civil and criminal courts hear these cases or, where none exist, the courts of first instance designated by the Council of Judges and Prosecutors (Industrial Property Code art. 156/1).

Venue: Actions by the right holder against third parties may be brought where the claimant is domiciled, where the unlawful act occurred or where its effects were felt (Industrial Property Code art. 156/3). Actions against the right holder are brought at the defendant’s domicile (Industrial Property Code art. 156/5).

Mediation: According to the 11th Civil Chamber, a damages claim subject to mandatory mediation if brought alone is not separately subject to it when joined with a trademark invalidation claim, which is not mediable; the Chamber found that disregarding this was wrong but, as only the defendant had appealed, upheld the regional court judgment in view of the appellant’s position and grounds of appeal (Court of Cassation 11th Civil Chamber, E. 2025/2668, K. 2025/7412); for a damages-only action, the requirement should be checked before filing.

Limitation: The limitation rules of the Code of Obligations apply to private-law claims (Industrial Property Code art. 157). A tort claim for damages is time-barred two years after the injured party learns of the damage and the liable person and in any event ten years after the act, unless criminal law provides a longer period for the act (Code of Obligations art. 72); certain infringements are offences under Industrial Property Code art. 30.

Interim Measures (IPC art. 159)

Interim measures secure the effectiveness of the judgment while the infringement continues:

Statutory Provision — Industrial Property Code (Law No. 6769) art. 159/1 (Request for and nature of interim measures) (unofficial translation)

“(1) Persons entitled to bring an action under this Law may, in order to ensure the effectiveness of the judgment to be rendered, request the court to order interim measures, provided that they prove that the use subject to the action is being carried out within the country in a manner infringing their industrial property rights, or that serious and effective preparations are being made for it.”

They include in particular prevention and cessation, seizure of infringing goods and production means wherever located, including customs and free zones, and security (Industrial Property Code art. 159/2); otherwise the Code of Civil Procedure applies (Industrial Property Code art. 159/3). A licensee facing serious harm may seek measures before the three-month period expires and may then sue (Industrial Property Code art. 158/3).

Criminal Liability (IPC art. 30)

Infringing another’s trademark by copying it or creating confusion while manufacturing, offering, selling, importing, exporting, buying for commercial purposes, holding, transporting or storing goods, or providing services, carries one to three years’ imprisonment and a judicial fine of up to twenty thousand days (Industrial Property Code art. 30/1). Removing the protection notice and unauthorised disposal are also offences (Industrial Property Code art. 30/2-3). The mark must be registered in Türkiye and prosecution requires a complaint (Industrial Property Code art. 30/5-6); a seller of counterfeit goods who discloses their source, enabling the manufacturers to be found and the goods seized, is not punished (Industrial Property Code art. 30/7).

Proof and Evidence

The claimant proves the registration, the defendant’s use (products, packaging, invoices, business papers, website printouts, preliminary evidence records) and the likelihood of confusion, usually through expert reports in the decisions reviewed. The defendant proves its defences, while under the non-use plea the proprietor proves genuine use (Industrial Property Code art. 29/2, Industrial Property Code art. 19/2).

Commercial books are important evidence for damages: the parties must produce documents in their possession on which they or the other party rely (Code of Civil Procedure art. 219), and the infringer’s books and records should be obtained and examined on that basis and under Industrial Property Code art. 150/3 (Court of Cassation 11th Civil Chamber, E. 2023/6730, K. 2024/8662).

Court of Cassation Decisions

A later registration is no defence in an infringement action; similarity is assessed as a whole (IPC art. 155)

Court of Cassation Decision
11th Civil Chamber, E. 2024/2460, K. 2025/1301, 26.02.2025 (reversal reasoning of the Chamber; decided by majority)

“Although the defendant has a registered trademark and trade name, under article 155 of the IPC the defendant may not invoke the rights arising from its later registration as a defence in the infringement action. … when the parties’ marks are compared as a whole, it must be accepted that there is phonetic, visual and aural similarity between them and that this similarity is such as to create a risk of confusion, including the likelihood of association, in the eyes of the average consumer within the meaning of article 6/1 of the IPC.” (translation)

Highlighting the core element of a trade name is infringement; the defendant must prove acquiescence (upheld finding)

Court of Cassation Decision
11th Civil Chamber, E. 2025/1561, K. 2025/5659, 24.09.2025 (finding of the Regional Court of Appeal ruling in compliance with an earlier reversal; upheld by the Chamber)

“although the defendant had no registered trademark as of the date of the action, it used the ‘...’ element of its trade name as a trademark on its business papers in a manner highlighting the ‘...’ element, differently from the form in which the trade name is registered in the trade registry; these uses constituted infringement of the claimant’s trademark right; and the defendant failed to prove that the claimant had lost its rights through acquiescence” (translation)

Loss of rights through acquiescence is founded on Civil Code art. 2; the later user must be in good faith

Court of Cassation Decision
Civil General Assembly, E. 2024/376, K. 2025/798, 10.12.2025 (reasoning of the Civil General Assembly; the judgment of resistance was reversed by majority; the dispute concerns the Decree-Law No. 556 period)

“Loss of rights through acquiescence means that the prior trademark proprietor fails for a long time to exercise its right to sue a person who uses the sign in good faith in the course of trade and, by remaining silent in the face of the infringements, loses its right to protect the trademark. The principle of loss of rights through acquiescence is founded on article 2 of the Civil Code. … For there to be loss of rights through acquiescence, first of all, the person who later registered the trade name and used it as a trademark must be in good faith. … the defence that an action cannot be brought because of acquiescence is not a plea but an objection.” (translation)

Using a similar mark on goods within the registration is not additionally unfair competition (Commercial Code art. 55/1-a-4)

Court of Cassation Decision
11th Civil Chamber, E. 2019/5189, K. 2022/1852, 14.03.2022 (reversal reasoning of the Chamber)

“in view of the protection of trademarks under their own special law, the Industrial Property Code No. 6769 (formerly Decree-Law No. 556), and of the provision in article 55/1-a-4 of the Turkish Commercial Code No. 6102, it no longer being possible for the Chamber to maintain its former case law on this matter, the regional court’s reasoning that the defendant’s act of using a mark similar to the claimant’s registered trademark on pharmaceutical goods within the scope of the registration also constituted unfair competition under article 55/1-a-4 of the Commercial Code was not found correct” (translation)

The court reviews loss of profit by the factors in IPC art. 151/3, whatever method is chosen

Court of Cassation Decision
11th Civil Chamber, E. 2021/2797, K. 2022/7133, 18.10.2022 (reversal reasoning of the Chamber)

“In determining the loss of profit, unlike the previous regulation, courts must determine compensation by taking into account variables such as, in particular, the economic importance of the industrial property right or the number, duration and type of licences relating to it at the time of the infringement, and the nature and extent of the infringement. … whether the mark used by the defendant is registered in its name must also be accepted among the elements affecting the amount of loss of profit. In other words, where these facts exist, whichever option under article 151/2 of the IPC the right holder chooses, the courts may intervene in the amount of the loss of profit.” (translation)

Frequently Asked Questions

Which court hears a trademark infringement action?

The intellectual and industrial property civil court or, where none exists, the designated civil court of first instance (Industrial Property Code art. 156/1); venue lies at the claimant’s domicile or where the act occurred or had its effects (Industrial Property Code art. 156/3).

Can an infringement action be brought if the other party also has a registered trademark?

Yes. A later registration is no defence against the holder of an earlier right (Industrial Property Code art. 155), and the later mark may also be invalidated.

Is using the same word in a trade name trademark infringement?

Use as a trade name may be prohibited (Industrial Property Code art. 7/3-e); what matters is use as a trademark. In an upheld judgment, use of the name as registered was lawful, while highlighting its core element infringed (Court of Cassation 11th Civil Chamber, E. 2025/1561, K. 2025/5659).

Does a proprietor who stays silent for a long time lose its rights?

A proprietor who knew or should have known of a later mark’s use and acquiesced for five successive years cannot rely on its mark for invalidity unless the later registration was in bad faith (Industrial Property Code art. 25/6). For infringement, acquiescence rests on Civil Code art. 2 and requires the later user’s good faith (Court of Cassation Civil General Assembly, E. 2024/376, K. 2025/798); the defendant must prove the conditions (Court of Cassation 11th Civil Chamber, E. 2025/1561, K. 2025/5659).

How are damages for trademark infringement calculated?

Damage comprises actual loss and loss of profit, the latter calculated at the right holder’s choice on probable income, the infringer’s net profit or a licence fee (Industrial Property Code art. 151/2); compensation for loss of reputation (Industrial Property Code art. 150/2) and non-pecuniary damages (Industrial Property Code art. 149/1) may be added.

Is trademark infringement a criminal offence?

Yes. Infringement by copying or creating confusion, such as manufacturing, selling, importing or exporting goods, carries one to three years’ imprisonment and a judicial fine of up to twenty thousand days (Industrial Property Code art. 30/1); the mark must be registered in Türkiye and prosecution requires a complaint (Industrial Property Code art. 30/5-6).
