Reinstatement Action under Turkish Labour Law: Conditions, Valid Reason, Mediation, Time Limits, Compensation

Short answer: Under Turkish law, the reinstatement action is an action to establish the invalidity of the dismissal, brought by an employee within the scope of job security whose indefinite-term contract has been terminated without a valid reason or on a reason that is not valid (Labour Act No. 4857 arts. 18-21). It requires a workplace employing thirty or more employees, at least six months’ seniority and an indefinite-term contract. Applying to a mediator within one month of notification of the dismissal is a condition of action; if no agreement is reached, the action must be brought before the labour court within two weeks of the date of the final minutes. The burden of proving that the dismissal rests on a valid reason lies with the employer. If the dismissal is held invalid and the employee, having applied within ten working days of notification of the final judgment, is not reinstated within one month, compensation for non-reinstatement of four to eight months’ wages and up to four months’ wages for the idle period are paid.

This article explains Turkish law. It is an English translation of the Turkish original; in case of any discrepancy, the Turkish text prevails.

In brief

The legal regime of the reinstatement action (action to establish the invalidity of a dismissal) available to an employee within the scope of job security whose contract has been terminated without a valid reason or on a reason that is not valid: under Articles 18-21 of Labour Act No. 4857, the thresholds of thirty employees and six months' seniority, an indefinite-term contract and the scope of job security; the concept of a valid reason arising from the employee's competence or conduct or from the operational requirements of the undertaking, workplace or work, and its distinction from a just (immediate) cause; judicial review of the employer's operational decision for consistency, arbitrariness and proportionality, and the ultima ratio (dismissal as a last resort) principle; the employer's duty under Art. 19 to give written notice and to obtain the employee's defence; mandatory mediation as a condition of action under Art. 3 of Act No. 7036, application to a mediator within one month of dismissal and the two-week period to sue from the date of the final minutes; the burden of proof resting on the employer under Art. 20/2; and, where the dismissal is invalid, the duty to reinstate, the ten-working-day application period, compensation for non-reinstatement (four to eight months' wages) and up to four months' wages for the idle period. What Is the Reinstatement Action (Invalidity of Dismissal)?

The reinstatement action, governed by Articles 18 to 21 of Labour Act No. 4857, is the action brought by an employee who benefits from job-security provisions where the employer has terminated the indefinite-term employment contract without a valid reason, or on a reason that is not valid. Although it is called an action for “reinstatement”, its legal nature is an action to establish the invalidity of the dismissal; the court declares the dismissal invalid, and reinstatement follows as the statutory consequence of that finding.

The purpose of job security is to protect the economically weaker employee against arbitrary and unjustified dismissals, and to subject the employer's power of dismissal to review by tying it to a reason. For an employee within this scope, dismissal is therefore no longer a free declaration of will but an act that requires a valid reason and is open to judicial review. Failure to state a reason in the notice, or a reason that is untrue or invalid, renders the dismissal invalid.

Conditions of the Action: Who May Bring It?

The action is available only to an employee within the scope of job security. The Act defines this scope by three core criteria: the workplace must employ thirty or more employees, the employee must have at least six months' seniority, and the contract must be of indefinite term. For employees working underground, the six-month seniority requirement is not sought.

Relevant Provision — Labour Act No. 4857, Art. 18/1 (unofficial translation)

“An employer who terminates the indefinite-term employment contract of an employee having at least six months' seniority, in a workplace employing thirty or more employees, must rely on a valid reason arising from the competence or conduct of the employee, or from the requirements of the undertaking, the workplace or the work. (Added sentence: 10/9/2014-6552/2) The seniority requirement is not sought for employees working underground.”

The thirty-employee threshold is, as a rule, determined by the number of employees at the time notice of dismissal is given. Where the employer has more than one workplace in the same branch of activity, the number of employees is determined by the total number employed in those workplaces. The six-month seniority is calculated by aggregating the periods spent in one or various workplaces of the same employer, and periods deemed to be working time under Art. 66 are taken into account. According to the settled practice of the Court of Cassation, the six-month period must be complete on the date the notice of dismissal reaches the employee; it is not sufficient for the period to be completed at the end of the notice terms.

Those outside the scope of job security are also expressly identified: Articles 18, 19 and 21, and the last paragraph of Art. 25, do not apply to the employer's representative and assistants who manage the entire undertaking, nor to the employer's representatives who manage the entire workplace and have authority to hire and dismiss employees. Employees on fixed-term contracts likewise do not, as a rule, benefit from this security. Nevertheless, it is accepted that provisions creating exceptions and narrowing the scope must be interpreted restrictively.

What Is a Valid Reason? Distinction from Just Cause

A valid reason is one arising from the employee's competence or conduct, or from the requirements of the undertaking, the workplace or the work, which makes it unreasonable to expect the employer to continue the employment relationship. A valid reason is less grave than the just cause governed by Art. 25, which permits immediate (notice-free) termination. Just cause points to a serious fact that makes the relationship intolerable and justifies immediate termination, whereas a valid reason is a less grave one that justifies terminating the contract by notice, observing the notice periods. A fact may constitute a valid reason even if it does not reach the level of a just cause.

The Act also lists, by way of example, the matters that do not constitute a valid reason for dismissal; these are safeguard norms protecting the essence of job security:

Relevant Provision — Labour Act No. 4857, Art. 18/3 (unofficial translation)

“In particular, the following do not constitute a valid reason for dismissal: a) union membership, or participation in union activities outside working hours or, with the employer's consent, within working hours; b) acting as a workplace union representative; c) applying to administrative or judicial authorities against the employer to pursue rights or fulfil obligations arising from legislation or contract, or participating in a process initiated in this respect; d) race, colour, sex, marital status, family responsibilities, pregnancy, birth, religion, political opinion and similar grounds; e) not attending work during the periods, provided for in Article 74, in which the employment of female employees is prohibited; f) temporary absence during the waiting period provided for in Article 25(I)(b) due to illness or accident.”

Valid reasons arising from the employee's conduct are behaviours contrary to the contract that adversely affect harmony at the workplace and the performance of the work (e.g., frequent lateness, incompatibility with colleagues, failure to carry out assigned tasks). Reasons arising from the employee's competence are facts such as low productivity, inability to adapt, or professional inadequacy. Reasons arising from the requirements of the undertaking, workplace or work are operational decisions—originating outside the workplace (a fall in sales opportunities, economic crisis, market contraction) or within it (new working methods, downsizing the workplace, abolition of certain types of work)—that give rise to surplus staffing.

The Ultima Ratio Principle and Review of the Operational Decision

Especially in dismissals based on operational requirements, the employer's operational decision is subject to judicial review. The employer is free to determine the aim and content of the operational decision; however, the court examines whether that decision was actually implemented and whether it gave rise to surplus staffing. According to the settled practice of the Court of Cassation, the review proceeds on three axes: whether the employer applied the operational decision consistently (consistency review), whether it acted arbitrarily in the dismissal (arbitrariness review), and whether the dismissal was inevitable as a result of the operational decision (proportionality review — the ultima ratio / last-resort principle).

The ultima ratio principle means that the employer may terminate the contract only where no option other than dismissal remains. The employer must investigate measures that would avoid dismissal, such as employing the worker in another department or in another job the worker could perform after brief training, abolishing overtime, or adopting flexible working methods. Conversely, hiring new employees for the same or similar work after the dismissal, or an increase in production and sales, shows that the operational decision is inconsistent and contrary to the ultima ratio principle. The application of these principles to concrete cases is examined in detail in the decisions below.

Procedure: Written Notice and the Duty to Obtain a Defence

The existence of a valid reason is not, by itself, sufficient; the dismissal must also comply with procedure. The employer must give notice of dismissal in writing and state the reason clearly and precisely. Moreover, in dismissals based on the employee's conduct or productivity, the employee's defence must be obtained beforehand.

Relevant Provision — Labour Act No. 4857, Art. 19 (unofficial translation)

“The employer must give the notice of dismissal in writing and state the reason for dismissal clearly and precisely. An indefinite-term employment contract of an employee may not be terminated for reasons related to that employee's conduct or productivity without obtaining the employee's defence against the allegations. However, the employer's right to terminate in accordance with the conditions of Article 25(II) is reserved.”

Obtaining a defence serves to give the employee a genuine opportunity to respond to the allegations. The period allowed for the defence must therefore be reasonable; just as failing to request a defence at all, giving an unreasonably short period that affords no genuine opportunity to defend is treated as no defence having been obtained, and renders the dismissal invalid. By contrast, obtaining a defence is not required for dismissals based on operational requirements; the duty to obtain a defence is peculiar to dismissals related to the employee's conduct and productivity.

Mandatory Mediation, Time Limits and Competent Court

In reinstatement disputes, application to a mediator before bringing the action is compulsory; mediation is a condition of action. An action brought directly without applying to a mediator is dismissed on procedural grounds for lack of a condition of action, without any further step being taken.

Relevant Provision — Labour Courts Act No. 7036, Art. 3/1 (unofficial translation)

“In actions brought with a claim for an employee's or employer's receivable and compensation based on statute or on an individual or collective labour agreement, and with a claim for reinstatement, having applied to a mediator is a condition of action.”

The time-limit regime is governed by Art. 20 of the Labour Act. The dismissed employee must apply to a mediator with a reinstatement claim within one month of service of the notice of dismissal. If no agreement is reached at the end of the mediation, the action may be brought before the labour court within two weeks of the date on which the final minutes were drawn up; if the parties agree, the dispute may within the same period be referred to a private arbitrator instead.

Relevant Provision — Labour Act No. 4857, Art. 20/1 (unofficial translation)

“(Amended first paragraph: 12/10/2017-7036/11) An employee whose contract has been terminated must, alleging that no reason was stated in the notice of dismissal or that the reason stated is not a valid reason, apply to a mediator with a reinstatement claim within one month of service of the notice of dismissal, in accordance with the provisions of the Labour Courts Act. If no agreement is reached at the end of the mediation, an action may be brought before the labour court within two weeks of the date on which the final minutes were drawn up. If the parties agree, the dispute may within the same period be referred to a private arbitrator instead of the labour court.”

These periods are preclusive. During the period from application to the mediation office until the date the final minutes are drawn up, prescription is suspended and the preclusive period does not run. The competent court is the labour court; where there is no labour court, the civil court of first instance hears these actions in the capacity of a labour court. The action is concluded expeditiously; where an appeal (istinaf) is made against the first-instance decision, the regional court of justice decides expeditiously and with finality, and such decisions are, as a rule, not subject to cassation.

Who Bears the Burden of Proof?

In a reinstatement action, the burden of proof rests, as a rule, on the employer. The obligation to prove that the dismissal was based on a valid reason lies with the employer; if the employee alleges that the dismissal was based on another reason (e.g., a union-related reason), the employee must prove that allegation.

Relevant Provision — Labour Act No. 4857, Art. 20/2 (unofficial translation)

“The obligation to prove that the dismissal was based on a valid reason lies with the employer. If the employee alleges that the dismissal was based on another reason, the employee is obliged to prove that allegation.”

This rule is decisive for the operation of job security. The employer is bound only by the reason stated in the notice of dismissal and may prove only that reason at trial; it may not later put forward new reasons for dismissal. In dismissals based on operational requirements, the employer must, by explaining the aim of the operational decision, establish with concrete evidence why the dismissal was necessary and inevitable, that surplus staffing genuinely arose, and that dismissal was the last resort. Where this burden cannot be discharged, the dismissal is held invalid.

Consequences: Reinstatement, Compensation and Idle-Period Wages

If the court holds the dismissal invalid, the employer must reinstate the employee within one month. If, upon the employee's application, the employer does not reinstate within one month, it becomes liable to pay the employee compensation of not less than four and not more than eight months' wages. In addition, the employee is paid up to four months' accrued wages and other entitlements for the period during which the employee was not employed until the decision became final (idle-period wages).

Relevant Provision — Labour Act No. 4857, Art. 21/1 and 21/3 (unofficial translation)

“Where it is established by the court or the private arbitrator that the employer did not state a valid reason, or that the reason stated is not valid, and the dismissal is held invalid, the employer must reinstate the employee within one month. If, upon the employee's application, the employer does not reinstate within one month, it becomes liable to pay the employee compensation in an amount of not less than four and not more than eight months' wages. … For the period during which the employee was not employed until the decision became final, the employee is paid up to four months' accrued wages and other entitlements.”

For the reinstatement decision to produce effect, the employee must apply to the employer to start work within ten working days of service of the finalised decision. If no application is made within this period, the dismissal made by the employer is deemed valid, and the employer is liable only for its legal consequences (such as severance and notice pay).

Relevant Provision — Labour Act No. 4857, Art. 21 (the employee's application) (unofficial translation)

“The employee must apply to the employer to start work within ten working days of service of the finalised decision of the court or private arbitrator. If the employee does not apply within this period, the dismissal made by the employer is deemed a valid dismissal, and the employer is liable only for its legal consequences.”

Compensation for non-reinstatement and idle-period wages are determined in monetary terms on the basis of the wage at the date the action was brought. If the employee is reinstated, the previously paid wages for the notice period and the severance pay are set off against the payment to be made under Art. 21. The amount of the compensation for non-reinstatement is determined, as seen in the current decision below, according to the criteria of the employee's seniority and the reason for dismissal.

Decisions of the Court of Cassation

Amount of compensation for non-reinstatement: seniority tiers and evaluation of all periods together

Yargıtay Kararı
Yargıtay 9. Hukuk Dairesi, 2026/1 E., 2026/823 K., 04.02.2026 (resolution of conflict between regional courts of justice)

“Under Article 21 of Labour Act No. 4857, where the court holds the dismissal invalid and, upon the employee's application, the employer does not reinstate within one month, compensation of not less than 4 and not more than 8 months' wages must be determined for payment to the employee. Under our Chamber's settled practice, this compensation, being in the nature of job security, must be determined by taking into account facts such as the employee's seniority and the reason for dismissal. The lower and upper limits of the provision may not be exceeded. The only exception to exceeding the upper limit is dismissals for union-related reasons. Our Chamber provides that compensation for non-reinstatement be set at 4 months' wages for an employee with seniority between 6 months and 5 years, 5 months for one with seniority between 5 and 15 years, and 6 months for one with more than 15 years' seniority, and, depending on the reason for dismissal, a determination may be made up to the maximum limit of 8 months. … it has been concluded that the conflict … should be resolved by accepting that the amount of compensation for non-reinstatement is to be determined by taking into account all periods spent in the workplace of the same employer.”

This current decision holds, unanimously and with finality, that the seniority used to calculate the amount of compensation for non-reinstatement is found by evaluating together all periods spent in the workplace of the same employer. It is decisive in practice for clarifying that the lower/upper limits (four to eight months) may not be exceeded, that the sole exception permitting the upper limit to be exceeded is a union-related dismissal, and that the determination follows seniority tiers (4 months for 6 months–5 years, 5 for 5–15 years, 6 for more than 15 years).

Consistency, arbitrariness and proportionality review in operational-requirement dismissals; hiring new staff is contrary to the ultima ratio principle

Yargıtay Kararı
Yargıtay 9. Hukuk Dairesi, 2022/7871 E., 2022/8807 K., 12.09.2022 (affirmed finding)

“… the claimant was not employed in another job, there was an increase in production, the employer did not act consistently, the restructuring at the workplace did not eliminate the need for the employee's work, the restructuring did not require termination of the … contract, no concrete and effective measure to preserve the … contract was found, it was not proven that surplus staffing had arisen at the workplace and that the dismissal was inevitable, and, by reason of hiring new staff, the dismissal was not consistent, was arbitrary, and acted contrary to the ultima ratio principle …”

By affirming this finding of the regional court of justice, the Chamber confirmed that in operational-requirement dismissals the employer must prove surplus staffing and the inevitability of dismissal with concrete evidence, and that facts such as an increase in production, the failure to employ the claimant in another job, and hiring new staff after the dismissal show the dismissal to be inconsistent, arbitrary and contrary to the ultima ratio principle.

A defence must be obtained in conduct-based dismissals; an unreasonable defence period renders the dismissal invalid

Yargıtay Kararı
Yargıtay 9. Hukuk Dairesi, 2016/35745 E., 2016/21382 K., 01.12.2016

“… a conduct-based valid dismissal cannot be effected without requesting a defence; the letter requesting the claimant's defence, said to have been refused for service, was dated 20/04/2015 and required the defence to be given by 17.30 on the same day; the hour at which the defence was requested being unknown, the period allowed for defence was not reasonable, and, consequently, the dismissal was made without obtaining the claimant's defence and is invalid solely for this reason; therefore … the decision, which is in accordance with procedure and law, is AFFIRMED with this additional reasoning …”

The decision establishes that obtaining a defence is compulsory in a valid dismissal based on the employee's conduct, and that the period allowed for the defence must be reasonable. Requesting a defence by an unspecified hour on the same day was not considered a reasonable period; the defence was deemed not to have been obtained, and the dismissal was held invalid solely for this procedural deficiency.

The six-month seniority threshold for job security and the requirement that seniority be complete at the date of the notice of dismissal

Yargıtay Kararı
Yargıtay 9. Hukuk Dairesi, 2015/35440 E., 2016/18789 K., 31.10.2016 (by majority)

“… where the employer, with the aim of preventing the employee from benefiting from the job-security provisions, terminates the contract a few days before the six-month period ends, this may be held invalid for breach of the rule of good faith. Since the Act provides that employees with six months' seniority benefit from job security, this period must be complete on the date the notice of dismissal reaches the employee. That the period will be completed at the end of the notice terms is not sufficient to benefit from the security.”

This reasoning of the Chamber establishes that the six-month seniority required for the scope of job security must be complete on the date the notice of dismissal reaches the employee, and that it is not sufficient for it to be completed at the end of the notice terms. The decision also indicates that dismissals made shortly before the period ends, with the aim of preventing the employee from benefiting from the security, may be held invalid for breach of the rule of good faith. (In that case the majority dismissed the action on the ground that, on the facts, the seniority was not complete.)

The application to return to work requires no particular form; statutory interest applies to compensation for non-reinstatement

Court of Cassation, Assembly of Civil Chambers (the Assembly’s reasoning)
Yargıtay Hukuk Genel Kurulu, 2018/677 E., 2021/499 K., 15.04.2021

“The Law prescribes no particular form for the application to return to work. The employee may apply in writing or orally…” — “However, since compensation for non-reinstatement is by its nature compensation, the interest to be applied must be statutory interest.” (unofficial translation)

The Assembly held that an application duly served on one of the partners of an employer that was a joint venture in the form of an ordinary partnership was sufficient, and that the failure to serve the other partner, who had moved from its registered address, could not be blamed on the employee; it unanimously reversed the insistence judgment. The same decision states that the application must be sincere and that the interest under Labour Code art.34 applies to the wages and other entitlements for the idle period. The decision was given under the text of art.21 before the paragraphs added by Law No. 7036; as to which date’s wage is used in calculating the compensation and wages, the current text of art.21 governs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who may bring a reinstatement action?

The action is available to an employee within the scope of job security: the workplace must employ thirty or more employees, the employee must have at least six months' seniority, and the contract must be of indefinite term. Employer's representatives who manage the entire undertaking or workplace and have authority to hire and dismiss do not benefit from this security.

Is applying to a mediator compulsory before bringing the action?

Yes. Mediation is a condition of action. Application to a mediator must be made within one month of service of the notice of dismissal; if no agreement is reached, the action must be brought before the labour court within two weeks of the final minutes. An action brought without applying to a mediator is dismissed on procedural grounds.

Are a valid reason and a just cause the same?

No. A just cause (Art. 25) is a serious fact that makes the relationship intolerable and permits immediate termination. A valid reason is a less grave one that justifies terminating the contract by notice. A fact may constitute a valid reason even if it does not reach the level of a just cause.

How much is the compensation for non-reinstatement?

It is between four and eight months' wages. In the Court of Cassation's practice, by seniority, 4 months' wages apply for 6 months–5 years, 5 for 5–15 years and 6 for more than 15 years; depending on the reason for dismissal it may rise to eight months. The only case permitting the upper limit to be exceeded is a union-related dismissal.

I won the case; am I reinstated automatically?

No. You must apply to the employer to start work within ten working days of service of the finalised decision. If you do not apply, the dismissal is deemed valid. If, despite your application, the employer does not reinstate within one month, you become entitled to compensation for non-reinstatement and idle-period wages.

Is the dismissal invalid if the employer dismissed me without obtaining my defence?

In dismissals based on conduct or productivity, obtaining a defence is compulsory; a dismissal made without it is invalid solely for that reason. The period allowed for the defence must also be reasonable. In dismissals based on operational requirements, however, obtaining a defence is not required.
