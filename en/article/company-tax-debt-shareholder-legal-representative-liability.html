Company Tax Debt in Türkiye: Shareholder and Legal Representative Liability (Law 6183 Art. 35), Payment Orders

Short answer: Under Turkish law, a company’s tax debt is first claimed from the company; the shareholder or the legal representative may be pursued only when the debt cannot be collected from the company or it becomes clear that it cannot be collected. As a rule, a limited company shareholder is liable for debts of the shareholding period in proportion to its capital share (Law No. 6183 art. 35); in a 2025 decision, the Council of State Assembly of Tax Law Chambers characterised this liability as strict (no-fault) liability. According to the Constitutional Court, the legal representative’s liability under Tax Procedure Law art. 10 is based on breach of tax duties, that is, on fault; in 2025 the Court also stated, with regard to Law No. 6183 repeated art. 35, that a legal representative who performs its duties fully and on time cannot be held liable for unpaid public debts. An action against a payment order issued in your name may be filed with the tax court within 15 days of service (Law No. 6183 art. 58; Law No. 2576 art. 13).

This article explains Turkish law. It is an English translation of the Turkish original; in case of any discrepancy, the Turkish text prevails.

In brief

Under Turkish law, the shareholder's proportional liability and the legal representative's duty-based liability for a limited company's unpaid tax debt, the order of enforcement, the relevant period and the 15-day time limit to challenge a payment order, with Constitutional Court and Council of State decisions.

Under Turkish law, when the tax authority may pursue the shareholder and the legal representative for a limited company’s unpaid tax debt, the limits of that liability, the order of enforcement and the action against a payment order issued in your name: Law No. 6183 art. 35, repeated art. 35 and art. 58, and Tax Procedure Law art. 10, with decisions of the Constitutional Court and the Council of State Assembly of Tax Law Chambers.

Who Is Liable for a Company’s Tax Debt? Legal Basis

The principal debtor of a company’s tax debt is the company. For public receivables that cannot be collected from the company, the law places two groups of persons in second rank: limited company shareholders and legal representatives. Both liabilities are regulated in the same law, in two consecutive articles, but their conditions and limits differ.

The shareholders’ liability is based on article 35 of Law No. 6183:

Statutory Provision — Law on the Procedure for the Collection of Public Receivables (Law No. 6183) art. 35 (Public debts of limited companies; unofficial translation)

“Limited company shareholders are directly liable, in proportion to their capital shares, for public receivables that cannot be collected, wholly or partly, from the company or that are understood to be uncollectable, and are subject to enforcement under this Law.

… Where a shareholder transfers its capital share in the company, the transferor and the transferee shall be held jointly and severally liable, under the first paragraph, for payment of public receivables relating to the period before the transfer.

… Where the shareholders were different persons at the times the public receivable arose and should have been paid, those persons shall be held jointly and severally liable, under the first paragraph, for payment of the public receivable.”

The article mentions only limited company shareholders. Joint stock company shareholders are not referred to in this article; that a joint stock company shareholder cannot be held liable on the basis of this provision is this article’s inference. A joint stock company’s legal representatives may be held liable under the provisions below (repeated art. 35, Tax Procedure Law art. 10); who counts as a legal representative in a joint stock company is outside the scope of this article.

The legal representative’s liability has two sources. The first is repeated article 35 of Law No. 6183:

Statutory Provision — Law on the Procedure for the Collection of Public Receivables (Law No. 6183) repeated art. 35 (Liability of legal representatives; unofficial translation)

“Public receivables that cannot be collected, wholly or partly, from the assets of legal persons, minors and persons under guardianship, and of entities without legal personality such as foundations and communities, or that are understood to be uncollectable, shall be collected under this Law from the personal assets of the legal representatives and of those managing the entity without legal personality.

… Representatives, those managing the entity or agents may claim recourse against the principal public debtor for amounts they have paid under this article.”

Two paragraphs added to the article in 2008 were annulled by the Constitutional Court’s decision of 19.03.2015; one of them made all persons who were legal representatives at the times the receivable arose and should have been paid jointly and severally liable. The current text contains only an annulment note in place of those paragraphs.

The second source is article 10 of the Tax Procedure Law on tax duties:

Statutory Provision — Tax Procedure Law (Law No. 213) art. 10 (Duty of legal representatives; unofficial translation)

“Tax and related receivables that cannot be collected, wholly or partly, from the assets of taxpayers or tax withholders because the persons named above failed to perform these duties shall be collected from the assets of those who failed to perform their legal duties.

… Limited company shareholders are liable, in proportion to the capital shares they contributed to the company, for public receivables arising in this context relating to the periods before liquidation.”

The sentence in the second paragraph comes from the paragraph on companies liquidated and struck off the trade registry. Under the same paragraph, assessments and penalties relating to the pre-liquidation period of such companies are issued in the name of any one of the legal representatives, who are jointly and severally liable; the shareholder, in turn, is liable for receivables arising from that period in proportion to its capital share.

Differences Between the Shareholder’s and the Legal Representative’s Liability

In many small companies the shareholder and the legal representative are the same person; even so, the consequences of the two capacities are assessed separately. The differences can be summarised as follows:

  • Fault: The Council of State Assembly of Tax Law Chambers has characterised the shareholder’s liability as strict (no-fault) liability. According to the Constitutional Court, the legal representative’s liability under Tax Procedure Law art. 10 is fault-based; the fault here is the breach of tax duties.
  • Amount: By law, the shareholder is liable “in proportion to their capital shares”. Additional art. 35 contains no such proportional limit for the legal representative.
  • Recourse: Additional art. 35 and Tax Procedure Law art. 10 grant a paying legal representative a right of recourse against the principal debtor. Article 35 contains no similar recourse provision for the shareholder.
  • Common ground: Both liabilities are secondary; the debt is first claimed from the company.
Council of State Assembly of Tax Law Chambers Decision
Council of State Assembly of Tax Law Chambers, E. 2024/314, K. 2025/281, 09.04.2025 (the Assembly’s reasoning for partly quashing the decision of persistence)

“… since the liability provided for collection from company shareholders is secondary (second-degree) in nature and no condition other than being a shareholder is imposed for holding shareholders liable for company debts, it is understood that the liability provided for shareholders, unlike the liability of the legal representative, is proportional to the share and strict.”

In that case the claimant had been a fifty per cent shareholder between 2006 and 2009 and had left the company by transferring the share. The Assembly accepted that the claimant was liable in proportion to the share for tax debts of the shareholding period; however, it required the file to be re-examined as to the capital share, whether enforcement against the company had been duly completed and limitation. The decision was given by majority.

The text that states the fault requirement for the legal representative most clearly is the Constitutional Court decision annulling the two paragraphs added to repeated art. 35:

Constitutional Court Decision (constitutional review)
Constitutional Court, E. 2014/144, K. 2015/29, 19.03.2015 (annulment of the fifth and sixth paragraphs of repeated art. 35)

“The liability accepted for legal representatives in article 10 of Law No. 213 is fault-based liability. The fault here is the breach of duties relating to taxation.”

The Court found it incompatible with justice and equity that a legal representative who performed its duties fully and on time be held jointly and severally liable for an act occurring in a period when it was not in office and had no opportunity to intervene. The decision was given by majority; four members wrote dissenting opinions holding that joint and several liability was not unconstitutional.

Order of Enforcement: Company First, Then Shareholder and Legal Representative

For the shareholder or the legal representative to be pursued, the receivable must be one that is “uncollectable” or “understood to be uncollectable” from the company. The law defines both terms:

Statutory Provision — Law on the Procedure for the Collection of Public Receivables (Law No. 6183) art. 3 (Terms in the Law; unofficial translation)

“The term uncollectable public receivable: public receivables that cannot be collected for reasons such as the absence of any attachable asset as a result of an asset search carried out under this Law, or the sale proceeds failing to cover the public receivable despite the attached assets being sold and converted into money,

… The term public receivable understood to be uncollectable: public receivables that, at any stage of the enforcement proceedings conducted by the collection offices, are found to be uncollectable from the public debtor for reasons such as it becoming clear that the values assigned under this Law to the debtor’s attached assets cannot cover the public receivable, or that the public receivable claimed from a debtor declared bankrupt cannot be collected from the bankruptcy estate,”

In 2025, when rejecting the objection to the words “legal persons” in the first paragraph of repeated art. 35, the Constitutional Court explained the secondary nature of the liability as follows:

Constitutional Court Decision (constitutional review)
Constitutional Court, E. 2025/55, K. 2025/240, 26.11.2025 (objection rejected; Official Gazette 18.03.2026)

“That liability is secondary in nature and, under the rule, the public receivable must first be sought to be collected from the legal persons. Where the public receivable cannot be collected from the legal persons or it is understood that it cannot be collected, the liability of the legal representative may then be invoked.”

In the same decision the Court found the rule proportionate, having regard to the fact that a legal representative who performs its duties fully and on time cannot be held liable for unpaid public debts, that the liability is secondary and that recourse is available. The decision was given by majority; the dissenting opinions state that the rule is vague and can lead to liability without fault.

In practice, impossibility of collection is shown by due service of a payment order on the company followed by an asset search. Reversing the order can vitiate the payment order:

Council of State Assembly of Tax Law Chambers Decision (upheld reasoning of the regional administrative court’s decision of persistence)
Council of State Assembly of Tax Law Chambers, E. 2023/1062, K. 2025/296, 09.04.2025 (payment order issued to the legal representative)

“… it is understood that, while an asset search should have been carried out after service of the payment orders issued in the name of the principal debtor company, carrying out an asset search on the company after a payment order had been issued in the claimant’s name in the capacity of legal representative was unlawful. Moreover, a search conducted only with banks will not be sufficient for an asset search within the meaning of the Law to be deemed carried out.”

The Assembly did not write this reasoning itself; it upheld the regional administrative court’s decision of persistence as “in accordance with procedure and law”. The Council of State’s Fourth Chamber had earlier held that a search conducted after the payment order did not change the outcome; the decision was given by majority and that view was defended in the dissent.

Whether an asset search was carried out is also argued on the documents. In another shareholder case, the Assembly noted that the query output submitted by the administration showed that letters had been sent to all bank head offices, and quashed the decision of persistence so that those letters and the steps taken on them could be requested and examined (Council of State Assembly of Tax Law Chambers, E. 2022/1227, K. 2023/1171, 11.10.2023). In other words, a claim that the search was inadequate is assessed together with the records in the file.

Is there an order between the shareholder and the legal representative? According to the regional administrative court’s reasoning reported in Assembly decision 2025/281, the Council of State Assembly for the Unification of Case-Law held, in its decision dated 11.12.2018, E. 2013/1, K. 2018/1, that there is no order of priority between the legal representative and the shareholder. This article relies on that report in the Assembly decision, not on the text of the unification decision itself.

For Which Period’s Debts Is One Liable?

Shareholder: What the phrase “the time the public receivable arose” in article 35 refers to is disputed. In decision 2025/281, the regional administrative court took the date of accrual as the basis; the Assembly, however, took the taxable event as the basis and accepted that the shareholder remains liable for the debts of the shareholding period even after transferring the share. The transferor and the transferee are, by law, jointly and severally liable for debts relating to the period before the transfer (art. 35/2).

In October 2025 the Assembly placed an important limit on this rule. After the shareholder had left, the company had filed a return using the voluntary disclosure (repentance) provision but, having failed to pay the tax on time, was fined for tax loss:

Council of State Assembly of Tax Law Chambers Decision
Council of State Assembly of Tax Law Chambers, E. 2024/181, K. 2025/752, 08.10.2025 (payment order issued to a shareholder; breach of voluntary disclosure)

“Shareholders, who have the opportunity to access all information on the company’s transactions only during the periods in which their shareholder status continues, cannot be held liable, merely because they held shares in the taxation periods, for a tax loss penalty arising from a breach of the voluntary disclosure provision that did not occur during their shareholding, after they have transferred their shares, in other words after their ties with the company have ended.”

The Assembly reiterated that, as a rule, the shareholder is liable for taxes and penalties relating to the periods in which the shareholder status continued; but it said that the date on which the act giving rise to the penalty was committed must also be considered. The Assembly upheld the decision of persistence, which had relied on the date of accrual, only as to its outcome, not its reasoning. The decision was given by majority; the dissent argued that the sole criterion is the taxable event. Whether acts other than breach of voluntary disclosure committed after departure lead to the same result was not discussed in the decision.

Legal representative: In decision 2025/240, the Constitutional Court stated that the legal representative is held liable for public receivables arising from acts and transactions in the period during which it had the power to intervene and prevent them. In the same decision, the Court stated that where different persons were legal representatives between the date the receivable arose and the date it should have been paid, the courts may determine which of them is liable according to the specific case. A Assembly decision applying the term-of-office criterion is discussed in the article on the liabilities of the company director.

Action Against the Payment Order: Time Limit, Court and Available Objections

The shareholder or the legal representative is pursued by a payment order issued in its own name:

Statutory Provision — Law on the Procedure for the Collection of Public Receivables (Law No. 6183) art. 55/1 (Payment order; unofficial translation)

“Those who fail to pay a public receivable on its due date shall be served with a “payment order” requiring them to pay their debts or declare their assets within 15 days.”

Statutory Provision — Law on the Procedure for the Collection of Public Receivables (Law No. 6183) art. 58 (Objection to the payment order; unofficial translation)

“A person served with a payment order may, within 15 days of the date of service, object before the tax objection commission handling objections of the creditor collection office on the grounds that there is no such debt, that it has been partly paid or that it is time-barred.

… A debtor objecting to part of the debt must clearly indicate the nature and amount of that part; otherwise the objection shall be deemed not to have been made.”

The terms “objection commission” and “objection” in the article now mean the tax court and an action:

Statutory Provision — Law on the Establishment and Duties of Regional Administrative Courts, Administrative Courts and Tax Courts (Law No. 2576) art. 13 (Amended terms; unofficial translation)

“Upon the tax courts commencing their duties, in matters within the jurisdiction of these courts, the following terms in other laws:

a) the terms objection commission, Tax Appeals Commission, Customs Arbitration Board mean Tax Court,

… c) the term objection means filing an action in the tax court.”

Accordingly, an action against the payment order is filed with the tax court within 15 days of service. Which province’s tax court has jurisdiction and how the time limit is calculated are explained in detail in the article on the competent court in tax cases. If only part of the debt is challenged, that part and its amount must be clearly indicated (art. 58/2). If an objection to the whole debt is rejected wholly or partly, the debtor must declare its assets within 15 days of service of the rejection decision (art. 58).

The article lists three grounds of objection: there is no debt, it has been partly paid, it is time-barred. In shareholder and legal representative cases the Council of State also examines whether the conditions of liability are met. The main objections arising from the decisions and provisions above are:

  • The claimant was not a shareholder or legal representative in the period to which the debt relates.
  • The payment order was not duly served on the company, or the asset search was incomplete or carried out in the wrong order.
  • The payment order was issued for an amount exceeding the shareholder’s capital share.
  • For the legal representative, there was no breach of tax duties.
  • The limitation period for collection has expired.

This list is compiled from the specific facts of the decisions; which objection will work in a given file depends on the service and search records in that file.

Practical Points

  • Document your capacity: keep the Turkish Trade Registry Gazette announcements of the dates of joining and leaving the company, and of appointment and departure as manager, at hand. In the decisions cited, the periods were determined from Trade Registry Gazette announcements and share transfer agreements.
  • Read the payment order period by period: separate each line by the tax period, due date and capacity (shareholder or legal representative) on which it is based.
  • Request the records of enforcement against the company: when and how the payment order was served on the company, and the date and scope of the asset search (land registry, vehicles, banks) may decide the case.
  • Transferring the share is not enough: a transferring shareholder remains jointly and severally liable with the transferee for debts relating to the period before the transfer (art. 35/2).
  • Document the date of service: the 15-day time limit for filing an action runs from service of the payment order (art. 58).
  • Do not forget recourse: a legal representative who pays may claim recourse against the company for the amount paid (repeated art. 35; Tax Procedure Law art. 10).

Frequently Asked Questions

Up to what amount is a shareholder liable for a limited company’s tax debt in Türkiye?

A limited company shareholder is liable, in proportion to its capital share, for tax debts that cannot be collected from the company or are understood to be uncollectable (Law No. 6183 art. 35). In a 2025 decision, the Council of State Assembly of Tax Law Chambers characterised this liability as secondary and strict. The transferor and transferee of a share are jointly and severally liable for debts relating to the period before the transfer.

Under Turkish law, is a company’s tax debt first claimed from the company or from the shareholder or legal representative?

It is first claimed from the company. The Constitutional Court has stated that the legal representative’s liability is secondary and that the public receivable must first be sought to be collected from the legal person. Decisions given or upheld by the Council of State Assembly of Tax Law Chambers have required due service of a payment order on the company followed by an adequate asset search.

Can a limited company shareholder who is not a manager be held liable for the company’s tax debt in Türkiye?

Yes, as a rule. Law No. 6183 art. 35 does not tie the shareholder’s liability to the capacity of manager; the Council of State Assembly of Tax Law Chambers has stated that no condition other than being a shareholder is required. The liability is limited to debts of the shareholding period that cannot be collected from the company, in proportion to the capital share.

Under Turkish law, is a limited company shareholder who transferred its shares liable for a tax penalty imposed after the transfer?

It depends on the circumstances. The Council of State Assembly of Tax Law Chambers has accepted that liability for the taxes of the shareholding period continues after the transfer. In one decision, however, it held by majority that a former shareholder cannot be held liable for a tax loss penalty imposed for a breach of voluntary disclosure committed after the shareholding ended.

What is the time limit for an action against a tax payment order issued to a shareholder or legal representative in Türkiye?

The time limit is 15 days from service of the payment order (Law No. 6183 art. 58). Because the statutory term “objection commission” means the tax court and “objection” means filing an action, the action is filed with the tax court (Law No. 2576 art. 13). If only part of the debt is challenged, that part and its amount must be clearly indicated.
