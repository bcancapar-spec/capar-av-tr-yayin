Right of Withdrawal in Turkish Distance Contracts: Fourteen Days, Exceptions, Refunds, Marketplace Liability

Short answer: Under Turkish law, in a distance contract the consumer may withdraw within fourteen days without giving any reason and without paying a penalty, and it is sufficient for the withdrawal notice to be directed to the seller or provider within that period (Consumer Protection Law art. 48/4). The period starts on the day the contract is concluded for services and on the day the consumer receives the goods for contracts for the delivery of goods; delivery of the goods to the carrier does not count as delivery to the consumer (Distance Contracts Regulation art. 9). A consumer who has not been properly informed about the right of withdrawal is not bound by the fourteen-day period, but the right expires in any event one year after the end of the withdrawal period. The seller must refund all payments collected, including any delivery costs, within fourteen days of the goods being handed to the carrier specified for returns, while in certain contracts listed in the Regulation the right of withdrawal cannot be exercised unless the parties agree otherwise. A marketplace (intermediary service provider) that collects the price on behalf of the seller is jointly and severally liable with the seller for the obligations relating to withdrawal, unless the price was transferred to the seller after delivery (Consumer Protection Law art. 48/6).

This article explains Turkish law. It is an English translation of the Turkish original; in case of any discrepancy, the Turkish text prevails.

What Is a Distance Contract? (Consumer Protection Law art. 48)

A distance contract is a contract concluded between a seller or provider and a consumer without their simultaneous physical presence, within a system organised for the distance marketing of goods or services, by the exclusive use of means of distance communication up to and including the moment the contract is concluded (Consumer Protection Law art. 48/1). The Regulation defines a means of distance communication as any tool or medium, such as letters, catalogues, telephone, fax, radio, television, e-mail, text messages and the internet, that makes it possible to conclude a contract without the parties meeting physically (Distance Contracts Regulation art. 4). Orders placed through a website or a mobile application, telephone sales and catalogue sales fall within this scope.

The Law leaves excluded contracts, the right of withdrawal and the details of the information duty to secondary legislation (Consumer Protection Law art. 48/7). That legislation is the Distance Contracts Regulation published in the Official Gazette of 27/11/2014. Financial services, the transfer of immovable property or of rights over it, residential leases, package tours, timeshare and holiday clubs, passenger transport services (subject to certain information duties) and the assembly, maintenance and repair of goods are outside the Regulation’s scope (Distance Contracts Regulation art. 2/2).

Statutory Provision — Consumer Protection Law (Law No. 6502) art. 48 (Distance contracts) (unofficial translation)

“(1) A distance contract is a contract concluded between the seller or provider and the consumer, without their simultaneous physical presence, within a system organised for the distance marketing of goods or services, through the use of means of distance communication up to and including the moment the contract is concluded. (2) Before accepting a distance contract or any corresponding offer, the consumer shall be informed by the seller or provider, clearly and comprehensibly, of the matters specified in the regulation and of the fact that by confirming the order he or she assumes a payment obligation. The burden of proving that the consumer was informed lies with the seller or provider. … (4) The consumer has the right to withdraw from the contract within fourteen days without giving any reason and without paying any penalty. It is sufficient that the notice of withdrawal is directed to the seller or provider within this period. The seller or provider must prove that the consumer was informed of the right of withdrawal. If the consumer is not duly informed of the right of withdrawal, he or she is not bound by the fourteen-day period in exercising it. In any event, this period ends one year after the date on which the withdrawal period expires. The consumer is not liable for changes and deterioration resulting from the normal use of the goods during the withdrawal period.”

Pre-Contractual Information and the Burden of Proof

Before the contract is concluded, or before the consumer accepts an offer, the seller or provider must inform the consumer of all the matters listed in the Regulation. They include the main characteristics of the goods or services, the total price including all taxes and any additional costs, the delivery or performance period, the conditions, period and procedure for exercising the right of withdrawal and the carrier designated by the seller for returns, the address to which the withdrawal notice is to be sent and, where the right of withdrawal does not apply, information to that effect (Distance Contracts Regulation art. 5/1). The information must be provided in at least twelve-point type, in comprehensible language, in writing or on a durable medium (Distance Contracts Regulation art. 6/1).

The burden of proving that the pre-contractual information was given lies with the seller or provider (Consumer Protection Law art. 48/2); for contracts concluded through a platform, the Regulation places it on the intermediary service provider as well. If the consumer’s confirmation of having received the information is not obtained, the contract is deemed not to have been concluded; if the consumer is not told clearly immediately before confirming the order that the order entails a payment obligation, he or she is not bound by the order; and if no information was given about additional costs, the consumer does not have to bear them (Distance Contracts Regulation arts. 5/3, 5/6, 7 and 8/1).

The Withdrawal Period: Fourteen Days and When It Starts

The consumer may withdraw from the contract within fourteen days without giving reasons and without paying a penalty. The starting point depends on the type of contract: for services, the day the contract is concluded; for contracts for the delivery of goods, the day the consumer or a third party designated by the consumer takes delivery of the goods. Handing the goods to the carrier is not delivery to the consumer, and the consumer may also withdraw between the conclusion of the contract and delivery. Where goods are delivered and services performed together, the rules on the delivery of goods apply.

Regulatory Provision — Distance Contracts Regulation art. 9 (Right of withdrawal) (unofficial translation)

“(1) The consumer has the right to withdraw from the contract within fourteen days without giving any reason and without paying any penalty. (2) The withdrawal period starts, for contracts for the performance of services, on the day the contract is concluded; for contracts for the delivery of goods, on the day the consumer or a third party designated by the consumer takes delivery of the goods. However, the consumer may also exercise the right of withdrawal during the period between the conclusion of the contract and the delivery of the goods. … (4) Delivery of the goods by the seller to the carrier shall not be regarded as delivery to the consumer. (5) In contracts where goods are delivered and services performed together, the provisions on the right of withdrawal relating to the delivery of goods apply.”

In a dispute concerning a holiday package bought online, the Court of Cassation held that where the contract stated that the right of withdrawal could be exercised within fourteen days of the contract date, the period runs from the contract date, not from the time the service is used, and reversed a judgment that had allowed the claim because the service had not yet been used (13th Civil Chamber, E. 2015/25017, K. 2018/556; see the box below). It should also be recalled that accommodation services to be provided on a specific date or within a specific period are among the exceptions to the right of withdrawal unless otherwise agreed (Distance Contracts Regulation art. 15/1-g); in the holiday package decisions discussed here the outcome turned on the right of withdrawal granted in the contract and on the information duty.

Missing Information: Extension of the Period to One Year

The burden of proving that the consumer was informed of the right of withdrawal lies with the seller or provider (Consumer Protection Law art. 48/4). A consumer who was not properly informed is not bound by the fourteen-day period; even then, however, the right ends in any event one year after the date on which the withdrawal period expires. If proper information is given within that year, the fourteen-day period starts on the day the information is given.

Regulatory Provision — Distance Contracts Regulation art. 10 (Incomplete information) (unofficial translation)

“(1) The seller or provider … and the intermediary service provider must prove that the consumer was informed of the right of withdrawal. If the consumer is not duly informed of the right of withdrawal, he or she is not bound by the fourteen-day period in exercising it. In any event, this period ends one year after the date on which the withdrawal period expires. (2) If due information on the right of withdrawal is given within the one-year period, the fourteen-day withdrawal period starts to run from the day on which that information is duly given.”

The Court of Cassation’s 3rd Civil Chamber held that a consumer who bought a holiday package online and was found not to have been informed of the right of withdrawal was not bound by the fourteen-day period and could withdraw by bringing an action within one year of the end of the withdrawal period; it upheld the local court’s judgment for the consumer while replacing its reasoning (3rd Civil Chamber, E. 2020/5130, K. 2021/6041). Read together, the two decisions show that in a similar service the outcome depends on whether the consumer was informed.

How Is the Right Exercised? Notice and Proof

No reason has to be given. It is sufficient that the withdrawal notice is directed, before the period expires, in writing or on a durable medium (such as e-mail or text message) to the seller, provider or intermediary service provider; the Law looks to the notice being sent within the period rather than received (Consumer Protection Law art. 48/4). The consumer may use the model withdrawal form annexed to the Regulation or make an explicit statement; where withdrawal is offered through a website, the seller must immediately send the consumer an acknowledgement of receipt.

Regulatory Provision — Distance Contracts Regulation art. 11 (Exercise of the right of withdrawal) (unofficial translation)

“(1) It is sufficient that the notice of exercise of the right of withdrawal is directed, before the withdrawal period expires, in writing or on a durable medium … to the seller, provider or intermediary service provider. (2) In exercising the right of withdrawal, the consumer may use the form set out in the ANNEX or make any other unequivocal statement setting out the decision to withdraw. … (4) The burden of proof regarding the exercise of the right of withdrawal under this article lies with the consumer.”

Because the consumer bears the burden of proving the exercise of the right, it is advisable in practice to send the notice by a means that records its date and content (e-mail, the platform’s return screen, registered mail with acknowledgement of receipt or a notary) and to keep that record.

Refund of the Price, Return of the Goods and Return Costs

The seller must refund all payments received, including any delivery costs, within fourteen days of the date on which the goods are handed to the carrier designated for returns in the pre-contractual information; if the consumer uses a different carrier, the period starts when the goods reach the seller. Where the consumer withdraws before delivery, and in service contracts, the period runs from the date the withdrawal notice reaches the seller or provider. The refund is made in a single payment, using the means of payment the consumer used and without imposing any cost or obligation on the consumer; where payment was made by credit card, the card issuer must add the amount transferred to it to the cardholder’s available limit in a single transaction.

Regulatory Provision — Distance Contracts Regulation art. 12 (Obligations of the seller or provider) (unofficial translation)

“(1) The seller must refund all payments received, including any costs of delivering the goods to the consumer, within fourteen days of the date on which the goods subject to withdrawal are handed to the carrier specified for returns in the pre-contractual information. However, if the consumer returns the goods with a carrier other than the one designated for returns, this obligation starts on the date the goods reach the seller. … (4) The seller or provider must make all refunds referred to in the first, second and third paragraphs … in a manner appropriate to the means of payment used by the consumer at the time of purchase and in a single payment, without imposing any cost or obligation on the consumer. … (5) … Where, in exercising the right of withdrawal, the goods are returned through the carrier specified by the seller for returns under subparagraph (g) of the first paragraph of article 5, the consumer cannot be held liable for the costs of the return. Where the seller has not specified any carrier for returns in the pre-contractual information, no charge may be claimed from the consumer for return costs.”

The consumer, for his or her part, must send the goods back to the seller or a person authorised by the seller within fourteen days of sending the withdrawal notice, unless the seller has offered to collect them (Distance Contracts Regulation art. 13/1). The current wording of the seller’s refund obligation results from amendments made in 2022 and 2025, and the word “four” in the consumer’s return period (making it fourteen days) was added in 2022. Under the Regulation’s transitional provision and footnotes, these rules apply from 1/1/2026; until that date the pre-amendment text applied (Distance Contracts Regulation provisional art. 1).

When the right of withdrawal is exercised, ancillary contracts for goods or services supplied in addition to the main goods or services also terminate automatically (Distance Contracts Regulation art. 14/1). Where the purchase was financed by a consumer loan, the linked credit agreement also terminates without any obligation to pay compensation or a penalty, provided that the withdrawal notice is also sent to the lender within the withdrawal period (Consumer Protection Law art. 30/3).

Normal Use: Trying Out and Using the Product

The consumer is not liable for changes and deterioration caused by the normal use of the goods during the withdrawal period (Consumer Protection Law art. 48/4). Under the Regulation, a consumer who uses the goods in accordance with their functioning, technical characteristics and instructions for use cannot be held liable for the resulting changes and deterioration (Distance Contracts Regulation art. 13/2). Where use goes beyond normal use, the loss suffered by the seller becomes relevant.

In a dispute over the return of a mobile phone bought online and used for six days during the withdrawal period, with a SIM card inserted and applications downloaded, the 3rd Civil Chamber held that a mobile phone does not fall within the exception for opened books, digital content and computer consumables (Distance Contracts Regulation art. 15/1-e), but that whether six days’ use exceeded normal use and, if so, the seller’s loss had to be established by an expert report (3rd Civil Chamber, E. 2022/6784, K. 2022/8533; reversal in the interest of the law). The decision did not treat use as such as extinguishing the right of withdrawal; it accepted that, if the use went beyond normal use, the consumer would be liable for the loss the seller suffered as a result.

Exceptions to the Right of Withdrawal

Unless otherwise agreed, the consumer cannot exercise the right of withdrawal in certain contracts. Where the right does not apply, the seller must state in the pre-contractual information that the consumer cannot benefit from it, or the conditions under which it will be lost (Distance Contracts Regulation art. 5/1-h); since the seller bears the burden of proving the pre-contractual information, it must also prove this information.

Regulatory Provision — Distance Contracts Regulation art. 15 (Exceptions to the right of withdrawal) (unofficial translation)

“(1) Unless otherwise agreed by the parties, the consumer cannot exercise the right of withdrawal in the following contracts: a) Contracts for goods or services whose price depends on fluctuations in the financial markets which are outside the control of the seller or provider. b) Contracts for goods made to the consumer’s specifications or personal needs. c) Contracts for the delivery of goods which are liable to deteriorate rapidly or whose expiry date may pass. ç) Contracts for the delivery of goods whose protective elements such as packaging, tape, seal or package have been opened after delivery and which are not suitable for return for health and hygiene reasons. d) Contracts for goods which, after delivery, are mixed with other products and by their nature cannot be separated. e) Contracts for books, digital content and computer consumables supplied on a tangible medium where their protective elements such as packaging, tape, seal or package have been opened after delivery. f) Contracts for the delivery of periodicals such as newspapers and magazines, other than those supplied under a subscription contract. g) Contracts for accommodation, carriage of goods, car rental, food and beverage supply and leisure activities for entertainment or recreation to be provided on a specific date or within a specific period. ğ) Contracts for services performed instantly in electronic form or intangible goods delivered instantly to the consumer. h) Contracts for services whose performance has begun with the consumer’s consent before the withdrawal period expires.”

Note on the current text: of the subparagraphs added in 2022 and not applied until 1/1/2026 under the transitional provision, subparagraph (i) was repealed by the amendment of 24/5/2025. According to the current text in the Official Legislation Information System, the exceptions for movables whose registration is compulsory under the Highway Traffic Law and for unmanned aerial vehicles subject to registration (ı), for contracts concluded by public auction in the form of a live auction (j), and for goods whose installation, stated in the user manual to be carried out by the seller or an authorised service, has been carried out (k), were annulled by a decision of the Council of State’s 10th Chamber dated 6/5/2026 (Distance Contracts Regulation art. 15/1). As the full text of the annulment decision could not be accessed in the official case-law database when this article was prepared, its content is not reported here; in any dispute concerning these subparagraphs the current position should be verified separately.

The 3rd Civil Chamber applied subparagraph (e) according to its wording and kept mobile phones outside that exception (see above). Another subparagraph frequently debated in practice concerns services whose performance has begun with the consumer’s consent before the withdrawal period expires (h); what matters there is the existence of that consent and of the pre-contractual information.

Liability of Marketplaces (Intermediary Service Providers)

Where a purchase is made through a marketplace platform, the party to the contract is, as a rule, the seller offering the product, while the operator of the platform is an “intermediary service provider”. Following the 2022 amendment, the intermediary service provider is jointly and severally liable with the seller for the pre-contractual information and, where it has collected the price on the seller’s behalf, for the obligations relating to the right of withdrawal; cases where the price has been transferred to the seller after delivery and the remedies for defects are excluded from that liability (Consumer Protection Law art. 48/6).

Statutory Provision — Consumer Protection Law (Law No. 6502) art. 48/6 (Liability of intermediary service providers) (unofficial translation)

“(6) Those operating as intermediary service providers shall, in respect of the distance contracts in which they act as intermediaries, … a) be jointly and severally liable with the seller or provider for providing, confirming and proving the pre-contractual information, … d) where they collect the price on behalf of the seller or provider, be jointly and severally liable with the seller or provider for the obligations relating to delivery or performance and to the right of withdrawal, except for cases where the price has been transferred to the seller or provider after delivery or performance to the consumer and except for the exercise of the rights set out in articles 11 and 15, …”

The intermediary service provider must also set up, and keep continuously open, a system through which consumers can transmit and follow up requests such as withdrawal notices and refund requests (Consumer Protection Law art. 48/5); where it has collected the price on the seller’s behalf, it is jointly and severally liable with the seller, in the cases listed in the Regulation, for refunding that price within fourteen days (Distance Contracts Regulation art. 12/A).

The case law reflected this distinction even before the amendment. The 3rd Civil Chamber held that a platform which merely provides an e-commerce environment cannot be held liable for defects in goods sold by the seller (3rd Civil Chamber, E. 2021/4000, K. 2021/11403). The 11th Civil Chamber, by contrast, held that a platform which collected the price from the consumer on the seller’s behalf and also undertook the cargo carriage was liable as an electronic intermediary service provider under the statutory text in force at the time and its legislative rationale (11th Civil Chamber, E. 2022/3467, K. 2022/6446).

Withdrawal Compared with Rights Arising from Defects

The right of withdrawal is a special right exercised within a short period, without giving reasons and regardless of whether the goods are defective. Where the goods turn out to be defective, the consumer relies instead on the remedies for defective goods: termination of the contract, price reduction, free repair or replacement with a non-defective item; free repair and replacement may also be claimed from the manufacturer or importer (Consumer Protection Law art. 11). Defects and their proof are discussed in our articles A Defective Zero-km Vehicle (Consumer Rights) and Liability for Defects in the Sale of a Second-Hand Vehicle.

Dispute Resolution: Consumer Arbitration Committees and Consumer Courts

For disputes below the monetary threshold set by the Law, application to a consumer arbitration committee is mandatory; disputes above the threshold cannot be brought before the committees (Consumer Protection Law art. 68/1). Since the threshold is increased each calendar year by the revaluation rate (Consumer Protection Law art. 68/4), the amount applicable on the date of application should be checked. The application may be made to the committee where the consumer resides or where the consumer transaction took place (Consumer Protection Law art. 68/3).

Disputes outside the committees’ jurisdiction are heard by the consumer courts (Consumer Protection Law art. 73/1), and the action may also be brought in the consumer court of the consumer’s place of residence (Consumer Protection Law art. 73/5). Applying to a mediator before bringing an action in the consumer court is a procedural prerequisite; disputes within the committees’ jurisdiction and objections to committee decisions are excluded from this requirement (Consumer Protection Law art. 73/A). The seller must also inform the consumer of these avenues in the pre-contractual information (Distance Contracts Regulation art. 5/1-k). Breaches of the obligations relating to distance contracts are, in addition, subject to an administrative fine for each transaction or contract in which the breach is established (Consumer Protection Law art. 77/1-a).

Court of Cassation Decisions

The decisions below have been verified against their full texts in the official case-law database. Decisions on consumer disputes were rendered by different chambers under the division of work in force at the time. Reversals in the interest of the law do not affect the outcome of judgments rendered with final effect; they reflect the Chamber’s legal assessment.

In a service contract, the withdrawal period starts on the day the contract is concluded

Court of Cassation Decision
13th Civil Chamber, E. 2015/25017, K. 2018/556, 25.01.2018 (the Chamber’s reversal reasoning; concerns a holiday package bought online)

“In the present case, the contract confirmed by the claimant for the purchase over the internet stated that withdrawal could be made within 14 days of the contract date, and under both article 48/4 of Law No. 6502 and article 9/2 of the relevant regulation, since the relevant notification was made, it is understood that the claimant could exercise the right of withdrawal from the contract date. The claimant, however, did not exercise this right within the statutory period; while the action should therefore have been dismissed, allowing it on the ground that the service had not yet been used is contrary to procedure and law and constitutes a ground for reversal.” (translation)

A consumer not informed of the right of withdrawal is not bound by the fourteen-day period

Court of Cassation Decision
3rd Civil Chamber, E. 2020/5130, K. 2021/6041, 04.06.2021 (the Chamber’s own reasoning; the local court’s judgment was upheld with its reasoning replaced)

“Examination of the file and of the contract relied on in the action shows that the defendant company did not inform the claimant consumer “about the right of withdrawal”. In that case it cannot be said that the claimant was bound by the 14-day period. That being so, the consumer may withdraw from the contract by bringing an action within one year of the end of the withdrawal period. Since in the present action the claimant brought the action within the one-year period provided by law, the action must be allowed.” (translation)

A mobile phone is not covered by the exception; use beyond normal use is determined by an expert

Court of Cassation Decision
3rd Civil Chamber, E. 2022/6784, K. 2022/8533, 07.11.2022 (the Chamber’s reasoning for reversal in the interest of the law; the reversal does not affect the outcome of the judgment)

“… it is understood that a mobile phone, where it is the subject of a distance sales contract, does not constitute an exception to the 14-day right of withdrawal. In addition, the last sentence of article 48/4 of Law No. 6502 and article 13/2 of the Distance Contracts Regulation provide that the consumer is liable for deterioration exceeding normal use in exercising the right of withdrawal. … That being so, whereas the court should have obtained a report, open to review by the parties, the court and the Court of Cassation, from an expert in the field as to whether the defendant’s use of the mobile phone for 6 days during the withdrawal period, in exercising the right of withdrawal from the distance sales contract, exceeded normal use, bearing in mind that if it did the defendant consumer would be liable for the loss suffered by the claimant seller, and then ruled accordingly, deciding as stated was contrary to procedure and law…” (translation)

A platform that merely provides an e-commerce environment is not liable for defects in the goods

Court of Cassation Decision
3rd Civil Chamber, E. 2021/4000, K. 2021/11403, 15.11.2021 (the Chamber’s reasoning for reversal in the interest of the law; concerns the period before the 2022 statutory amendment and a claim for defective goods)

“That being so, whereas the court should have allowed the action, bearing in mind that under article 9 of Law No. 6563 and article 6 of the Regulation on Service Providers and Intermediary Service Providers in Electronic Commerce the claimant, as an intermediary service provider, bears no liability arising from the goods being defective, dismissing the action on an erroneous assessment was not found correct, and the Ministry of Justice’s request for appeal in the interest of the law on this point must be accepted.” (translation)

A platform that collects the price on the seller’s behalf is liable as an electronic intermediary service provider

Court of Cassation Decision
11th Civil Chamber, E. 2022/3467, K. 2022/6446, 29.09.2022 (the Chamber’s assessment based on the legislative rationale; rendered under the statutory text in force at the time; the judgment was reversed in the interest of the law as regards the extent of liability as carrier)

“… that they also collect the price of the goods or services from the consumer on behalf of the seller or provider, that in this case these persons will also be liable to consumers, like sellers or providers, for the matters set out in the article, but that internet sites which merely enable sellers or providers and consumers to communicate without collecting the price, and which generally have the character of hosting providers, will not be subject to the provisions of this paragraph. Accordingly, the defendant is liable as an electronic intermediary service provider, and the court’s finding to that effect is correct.” (translation)

Frequently Asked Questions

Within how many days can I return a product bought online?

In distance contracts the consumer may withdraw within fourteen days without giving reasons and without paying a penalty. For goods the period starts on the day of delivery, for services on the day the contract is concluded (Distance Contracts Regulation art. 9). After sending the withdrawal notice you must send the goods back within fourteen days, unless the seller has offered to collect them (Distance Contracts Regulation art. 13/1).

When must the seller refund the money I paid?

The seller must refund all payments received, including delivery costs, in a single payment and in a manner appropriate to your means of payment, within fourteen days of the date on which the goods are handed to the carrier specified for returns in the pre-contractual information; if you use a different carrier, the period starts when the goods reach the seller (Distance Contracts Regulation art. 12).

Who pays for return shipping?

If you send the goods back with the carrier specified by the seller for returns, you cannot be held liable for the return costs; if the seller did not specify any carrier for returns in the pre-contractual information, no return costs can be claimed from you either (Distance Contracts Regulation art. 12/5). The current wording of this rule applies from 1/1/2026.

Can I return a product whose box I have opened or which I have tried out?

Opening the packaging does not remove the right of withdrawal for every product; the cases in which the right cannot be exercised once the packaging is opened are listed in the Regulation (for example goods unsuitable for return for health and hygiene reasons, and books, digital content and computer consumables on a tangible medium). You are not liable for changes resulting from trying the product out to a normal extent; for use beyond normal use, however, you may be held liable for the seller’s loss. The Court of Cassation held that for a mobile phone used for six days this must be determined by an expert report.

What happens if the seller did not inform me about the right of withdrawal?

The seller bears the burden of proving that the information was given. If you were not properly informed, you are not bound by the fourteen-day period; however, the right ends in any event one year after the withdrawal period expires (Consumer Protection Law art. 48/4). The Court of Cassation has accepted that a consumer not informed of the right of withdrawal may withdraw by bringing an action within that one-year period.

Can I claim the refund from the platform for a product bought on a marketplace?

If the platform collected the price on the seller’s behalf and the price has not been transferred to the seller after delivery, the platform is jointly and severally liable with the seller for the refund obligation relating to the right of withdrawal. The remedies for defects are outside this joint liability (Consumer Protection Law art. 48/6).
