Lifelong Care Contracts in Türkiye (TCO Arts. 611–618): Form, Duty of Care, Termination, Simulation, Abatement

Short answer: Under Turkish law, a lifelong care contract is invalid unless made in the form of a contract of inheritance (before an official and two witnesses), even if it does not appoint an heir. For a contract made by a state-recognised care institution in compliance with the conditions set by the competent authorities, written form is sufficient (Code of Obligations art. 612, Civil Code art. 545). If a breach of the duty of care makes the contract unbearable, the parties may terminate it without notice (art. 617); the 7th Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation treats this right as one the care recipient must exercise during his or her lifetime. If heirs prove that the contract in fact concealed a gift made to deprive them of their inheritance, they may as a rule seek cancellation of title; if that intention is not proven, the 1974 Unification Decision does not apply, but the heirs’ right to abatement is expressly reserved by statute (art. 615/3).

This article explains Turkish law. It is an English translation of the Turkish original; in case of any discrepancy, the Turkish text prevails.

In brief

Under Turkish law, a lifelong care contract is invalid unless it is made in the form of a contract of inheritance, before an official and two witnesses. The article explains the duty of care, the termination routes in arts. 616–618 of the Code of Obligations and heirs' simulation and abatement claims, with decisions of the Assembly of Civil Chambers and the Court of Cassation.

Under Turkish law: the official form required for a valid lifelong care contract, the caregiver’s obligations, termination of the contract, and the simulation and abatement claims heirs may raise after the care recipient’s death (Code of Obligations arts. 611–618).

What Is a Lifelong Care Contract?

A lifelong care contract (ölünceye kadar bakma sözleşmesi) is a contract under which a person undertakes to transfer all or part of his or her property to another person in return for being cared for and looked after until death. The person cared for is the care recipient, and the person providing care is the caregiver. A common example is an elderly parent transferring a house under such a contract to a child or grandchild who will look after him or her.

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 611 (Definition)

“A lifelong care contract is a contract under which the caregiver undertakes to care for and look after the care recipient until death, and the care recipient undertakes to transfer to the caregiver his or her property or certain property assets.

If the caregiver has been appointed as heir by the care recipient, the provisions on contracts of inheritance apply to the lifelong care contract.”

Court of Cassation decisions describe it as a contract for consideration. Most disputes with heirs turn on whether the contract was really made for care or to conceal a gift.

The Assembly of Civil Chambers has stated that, for the contract to be valid, the care recipient need not be in particular need of care on the date of the contract, and that the need arising later or lasting only a very short time until death does not affect validity. However, the absence of a need for care is one of the facts the court considers in a simulation dispute.

Form: An Official and Two Witnesses

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 612 (Form)

“A lifelong care contract, even if it does not include the appointment of an heir, is not valid unless it is made in the form of a contract of inheritance.

If the contract has been made by a care institution recognised by the State in compliance with the conditions determined by the competent authorities, written form is sufficient for its validity.”

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code (Law No. 4721) art. 545 (Form of the contract of inheritance)

“For a contract of inheritance to be valid, it must be drawn up in the form of an official will.

The parties to the contract declare their wishes to the official at the same time and sign the contract so drawn up in the presence of the official and two witnesses.”

The result of these two provisions is that the parties declare their wishes to the official together and the contract is signed before the official and two witnesses. Therefore an ordinary written document, a document without witnesses or a text with merely certified signatures does not satisfy this form. In its decision cited below, the 7th Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation, referring to a decision of the Assembly of Civil Chambers dated 06.02.2008, stated that no judgment for cancellation of title and registration can be given on the basis of a contract not drawn up in official form.

In the cited decisions the contracts were drawn up by a notary as an “official deed” with two witnesses. In a case in Aksaray, the court of first instance held that a contract signed before a notary in the presence of two witnesses met the form requirements; the decision passed the regional appeal stage and was upheld by the 7th Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation with general reasoning (see the decisions section).

Scope of the Duty of Care and the Care Recipient’s Security

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 614 (Subject matter)

“Upon the conclusion of the contract, the care recipient joins the caregiver’s household. The caregiver is obliged to perform for the care recipient the performances required by equity according to the value of the property received and the social position the care recipient previously enjoyed.

The caregiver is obliged, in particular, to provide the care recipient with suitable food and housing, to look after him or her with due care in illness and to have him or her treated.”

The 7th Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation states that, unless otherwise agreed, the duty of care covers taking the care recipient into the family, providing a home, food and clothing, treatment in illness and every kind of moral help and support. The parties may agree otherwise in the contract; for example, they may agree that the care recipient stays in his or her own home.

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 613 (Security)

“A care recipient who has transferred real property to the caregiver has, in order to secure his or her rights, a statutory mortgage right over that property like a seller.”

Where the care recipient agrees that ownership will pass after death rather than transferring it at the time of the contract, according to the Court of Cassation the care recipient’s heirs are bound to transfer ownership; a caregiver who has performed the duty of care may bring an action for cancellation of title and registration against the heirs if they fail to do so.

Termination and End of the Contract

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 616/1 (Termination with notice)

“If there is a significant disproportion between the parties’ performances and the party receiving more cannot prove that the aim was to make a gift to him or her, the other party may terminate the contract at any time, provided that notice is given six months in advance. In determining this disproportion, the difference between the capital value corresponding to the value of what was given to the caregiver and the annuity to be paid, as determined by the relevant social security institution, is taken as the basis.”

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 617 (Termination without notice)

“If, owing to conduct contrary to the obligations arising from the contract, the continuation of the contract becomes unbearable, or other important reasons make its continuation impossible or excessively difficult, either party may terminate the contract without notice. Where the contract is terminated on one of these grounds, the party at fault returns what it received and is obliged to pay the party not at fault appropriate compensation for the damage suffered as a result.

The judge may find the termination without notice justified or, at the request of one of the parties or of his or her own motion, may end their life in a common household and award the care recipient a life annuity.”

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 618 (Death of the caregiver)

“If the caregiver dies, the care recipient may request termination of the contract within one year. In that case the care recipient may claim from the caregiver’s heirs payment of an amount equal to what he or she could have claimed from the bankruptcy estate if the caregiver had become bankrupt.”

Under art. 617, if the contract is terminated on this ground, the party at fault returns what it received; the 7th Civil Chamber decision cited below examined, within this framework, an action for cancellation of title and registration brought by a care recipient who alleged that care had not been provided. Even where termination is not proven, the judge’s option in the second paragraph should be kept in mind: in the decision cited below, the Chamber held, in a case where living together had become impossible, that even if the termination claim was rejected, maintenance to be assessed with regard to the elements of art. 617/2 (in the statute’s words, a life annuity) should have been awarded.

According to the 7th Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation, the right to seek termination on the ground that care was not provided must be exercised by the care recipient during his or her lifetime; once the care recipient dies, the heirs cannot set the contract aside on that ground.

Art. 615 of the Code of Obligations also gives persons to whom the care recipient owes statutory maintenance the right to seek annulment of the contract if, because of it, the care recipient can no longer meet that obligation; instead of annulment, the judge may order maintenance payments.

Heirs’ Simulation and Abatement Claims

The deceased’s simulated transaction. If the deceased, in order to deprive heirs of property, makes it appear in the land registry that real estate he or she actually wished to give away was transferred under a lifelong care contract, the apparent contract is void for simulation and the concealed gift is void because it was not made in official form. The Assembly of Civil Chambers identifies the main source of this rule as the Court of Cassation’s Unification Decision dated 01.04.1974, E. 1974/1, K. 1974/2. The operative sentence quoted by the Assembly of Civil Chambers refers to the will being declared “in the direction of a sale” for real estate actually intended as a gift; the decisions of the Assembly of Civil Chambers and the 1st Civil Chamber apply that decision also to lifelong care contracts in which the aim of making a gift is found to have prevailed. For details on this subject, see our article on the deceased’s simulated transactions.

The same decisions draw the limit: if the intention to deprive heirs is not proven, the 1974 Unification Decision does not apply. The court looks together at the deceased’s age, state of health, family relations and the proportion of the transferred property to the whole estate; in the cited decisions simulation was found where the transfer clearly exceeded a reasonable proportion and relations were poor.

Abatement. The law expressly reserves the heirs’ right to bring an abatement action and creditors’ right to bring an action for annulment against a lifelong care contract.

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 615/3 (Annulment and abatement)

“The heirs’ rights to bring an abatement action and the creditors’ rights to bring an action for annulment are reserved.”

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code (Law No. 4721) art. 565 (Gifts subject to abatement; opening sentence and item 4)

“The following gratuitous dispositions are subject to abatement like dispositions upon death:

… Dispositions made by the deceased that are clearly intended to circumvent the rules on reserved shares.”

The inference of this article is as follows: art. 565 of the Civil Code subjects “gratuitous dispositions” to abatement, whereas lifelong care is a contract with performances on both sides. The abatement debate therefore concentrates on the part of the transferred property’s value that clearly exceeds the care provided and remains without consideration. In practice heirs generally ask first for cancellation of title and registration based on the deceased’s simulated transaction and, if that is not accepted, for abatement (alternative claims). In one of the cited cases the regional court of appeal said that abatement could not apply because the contract was for consideration; the 1st Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation reversed the decision on the simulation point and made no separate assessment of abatement. We have not verified a general principle of the Court of Cassation on this question in this article. Note: by its decision E. 2025/4860, K. 2026/3278, 16.06.2026, the 7th Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation upheld, with general reasoning, a regional court of appeal decision dismissing the action on the grounds that simulation had not been proven and that a lifelong care contract, being a contract for consideration, is not subject to abatement; the Chamber did not lay down a separate principle (see the decisions section). For the calculation of abatement and its time limits, see our article on abatement actions.

Procedure: Who, Against Whom, With What Evidence?

  • The care recipient (during his or her lifetime): may seek termination under art. 617 of the Code of Obligations and registration of the transferred real estate in his or her own name, alleging that care was not provided. In the cited decisions these actions were heard by the civil court of general jurisdiction (asliye hukuk mahkemesi).
  • The caregiver: if ownership is to pass after death, may bring an action for cancellation of title and registration against the care recipient’s heirs, asserting that the duty of care was performed. In such an action, according to the 7th Civil Chamber, the heirs’ defence that no care was provided is not heard.
  • The heirs: may seek cancellation of title and registration in proportion to their inheritance shares on the basis of the deceased’s simulated transaction, and failing that abatement. According to the Assembly of Civil Chambers, the burden of proof lies on the claimant alleging simulation; since heirs are third parties to the contract, they may rely on any evidence, including witnesses.
  • Time limits: art. 616 of the Code of Obligations requires six months’ notice; under art. 618, if the caregiver dies, the care recipient may request termination within one year. Time limits for abatement actions are regulated separately in the Civil Code; those provisions are not quoted in this article.

The cited decisions come from the 1st and 7th Civil Chambers and the Assembly of Civil Chambers of the Court of Cassation; this article has not examined the division-of-work decisions.

Lifelong Care Contracts in Court Decisions

Criteria for simulation: the Assembly of Civil Chambers

Court of Cassation Assembly of Civil Chambers Decision
Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2022/388, K. 2024/97, 14.02.2024 (reversal of the decision of resistance; by majority)

“For this purpose it is necessary to make use of facts such as whether the deceased had a justified and reasonable ground for making the contract, his or her relations with the caregiver and the other heirs, the deceased’s age, state of health and the proportion of the transferred property to the whole estate. … Where the deceased had no intention of depriving the heirs of property, there is no possibility of applying the Unification Decision dated 01.04.1974, E. 1974/1, K. 1974/2.”

In this case the deceased had transferred shares in three plots to her daughters under a lifelong care contract in 2001 and another share by gift in 2009, leaving only one plot in which she was a co-owner. The court of first instance, accepting that care had been provided, rejected the claim for cancellation of title for the plots transferred under the lifelong care contract (granting abatement for the share transferred by gift) and insisted on its decision. The Assembly of Civil Chambers, considering that far more than a reasonable proportion had been transferred although the need for care could have been met with fewer plots, and that relations between the deceased and the claimants’ mother were poor, concluded that the intention to deprive the heirs had been proven. The decision was given by majority; during the deliberations a contrary view was expressed that it is normal for a person who wants relatives to provide care to transfer a large part of his or her property.

Exceeding a reasonable proportion: the 1st Civil Chamber

Court of Cassation Decision
1st Civil Chamber, E. 2023/4290, K. 2025/3276, 24.06.2025 (reversal of the regional court of appeal’s decision)

“having regard to the ratio of the value of the transferred real estate to the total value of the deceased’s remaining real estate, the limit that may be regarded as reasonable was exceeded, and the deceased transferred his most valuable real estate although he could have given less in order to be cared for”

In this case the 72-year-old deceased had transferred the bare ownership of a share in his most valuable property to his son from his second marriage under a lifelong care contract. The regional court of appeal, accepting a need for care, had dismissed the action. The Chamber compared the value of the transferred share on the date of the action (TRY 1,047,539.00) with the total value of the remaining property (TRY 412,096.44) and, also considering witness statements showing poor relations with the children of the first marriage, concluded that the transfer was simulated.

The right of termination and the end of living together: the 7th Civil Chamber

Court of Cassation Decision
7th Civil Chamber, E. 2023/2967, K. 2024/2503, 09.05.2024 (reversal of the court of first instance’s decision)

“Since the right to seek termination of the contract on the ground that the duty of care was not performed is a right that the care recipient must exercise during his or her lifetime, the defence of the care recipient’s heirs that the caregiver did not perform is not heard in this action. … in view of the fact that it had become impossible for the care recipient and the caregiver to live together, it was not found correct to render judgment as written instead of awarding maintenance to be assessed having regard also to the elements in article 617/2 of the Turkish Code of Obligations”

The care recipient had sought cancellation of title and registration, alleging that after the transfer the grandson did not look after him or her and drove him or her out of the home. After the reversal, the court of first instance dismissed the action on the ground that a breach of the duty of care had not been proven. The Chamber reversed that decision because, even if termination was not proven, the option in art. 617/2 had to be assessed since it had become impossible for the parties to live together.

The form of a contract made before a notary with two witnesses

Court of Cassation Decision (reasoning of the court of first instance upheld)
7th Civil Chamber, E. 2025/4862, K. 2026/2864, 02.06.2026 (regional court of appeal’s decision upheld; the quotation is the reasoning of the court of first instance)

“the contract was signed before a notary and in the presence of two witnesses, and upon examination it was understood that the form requirements were fully met”

The defendant had argued that the contract was drawn up like a will and contained no statement that the declarations had been made in the witnesses’ presence; this objection was rejected at first instance and on appeal, and the Chamber upheld the decision. Because the decision was upheld with general reasoning, it cannot be said that the Chamber laid down a separate principle on form; the decision depends on an examination of the elements of the particular document.

An abatement objection: approval with general reasoning

Court of Cassation Decision (reasoning of the regional court of appeal upheld)
7th Civil Chamber, E. 2025/4860, K. 2026/3278, 16.06.2026 (regional court of appeal’s decision upheld; the quotation is the reasoning of the regional court of appeal)

“the allegation that the transfer was made to deprive heirs and was simulated was also not proven, and since the lifelong care contract is a contract for consideration, it is not subject to abatement”

The court of first instance had found an intention to deprive heirs, the regional court of appeal dismissed the action, and the Chamber upheld that decision with general reasoning. Since the approval gives no reasons, it cannot be regarded as a principle laid down by the Court of Cassation on abatement; it is nevertheless an outcome to be taken into account for an abatement claim.

Check for later developments. Decisions of the Assembly of Civil Chambers and the 7th Civil Chamber after 2024 repeat the same criteria; no statutory amendment was found; no Unification Decision on the subject was found among the Grand General Assembly decisions available in the official case-law database up to 25.01.2023, and later decisions were not separately searched. The contrary decision upholding a judgment on abatement is shown separately above. Arts. 611–618 of the Code of Obligations are quoted in their current wording. Since the Assembly of Civil Chambers’ decision indicated that rectification of decision was available, its finality has not been verified in this article.

Where care was genuinely provided and no reason to deprive the heirs is shown, the transfer is not heir-deceiving simulation

Court of Cassation, Assembly of Civil Chambers (the Assembly’s reasoning)
Yargıtay Hukuk Genel Kurulu, E. 2021/1, K. 2023/168, T. 08.03.2023

“…it is understood that the immovable in dispute represented a reasonable value within the deceased’s assets on the date of the contract and that the defendant performed the obligation undertaken by the contract by caring for the deceased until his death. … In view of all these facts, it is understood that the deceased transferred his immovable by a lifelong care contract not merely to create an appearance but sincerely to secure care, that he did not intend to deprive his other heirs of property, and that the defendant, as care debtor, performed his counter-obligation by caring for his father; a contract made in return for genuine care cannot be said to be invalid on the ground of heir-deceiving simulation.” (unofficial translation)

In the case, the 68-year-old deceased had transferred, in 1995, his share in an immovable to his son by a lifelong care contract. The other heirs, claiming that the share formed most of his assets and that they themselves had cared for him, sought annulment of title and registration on the ground of heir-deceiving simulation, or failing that abatement. The regional court of appeal upheld the claim and insisted on its judgment against the reversal of the 1st Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation. The Assembly took into account that the defendant let the deceased benefit from his health insurance, took him to the doctor and had him treated, that the deceased’s health was not very good, that he never stated or claimed after the contract that he was not cared for, and that there was no evidence of strained relations with the other children or of any reason to deprive them of property. On the transfer date the deceased also had a house and some land in the village. The insistence judgment was reversed by majority on the grounds stated in the Chamber’s reversal. The dissent considered that the defendant’s attention fell within a child’s moral duty and that, since almost all the assets had been transferred, the intent to deprive the heirs should prevail. The contract was made under the former Code of Obligations No. 818; the Assembly cited art. 611 of Code No. 6098 together with former art. 511 of Code No. 818. The Assembly did not separately address the abatement claim. This decision shows the same criteria as in the 2024 Assembly decision above leading to the opposite result; the outcome depends on the specific facts.

A care contract made with a spouse and ended by death shortly afterwards is not in itself immoral

Court of Cassation, Assembly of Civil Chambers (the Assembly’s reasoning)
Yargıtay Hukuk Genel Kurulu, E. 2021/234, K. 2023/259, T. 22.03.2023

“…since the parties being spouses (husband and wife), the transaction being based on a lifelong care contract and the care creditor dying about one month after the contract do not in themselves show immorality, that reasoning of the Regional Court of Appeal is not correct. … Moreover, the care debtor being the transferor’s spouse does not prevent a transfer to him or her on condition of care, nor is there any statutory provision to the contrary.” (unofficial translation)

In the case, the deceased, in the final stage of cancer, had transferred her independent unit to her spouse by a lifelong care contract twenty-seven days before her death; by a will of 1991 she had left all her assets to her sibling. The sibling’s side sought annulment of title and registration on the grounds of incapacity, breach of form, immorality and heir-deceiving simulation. The regional court of appeal found the transfer immoral and simulated, upheld the claim and insisted on its judgment against the reversal of the 1st Civil Chamber. The Assembly first held unanimously that reviewing the legal consequences attached to established facts is a review of law which the Court of Cassation may carry out. On the merits, it took into account that relations between the deceased and her siblings were good, that the immovable was not all her assets, that the will was never revoked, that a Forensic Medicine report found her to have had capacity, and that the defendant performed the duty of care, and concluded that neither an intent to deprive the heirs nor immorality had been proved. It added that the contract lasting only a very short time until death does not affect its validity, and that a transfer in return for services to a spouse needing attention beyond ordinary care is to be regarded as made for consideration. The insistence judgment was reversed on the grounds stated in the Chamber’s reversal; the first issue was decided unanimously and the second, on the merits, by majority. During deliberations, the view that the transaction was made as a gift in the knowledge that death was near, and that the judgment should be upheld, was not adopted by the majority. The contract was made in 2010, under the former Code of Obligations No. 818.

Practical Points

  • Form: the contract must be made before an official, with two witnesses and following the procedure in art. 545 of the Civil Code; a defect of form is the most basic obstacle to a claim for cancellation of title and registration.
  • Content of care: the contract should state where, by whom and to what extent care will be provided. Unless otherwise agreed, the Court of Cassation expects the care recipient to be taken into the family.
  • Documenting care: the caregiver should be able to show, through hospital records, payments and witnesses, that care was actually provided.
  • Proportion of the transfer: transferring the most valuable asset or most of the estate is treated as a fact that strengthens a simulation claim.
  • Timing of termination: a care recipient who believes care is not being provided should exercise the right of termination during his or her lifetime; after death the heirs cannot rely on that ground.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a lifelong care contract valid in Türkiye if it is not made before a notary with two witnesses?

As a rule, no. Under art. 612 of the Turkish Code of Obligations, a lifelong care contract is invalid unless it is made in the form of a contract of inheritance, even if it does not appoint an heir; art. 545 of the Turkish Civil Code describes that form as the form of an official will and requires the parties to sign before the official and two witnesses. The only exception is a contract made by a state-recognised care institution in compliance with the conditions set by the competent authorities; written form is then sufficient.

How is a lifelong care contract terminated under Turkish law if the caregiver does not provide care?

If a breach of the duty of care makes the continuation of the contract unbearable, either party may terminate it without notice; the party at fault returns what it received and pays appropriate compensation (Code of Obligations art. 617). Instead of termination, the judge may end their life in a common household and award the care recipient a life annuity. The 7th Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation treats the right to terminate for failure to provide care as a right the care recipient must exercise during his or her lifetime.

Can heirs in Türkiye challenge a lifelong care contract after the care recipient's death by claiming that no care was provided?

According to the cited decision of the 7th Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation, in an action for cancellation of title and registration brought by the caregiver against the heirs, the heirs' defence that the duty of care was not performed is not heard, because the right of termination must be exercised by the care recipient during his or her lifetime. This does not prevent heirs from relying on other grounds such as simulation or a defect of form.

How do heirs in Türkiye prove that a lifelong care contract was a simulated transaction by the deceased?

The burden of proof lies on the heir alleging simulation; since heirs are third parties to the contract, they may rely on any evidence, including witnesses. The Assembly of Civil Chambers of the Court of Cassation requires an examination of whether the deceased had a justified and reasonable ground for the contract, his or her relations with the caregiver and the other heirs, age, health and the proportion of the transferred property to the whole estate. If the intention to deprive heirs is not proven, the Unification Decision of 01.04.1974 does not apply.

Can heirs in Türkiye bring an abatement action for real estate transferred under a lifelong care contract?

Art. 615/3 of the Turkish Code of Obligations expressly reserves the heirs' right to bring an abatement action against a lifelong care contract. In practice heirs usually ask first for cancellation of title on the ground of the deceased's simulated transaction and, failing that, for abatement. A principle of the Court of Cassation on how far abatement applies to this contract for consideration has not been verified in this article; the 7th Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation upheld, with general reasoning, a regional court of appeal decision holding that the contract is not subject to abatement because it is for consideration. The view that the value exceeding the care provided may be subject to abatement is this article's own inference.
