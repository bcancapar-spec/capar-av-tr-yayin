Annulment of Title for Gabin (Exploitation) in Türkiye: Elements, Time Limits, Valuation (TBK Art. 28)

Short answer: If a property was sold clearly below its real value by exploiting the seller’s distress, thoughtlessness or inexperience, the seller may choose between two remedies. The seller may either declare that he or she is not bound by the contract and claim the property back (annulment of title and re-registration), or remain bound and claim removal of the disproportion between the performances (Code of Obligations art. 28). This right must be exercised within one year from the date the thoughtlessness or inexperience was discovered or, in the case of distress, from the date the distress ended, and in any event within five years from the conclusion of the contract. The Court of Cassation requires, in addition to an excessive disproportion, proof of the seller’s distress, inexperience or thoughtlessness and of the buyer’s intent to exploit it; a low price alone is not enough. The disproportion is assessed by reference to the value of the property at the moment the contract was concluded, on the basis of an expert report fit for review.

This article explains Turkish law. It is an English translation of the Turkish original; in case of any discrepancy, the Turkish text prevails.

In brief

If a property was sold clearly below its value by exploiting the seller’s distress, thoughtlessness or inexperience, the seller may, under Code of Obligations art. 28, declare that he is not bound by the contract and claim annulment of title and re-registration, or keep the sale and claim removal of the disproportion. This article explains the legal nature of gabin and the differences from Law No. 818, the objective and subjective elements and the intent to exploit, the one-year and five-year preclusive periods, valuation at the date of sale by expert examination and site inspection, the understated title-deed price, mutual restitution, transfers to third parties, proof, heirs, the competent court, mediation and interim injunctions, with decisions of the Grand Civil Chamber and the chambers of the Court of Cassation.

What Is Gabin (Exploitation)?

Gabin, called excessive benefit (aşırı yararlanma) in the Code of Obligations, is a clear disproportion between the performances created by exploiting the injured party’s distress, thoughtlessness or inexperience; the typical case in a property sale is a purchase far below value, taking advantage of the seller’s hardship.

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 28 (Excessive benefit) (unofficial translation)

“Where there is a clear disproportion between the mutual performances in a contract and this disproportion was brought about by exploiting the injured party’s distress, thoughtlessness or inexperience, the injured party may, depending on the circumstances, either notify the other party that he is not bound by the contract and claim the return of his performance, or remain bound by the contract and claim removal of the disproportion between the performances. The injured party may exercise this right within one year from the date on which he discovered his thoughtlessness or inexperience or, in the case of distress, from the date on which the distress ended, and in any event within five years from the date the contract was concluded.”

Legal nature

In the Turkish Code of Obligations, excessive benefit is placed under the heading “Content of the contract”, immediately after the provision on absolute nullity and before the provisions on defects of consent (mistake, deceit, duress). The Grand Civil Chamber regards the institution as a limit on freedom of contract and states that the legislature gave the weaker party the possibility of reviewing the value relationship between the performances and, if the conditions are met, of avoiding the contract or claiming removal of the disproportion (Grand Civil Chamber, E. 2024/399, K. 2025/643). The sanction is not absolute nullity: the contract stands unless the injured party exercises the right in time, and the court does not take exploitation into account of its own motion; whether to exercise the right, and which option to choose, is for the injured party.

Differences from the former Code of Obligations (Law No. 818)

As stated in the same Grand Civil Chamber decision, unlike the repealed Code, Code of Obligations art. 28 gives the injured party, in addition to declaring that he is not bound and claiming the return of his performance, the right to remain bound and claim removal of the disproportion. The time limit has also been reformulated: the one-year period now runs not from the conclusion of the contract but from discovery or from the end of the distress, and in addition an absolute five-year period running in all cases from the conclusion of the contract has been introduced. The older concepts of müzayaka, hiffet and tecrübesizlik are now expressed as distress, thoughtlessness and inexperience. Whether transactions made before the entry into force of the Turkish Code of Obligations (01.07.2012) are binding is, as a rule, governed by the law in force when the transaction was made (Law No. 6101 art. 1); however, since the one-year period of that era ran from the date of the contract, a gabin action is in practice no longer possible today for such sales.

For the general framework of the action and its other grounds, see our article Action for Annulment of Title and Re-Registration.

What Are the Elements of Gabin?

The Court of Cassation requires one objective and two subjective elements to be met together; the objective element is examined first and, if it is proven, the subjective element is investigated in depth.

Objective element: a clear disproportion between the performances

There must be a clear disproportion between the sale price and the property’s real value. The law sets no ratio; the assessment depends on the facts of each case. The Grand Civil Chamber requires the disproportion to be obvious, high enough to strike anyone familiar with the matter, and stresses that the judge must take as the basis the objective value of the performance at the moment the contract was made (decision below). Not every clear disproportion is excessive benefit; what is decisive is that it was created by taking advantage of the other party’s weak position.

Subjective element: distress, thoughtlessness or inexperience, and its exploitation

The seller must have been in distress, thoughtless or inexperienced, and the buyer must have acted with intent to exploit that situation. The court examines the seller’s age, health, financial strength and psychological make-up; mere financial difficulty may not be enough (see the decisions below). The Grand Civil Chamber states that these situations are listed exhaustively in the law, but that they need not all be present together, one of them sufficing together with the other conditions, and explains the concepts as follows (Grand Civil Chamber, E. 2024/399, K. 2025/643):

  • Distress: a state of hardship or lack of money; it may arise from economic or personal causes and may be material or non-material. Legal persons, like natural persons, may also be in distress.
  • Thoughtlessness: not a person’s generally light-minded character, but the failure to take care in the specific contract; being insufficiently competent in a particular matter, failing to see ahead or being unable properly to think through the consequences of the contract.
  • Inexperience: not general inexperience, but a lack of adequate knowledge and experience relating to the contract concluded.

Intent to exploit: the buyer must have knowingly used the weakness

The other party must have knowingly taken advantage of the injured party’s distress, thoughtlessness or inexperience. The Grand Civil Chamber states that if the party did not act with the idea of taking advantage of that weakness, excessive benefit cannot be spoken of even if the other elements are present. In a 2026 decision the 1st Civil Chamber likewise repeated that, for a contract to be vitiated by excessive benefit, the disproportion must result from one party knowingly abusing and exploiting a particular situation of the other. In the same case an independent unit valued on site inspection at TRY 401,892.79 had been transferred for TRY 102,000, and on the seller’s complaint a public prosecution for usury was brought against the buyer and another person. The Chamber reversed the judgment, holding that material facts established and finalised by a criminal court bind the civil court and that the outcome of the criminal case had to be awaited (1st Civil Chamber, E. 2024/4589, K. 2026/1762). While criminal proceedings are pending, they mean only that an allegation is being tried; that is precisely why the civil court must wait.

What Can the Seller Claim? Annulment of Title, Re-Registration and Return of the Price

Code of Obligations art. 28 gives the injured party two options:

  • Declare that he is not bound and claim the return of his performance: In a property sale this is a claim for annulment of title and re-registration; according to the Court of Cassation the injured party may prove the claim by any means of evidence.
  • Remain bound and claim removal of the disproportion: The sale stands and the disproportion between the performances is removed.

What happens to the price paid? Once the contract ceases to bind, the price received by the seller no longer rests on a valid cause. Under Code of Obligations art. 77, a person enriched on a cause that is not valid must return the enrichment; the price received by the seller is therefore also subject to return.

The second option: keeping the contract and removing the disproportion

The last part of the first paragraph of the article allows the injured party to have the imbalance removed without setting the sale aside. This option may be more suitable where the property has changed hands or the seller does not want it back. The claim is in the nature of a money claim and does not affect the land register entry. Since the law states that the choice is made “depending on the circumstances”, the statement of claim should state clearly which right is being exercised and, where necessary, rank the claims as principal and subsidiary. Characterisation is for the judge: the 6th Civil Chamber reversed a dismissal, holding that an action filed as one for determination of rent was in fact a claim for removal of the disproportion between the performances on grounds of excessive benefit, and required the claim to be assessed under Code of Obligations art. 28/1 (6th Civil Chamber, E. 2015/9296, K. 2016/4392).

Which chamber reviews the case? Recent appeals on points of law concerning claims for removal of the disproportion and compensation in property sales have been reviewed by the 3rd Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation; in a 2026 decision of that chamber the regional court of appeal’s judgment was affirmed after it was found that the objective element of gabin was absent (3rd Civil Chamber, E. 2025/4917, K. 2026/3599). Claims for annulment of title and re-registration, as the decisions in this article show, are reviewed by the 1st Civil Chamber. The division of work among the chambers is determined by the division-of-work decisions of the Court of Cassation’s Grand General Assembly and may change.

The 2026 amendment: since the difference to be claimed for removal of the disproportion can often be determined only by expert examination, such actions were in practice also brought as actions for an undetermined claim. By Law No. 7589, published in the Official Gazette of 31.07.2026, Code of Civil Procedure art. 107 was repealed, and the paragraph added to Code of Civil Procedure art. 109 provides that in a partial action the claim may be increased once in the same action until the end of the taking of evidence without being subject to the prohibition on extending the claim, limitation being deemed interrupted from the date of the action also for the increased part.

Within What Time Must a Gabin Action Be Brought?

The injured party may exercise the right within one year from the date on which he discovered his thoughtlessness or inexperience or, in the case of distress, from the date on which the distress ended, and in any event within five years from the conclusion of the contract (Code of Obligations art. 28/2). The Court of Cassation treats this as a preclusive period, not a limitation period, and checks whether the action was brought in time (see the decision below).

The Grand Civil Chamber states expressly that these periods apply to both options: if the injured party wishes to be released from the contract or to obtain removal of the disproportion, he must exercise the right within the one-year and five-year preclusive periods; the five-year period is absolute and runs in all cases from the conclusion of the contract (Grand Civil Chamber, E. 2024/399, K. 2025/643). Note that the upper limit is five years, not ten. Whichever period expires first applies: for a seller who sold on 1 March 2023 under pressure of debt and whose distress ended on 15 June 2027, the one-year period would expire on 15 June 2028, but the absolute five-year period expires on 1 March 2028, so the right must be exercised by that date at the latest. For deceit the law contains no such absolute period; where both are pleaded in the same case, the periods must be calculated separately (annulment of title for fraud).

How Is a Clear Disproportion Established? The Understated Title-Deed Price

The disproportion is established by comparing the property’s real value at the date of sale with the sale price; the value is determined by site inspection and expert examination. In a decision upheld with corrections by the Court of Cassation, the value at the date of sale of a property shown in the land registry at TRY 31,000 was found by expert assessment to be TRY 93,981.03 and the action was granted (1st Civil Chamber, E. 2022/2487, K. 2022/5795).

What must the expert report contain? The Grand Civil Chamber requires the objective element to be determined by an expert or a panel of experts in the field; the report must investigate factors affecting value such as the zoning status, location, area of use, road frontage and topography of the property, as well as comparable values, must also examine encumbrances such as attachments on the property at the time of sale, and must reflect the criteria in a form fit for review and for a judgment. In the same decision, establishing the value at the date of the action and deflating it to the date of sale with annual inflation data was held to be wrong; holding that the market values at the dates of transfer and of the action had to be determined separately by research into comparables, the Grand Chamber reversed the insistence ruling on different grounds (Grand Civil Chamber, E. 2024/399, K. 2025/643). A resale of the property at a higher price after the sale may be an indication of value, but the criterion is the value at the moment the contract was concluded, not the later market.

The counter-performance is not only the price in the title deed: debts paid by the buyer on the seller’s behalf, attachments lifted or other values given to the seller are also debated as part of the counter-performance. In the case decided by the Grand Civil Chamber in 2025, both sides accepted that the attachments on the property had been lifted by payments made by the defendants, and the amount paid had been determined separately by an accounting expert.

Understated title-deed price: The 1st Civil Chamber reversed a dismissal based on the view that understating the price to pay less fee is common practice, and gave no effect to the buyer’s defence of a higher payment that exceeded the limit for proof by witnesses and was not proven by a written document (see the decision below).

Mutual Restitution and Adjustment

When the seller declares that he is not bound by the contract, he claims, in the words of the law, “the return of his performance”; as the property returns to him, the price he received also loses its ground:

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 77 (Conditions of unjust enrichment) (unofficial translation)

“A person who, without just cause, is enriched from another’s assets or labour is obliged to return that enrichment. This obligation arises in particular where the enrichment is based on a cause that is not valid, has not materialised or has ceased to exist.”

In exploitation cases, unlike fraud cases, the buyer has usually paid a price, even if a low one; the amount to be returned may therefore be substantial. A person who parted with the enrichment not in good faith, or who ought to have reckoned that he might have to return it, must return the whole enrichment (Code of Obligations art. 79). As to expenditure on the property, an enriched party in good faith may claim necessary and useful expenses, whereas one not in good faith may claim only necessary expenses and, of the useful expenses, the increase in value existing at the time of restitution (Code of Obligations art. 80). A claim for unjust enrichment is time-barred two years from the date on which the right to reclaim was discovered and in any event ten years from the date of the enrichment (Code of Obligations art. 82).

What If the Property Has Passed to a Third Party?

When the seller declares that he is not bound by the contract, the registration in the buyer’s name rests on a non-binding legal transaction and is wrongful:

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code (Law No. 4721) art. 1024 (Against third parties not in good faith) (unofficial translation)

“If a right in rem has been wrongfully registered, a third party who knew or ought to have known this cannot rely on that registration. A registration based on a non-binding legal transaction or lacking a legal cause is wrongful. A person whose right in rem is impaired by such a registration may invoke its wrongfulness directly against third parties not acting in good faith.”

By contrast, a third party who acquires ownership relying in good faith on the registration is protected (Civil Code art. 1023); the property then cannot be recovered from that party, but claims for compensation are reserved (Civil Code art. 1025). According to the Chamber’s earlier reversal in the same file, reproduced in 1st Civil Chamber, E. 2022/3724, K. 2022/6882, where the property passed during the proceedings to a new registered owner against whom the action was redirected, that owner’s good faith must be assessed. If the property is transferred after the action is filed, the claimant may continue the action against the transferee or convert it into a claim for compensation against the transferor (Code of Civil Procedure art. 125).

If it changed hands before the action: the claim for annulment of title and re-registration must be directed against the registered owner at the date of the action. The court may not examine the subsidiary claim unless it dismisses the principal claim on the merits (Code of Civil Procedure art. 111); for this reason, bringing against a first buyer who is no longer the registered owner only an alternative action of the type “annulment of title, failing that the price” led, in an affirmed finding of a regional court of appeal, to dismissal of the annulment claim for lack of standing to be sued and to a ruling that there was no need to decide the claim for the price (1st Civil Chamber, E. 2026/1388, K. 2026/2071; the principle in that fraud case rests on the same procedural rule for gabin actions). An up-to-date land register extract should be obtained before filing; if the property has changed hands, the annulment claim should be brought against the new owner alleged not to be in good faith, and the claim for the price or compensation against the first buyer as a principal claim.

How Is Gabin Proved?

The burden of proof lies on the seller who alleges excessive benefit. Although the sale is made by official deed, an allegation of exploitation is expressly listed among the exceptions to the requirement of documentary proof:

Statutory Provision — Code of Civil Procedure (Law No. 6100) art. 203/1(ç) (Exceptions to the requirement of documentary proof) (unofficial translation)

“(1) Witnesses may be heard in the following cases: … ç) Allegations of defective consent and of exploitation in legal transactions.”

The objective element is proved by expert examination and site inspection, the subjective element by evidence showing the seller’s situation at the time: enforcement files and attachment annotations in the land register, bank and loan records, documents relating to debt payments, medical records, the parties’ correspondence and witness statements. The Grand Civil Chamber requires that, once the objective element is proved, the seller’s personality, age, health, social position, financial strength and psychological make-up be investigated in depth. The buyer’s intent to exploit is usually inferred from external facts: knowledge of the seller’s hardship, closeness or a debt relationship between the parties, an obvious mismatch between price and value, and high-value transactions shortly after the sale are relevant in this respect.

Incapacity and Forensic Medicine: an allegation that the seller lacked capacity of discernment at the time of the transaction is a ground separate from excessive benefit and, as it concerns public order, is examined first. In a case where incapacity, a decedent’s simulated transaction, fraud and gabin had been pleaded together but only simulation had been examined, the Court of Cassation reversed the judgment; it required the deceased’s medical reports, prescriptions and hospital observation records to be obtained and a report sought from the Fourth Specialised Board of the Forensic Medicine Institute, and the fraud and gabin allegations to be assessed if the deceased was found to have had capacity (1st Civil Chamber, E. 2018/2713, K. 2020/2953). Thoughtlessness, however, is not incapacity; it denotes the failure of a person with full capacity of discernment to take care in the specific contract.

How Does Gabin Differ from Fraud (Deceit)?

In fraud the seller is induced to contract by the other party’s deceit; under Code of Obligations art. 36 the deceived party is not bound even if the mistake was not fundamental. In gabin no deceit is required; what matters is the clear disproportion and the exploitation of the seller’s distress, thoughtlessness or inexperience (Code of Obligations art. 28). The periods also differ: for deceit the one-year period runs from discovery of the deceit (Code of Obligations art. 39), whereas gabin is additionally subject to a five-year long-stop running from the conclusion of the contract.

Both may arise from the same facts. In a 2025 Grand Civil Chamber decision, a claimant who had referred to excessive benefit in the statement of claim explained at the preliminary examination hearing that his consent had been vitiated by fraud; holding that stating the facts is for the parties and legal characterisation for the judge, the Grand Chamber accepted that the action had been based on fraud from the outset (Grand Civil Chamber, E. 2024/46, K. 2025/282).

Can the Heirs Sue if the Seller Has Died?

On the death of the deceased, the estate passes into the joint ownership of the heirs; the heirs dispose jointly of the rights belonging to the estate, and at the request of one heir the civil court of peace may appoint a representative for the community of heirs (Civil Code art. 640). In an action brought on behalf of the deceased for return to the estate and based also on fraud and excessive benefit, the 1st Civil Chamber required the court first to determine, as regards those allegations, whether the action had been brought within the preclusive period and to carry out the necessary investigation; it also stated that the other heirs who consented to the action were to be treated not as main interveners but as heirs consenting to an action for return to the estate (1st Civil Chamber, E. 2025/4817, K. 2026/3320). In such actions the heirs must seek registration in the name of the estate rather than in proportion to their own shares, and must obtain the consent of the other heirs or secure the appointment of a representative; for fraud this principle is explained in detail in our article on annulment of title for fraud.

Which Court Hears a Gabin Action?

Unless otherwise provided, the competent court is the civil court of first instance (Code of Civil Procedure art. 2). Because the action may change the holder of a right in rem in the property, the court where the property is located has exclusive jurisdiction (Code of Civil Procedure art. 12). The judgment fee and attorney’s fees are calculated not on the value stated in the statement of claim but on the value at the date of the action determined by site inspection (1st Civil Chamber, E. 2022/2487, K. 2022/5795).

Merchants and companies: since legal persons too may be in distress, companies may also rely on excessive benefit. Actions arising from matters relating to the commercial enterprises of both parties are commercial cases (Commercial Code art. 4) and are heard by the commercial court of first instance; in a judicial district without a commercial court, failure to rely on the rule of subject-matter jurisdiction does not require a ruling of lack of jurisdiction (Commercial Code art. 5). Limits of exclusive jurisdiction: exclusive jurisdiction applies to actions that may change the holder of a right in rem (Code of Civil Procedure art. 12); where the seller keeps the contract and claims only removal of the disproportion, the action becomes a money claim and jurisdiction may have to be determined under the general rules. Fee: in actions concerning the immovable itself, such as cancellation of a title entry, the value of the property is taken as the basis, and where compensation is also claimed, the value of the property plus the compensation; the value must be stated in the statement of claim (Law No. 492 art. 16).

Mediation and Interim Injunctions

Mediation: a claim for annulment of title and re-registration is not among the disputes in which recourse to a mediator before filing suit is a precondition; Law No. 6325 art. 18/B exhaustively lists those disputes as lease, partition and termination of co-ownership, condominium and neighbour-law disputes. By contrast, in a commercial case in which a sum of money or compensation is claimed, recourse to a mediator is a precondition (Commercial Code art. 5/A). This distinction matters particularly for exploitation: if, between merchants, the contract is kept and removal of the disproportion, that is payment of money, is claimed, the mediation process must be completed before filing suit. Where mediation is not mandatory, disputes over the transfer of property are nonetheless suitable for mediation; by the parties’ written agreement an annotation may be entered in the land register for the duration of the process, for a maximum of three months, the enforceability annotation on the settlement agreement is obtained from the civil court of peace where the property is located, and under the paragraph added by Law No. 7531 in 2024 registration may be carried out by the land registry without an official deed being drawn up (Law No. 6325 art. 17/B).

Interim injunction and annotation: a transfer of the property to a person in good faith during the proceedings may make restitution in kind impossible. An interim injunction may be ordered where it is feared that, owing to a change in the existing situation, obtaining the right will become considerably more difficult or entirely impossible (Code of Civil Procedure art. 389). Court orders for the protection of disputed rights may be annotated in the land register, and an annotated restriction may be asserted against those who subsequently acquire rights in the property (Civil Code art. 1010). The applicant must as a rule provide security; if the request is based on an official document or other conclusive evidence, or if the circumstances so require, the court may, stating its reasons expressly, dispense with security (Code of Civil Procedure art. 392).

What Does the Court of Cassation Look For in Gabin Cases?

One objective and two subjective elements are required together

Court of Cassation Decision
1st Civil Chamber, E. 2022/3724, K. 2022/6882, 20.10.2022 (Chamber’s reasoning; dismissal upheld with corrected reasoning as gabin was not proven)

“Accordingly, gabin can be spoken of only if, besides the objective element of an excessive disproportion between the performances, two subjective elements are also met: one party’s distress, inexperience or thoughtlessness (rashness), and the other party’s intent to take advantage of and exploit it.” (translation)

The disproportion is assessed at the value at the time of contracting, by a qualified expert

Court of Cassation Decision
Grand Civil Chamber, E. 2024/399, K. 2025/643, 22.10.2025 (the Grand Chamber’s own reasoning; the insistence ruling was reversed on different grounds by majority)

“As the objective element, the excessive disproportion between the performances must be obvious and high enough to strike anyone familiar with the matter. In establishing this, the judge must take as the basis the objective value of the performance at the moment the contract was made. … The objective element of gabin is established through an expert or a panel of experts in the field.” (translation)

Understated price: a defence of higher payment must be duly proven

Court of Cassation Decision
1st Civil Chamber, E. 2015/13939, K. 2018/11460, 25.06.2018 (Chamber’s reasoning for reversal; dismissal reversed)

“It is understood that although the real value of the property is TRY 200,000, it was shown as TRY 14,000 in the contract. Although the defendant argued that he paid TRY 200,000, this amount is outside the limit for proof by witnesses, and the payment has not been duly proven by a written document. It is therefore established that both the objective and the subjective elements of gabin were met in this case and that the action was brought in time.” (translation)

Financial difficulty alone is not enough for gabin (upheld finding)

Court of Cassation Decision
1st Civil Chamber, E. 2022/337, K. 2022/2580, 30.03.2022 (reasoning of the first-instance court; found correct and upheld by the Chamber)

“given that the claimant is still actually engaged in commercial life and went to negotiate together with an estate agent, the subjective conditions were not met either, and a sale made merely because the claimant’s financial situation was poor cannot be regarded as gabin” (translation)

A gabin action is subject to a preclusive period, not a limitation period

Court of Cassation Decision
1st Civil Chamber, E. 2022/2487, K. 2022/5795, 15.09.2022 (Chamber’s reasoning; judgment granting the action upheld with corrections)

“it being understood that actions based on gabin are subject, under art. 28 of the Turkish Code of Obligations, not to a limitation period but to a preclusive period, and that the present action was brought within the preclusive period on the evidence collected” (translation)

Frequently Asked Questions

What is gabin?

Gabin (excessive benefit) is a clear disproportion between the performances in a contract created by exploiting the injured party’s distress, thoughtlessness or inexperience (Code of Obligations art. 28). In a property sale the seller may declare that he is not bound by the contract and claim annulment of title and re-registration.

Can a property sold far below its value be recovered?

A low price alone is not enough; the seller’s distress, thoughtlessness or inexperience and the buyer’s knowing exploitation of it must also be proven. If the conditions are proven and the right is exercised in time, the property returns to the seller by a judgment of annulment and re-registration and the seller returns the price received; a good-faith third party’s acquisition is, however, protected (Civil Code art. 1023).

Within what time must a gabin action be brought?

Within one year from discovery of the thoughtlessness or inexperience or, in the case of distress, from the end of the distress, and in any event within five years from the conclusion of the contract (Code of Obligations art. 28/2). The upper limit is five years, not ten; the Court of Cassation treats this as a preclusive period, not a limitation period, and applies it to both options.

How is a clear disproportion established?

The property’s real value at the moment the contract was concluded is determined by site inspection and examination by a qualified expert, taking into account zoning status, location, comparable sales and encumbrances such as attachments, and is compared with the counter-performance actually given to the seller. The law sets no ratio; the disproportion must be high enough to strike anyone familiar with the matter.

Can only the difference be claimed without setting the sale aside?

Yes. The injured party may also remain bound by the contract and claim removal of the disproportion between the performances (Code of Obligations art. 28). This is a money claim subject to the same one-year and five-year periods; if the parties are merchants and the case is commercial, recourse to a mediator before filing suit is a precondition.

What is the difference between gabin and fraud?

In fraud the seller is induced to contract by deceit and is not bound even if the mistake was not fundamental (Code of Obligations art. 36). In gabin no deceit is required; a clear disproportion and the exploitation of the seller’s distress, thoughtlessness or inexperience are required (Code of Obligations art. 28).
