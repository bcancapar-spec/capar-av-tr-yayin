Wedding Jewellery (Ziynet) Claims on Divorce in Turkish Law: Who Owns the Gold, Proof, Return, Limitation

Short answer: Under Turkish law, according to the new principled view of the Court of Cassation’s 2nd Civil Chamber, wedding jewellery is divided first according to any agreement between the spouses. Failing an agreement, it is divided according to local custom if its existence is alleged and proved; otherwise, everything of economic value given to the man and to the woman belongs, as a rule, to that spouse, and items specific to women or to men are deemed given to that sex. The burden of proving that the jewellery existed and was taken by the other spouse lies, as a rule, on the woman who makes that claim; wedding photographs and recordings, witness statements, expert examination and the oath are assessed together. Unless it is proved that the jewellery was given not to be returned, the spouse who took it must return it, even if it was sold. The claim is heard by the family court, is subject to a proportional court fee, and seeks the return of the jewellery in kind or, failing that, payment of its value. If the jewellery still exists, limitation does not apply; if it does not, the ten-year limitation period in Code of Obligations art. 146 applies, and limitation does not run for claims between spouses while the marriage continues (Code of Obligations art. 153).

This article explains Turkish law. It is an English translation of the Turkish original; in case of any discrepancy, the Turkish text prevails.

In brief

Wedding jewellery (ziynet) claims on divorce under Turkish law: who owns the gold given at the wedding (agreement, local custom and the new principled criteria of the Court of Cassation’s 2nd Civil Chamber), jewellery as personal property (Civil Code art. 220), the burden of proof and the oath (art. 222), the return of jewellery that was sold and the “given not to be returned” defence, return in kind or value (Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law art. 24), the family court’s jurisdiction and proportional fees, and limitation (Code of Obligations arts. 146 and 153), with recent Court of Cassation decisions.

A guide to claims for wedding jewellery (ziynet) on divorce under Turkish law: who owns the gold and jewellery given at the wedding (agreement, local custom and the new principled criteria of the Court of Cassation’s 2nd Civil Chamber), jewellery as personal property (Civil Code art. 220), the burden of proof (Civil Code art. 222, Code of Civil Procedure art. 190), the return of jewellery that was sold and the “given not to be returned” defence, return in kind or payment of value (Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law art. 24), the competent family court and proportional court fees (Law No. 4787 art. 4), limitation (Code of Obligations art. 146 and art. 153) and verified Court of Cassation decisions.

What Is Ziynet? Jewellery as Personal Property (art. 220)

The Court of Cassation’s 2nd Civil Chamber defines ziynet as ornaments made of precious metals such as gold and silver and worn by people; it adds that ziynet can also be described as gifts given to the bride and groom on the occasion of the marriage, and that items such as bracelets, gold bangles, necklaces, chokers, jewellery sets, wristlets, watches, earrings and rings are accepted as ziynet (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2026/136, K. 2026/6720). According to the Chamber, items intended solely for personal use, such as clothing, jewellery, watches and ornaments, are the personal property of the spouse concerned:

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code (Law No. 4721) art. 220 (Personal property) (unofficial translation)

“The following are personal property by operation of law: 1. Items serving exclusively the personal use of one of the spouses, 2. Assets belonging to one of the spouses at the beginning of the matrimonial property regime or acquired later by one spouse through inheritance or in any other way without consideration, 3. Claims for non-pecuniary damages, 4. Assets replacing personal property.”

The consequence of being personal property is that the jewellery must be returned to the spouse who owns it: each spouse takes back his or her property held by the other (Civil Code art. 226). The Court of Cassation relies on this provision in holding that spouses may at any time request the return of their personal belongings held by the other (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2021/5038, K. 2021/6749). Division of property under the participation in acquired property regime is a separate claim (see Division of Property in Divorce: The Participation in Acquired Property Regime).

Who Owns the Gold Given at the Wedding? The Court of Cassation’s Criteria

This is the most disputed question in practice. The 2nd Civil Chamber’s earlier case law held that, unless there was an agreement or a rule of custom to the contrary, everything given at the wedding (jewellery, gold, foreign currency, Turkish lira, etc.), by whomever and to whichever spouse, was deemed to belong to the woman. Citing changes in social traditions over time and the practice of giving the couple, besides jewellery specific to women, other things of economic value to support their new life together, the Chamber has explained that it changed its case law and adopted a new view of a principled nature (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2026/136, K. 2026/6720; see the decision box below). Under this view:

Who Owns the Gold Given at the Wedding? The Court of Cassation’s Criteria
ItemExplanation
AgreementIf the parties have agreed on how the jewellery is to be shared, the sharing follows that agreement.
Local customAbsent an agreement, if the existence of a local custom is alleged and proven, the sharing follows that custom.
Ownership by the spouse who received the itemOtherwise, everything of economic value attached to or given to the man and to the woman belongs, as a rule, to each of them.
Items specific to one sexIf an item is specific to women or to men, it is deemed to have been given to that sex. Where this is disputed, an expert examination is carried out if necessary; an item found to be suitable for both sexes belongs to the spouse to whom it was attached or given.
The jewellery chest or bagAn item placed in the chest or bag that is specific to women or to men is deemed given to that sex; if it suits both sexes, it is treated as jointly owned.

The Chamber stresses that disputes must be resolved in line with these principles, taking the parties’ allegations and defences into account. A party relying on local custom must prove it, and property that cannot be shown to belong to either spouse is deemed to be in their co-ownership by law (Civil Code art. 222). At the same time, in files where the wife alleges that her jewellery was taken by the husband, the Chamber continues to use the formula that “jewellery attached at the wedding is deemed donated to the woman” (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2024/9098, K. 2025/5031).

Burden of Proof: Existence of the Jewellery and Its Taking

Unless the law provides otherwise, each party must prove the facts on which it bases its right (Civil Code art. 6); the burden of proof lies on the party deriving a right in its favour from the legal consequence attached to the alleged fact (Code of Civil Procedure art. 190). For property between spouses, the special rule of proof is Civil Code art. 222:

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code (Law No. 4721) art. 222 (Proof) (unofficial translation)

“A person who claims that a particular asset belongs to one of the spouses must prove that claim. Assets that cannot be proven to belong to either spouse are deemed to be in their co-ownership. All assets of a spouse are accepted as acquired property until the contrary is proven.”

According to the Court of Cassation, the burden of proof falls on the party alleging a situation contrary to the ordinary course of life. In jewellery claims, the ordinary situation is that jewellery specific to women is kept under the wife’s protection; a wife who alleges the contrary must prove it, and both the existence of the gold and the fact that it is not with the wife must be proven beyond doubt (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2026/136, K. 2026/6720). In another decision, noting that such items are easy to hide and carry and that a woman planning to leave the home can always take them beforehand, the Chamber stressed that the wife bears the burden of proving both the existence of the jewellery and that it was taken from her by the husband (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2024/10485, K. 2025/11756; see the decision box below).

What evidence? The decisions assess wedding photographs and footage, witness statements, expert examination and the oath together (3rd Civil Chamber, E. 2022/2972, K. 2022/4456; 2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2025/2400, K. 2025/5136). However, where the witnesses only know what they heard from the wife, their statements do not prove the claim; if the wife has not relied on the oath either, the unproven claim is dismissed (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2024/10485, K. 2025/11756). If the wife has relied on the oath, she must be reminded of her right to tender it and, if she does, the other party must be made to take the oath in due form (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2026/136, K. 2026/6720). It is therefore important to rely expressly on the oath in the statement of claim.

Jewellery That Was Sold and the Duty to Return It: The “Given Not to Be Returned” Defence

Jewellery is often sold during the marriage to buy a home or a car or to pay debts. The basic statutory rule is clear:

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code (Law No. 4721) art. 226 (Recovery of property and debts) (unofficial translation)

“Each spouse takes back his or her property held by the other spouse. …”

According to the Court of Cassation, unless it is alleged and proven that the jewellery was given on the understanding that it would not be claimed back, the person who took it must return the gold; if that is proven, no duty to return arises. In a file where it was established that the jewellery had been sold and spent when a house was bought, the Chamber held that the husband, on whom the burden of proof had shifted, could not prove his defence that the jewellery had been given to him on condition that it would not be returned by an abstract witness statement unsupported by the other evidence in the file (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2024/9098, K. 2025/5031; see the decision box below).

What the money was spent on does not change the outcome. In a file where the wife alleged that her jewellery had been sold to buy real estate in the husband’s name, and both the existence of the jewellery and its handing over to the husband were proven, the Court of Cassation held that the purpose for which the husband spent or sold the jewellery does not affect the return of the wife’s personal property. In the same decision, the Chamber also noted that the wife must prove the existence of the jewellery and that it was handed to the husband to be returned (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2025/2400, K. 2025/5136).

Return in Kind or Payment of Value? The Relief Sought and Enforcement

The Court of Cassation characterises a jewellery claim as an action for recovery of property; the claim takes the form of return in kind of the items in dispute or, failing that, payment of their value on the date of the action (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2021/5038, K. 2021/6749). The statement of claim therefore specifies the type, kind and quantity of the jewellery and requests return in kind first and, if that is impossible, its value; the judgment must likewise set out the type, characteristics, quantity and value of each item separately (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2022/6938, K. 2023/2835).

The court is bound by the relief sought (Code of Civil Procedure art. 26). Where the claimant sought return in kind together with the value on the date of actual payment, an award of the value on the date of the action was held to exceed the claim; noting that where return in kind is ordered and proves impossible the current value of the jewellery can be taken into account at the enforcement stage, the Chamber held that the court should have confined itself to ordering return in kind (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2025/8636, K. 2026/4113; see the decision box below). In another decision, the Chamber recalled that where return in kind is impossible, the procedure under Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law art. 24 applies (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2022/6938, K. 2023/2835):

Statutory Provision — Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law (Law No. 2004) art. 24 (Delivery of movables) (unofficial translation)

“When a judgment ordering the delivery of a movable is submitted to the enforcement office, the enforcement officer orders the debtor, by serving an enforcement order, to deliver the item within seven days. … If it is not in his possession, the value stated in the judgment is collected. If he does not pay, it is collected by way of attachment without the need to serve a further enforcement order. Where the value of the movable is not stated in the judgment or is disputed, it is assessed by the enforcement officer according to the market value on the date of attachment.”

Nature of the Claim, Competent Court and Court Fees

A jewellery dispute between spouses arises from the provisions on personal property and the recovery of property in the Second Book of the Civil Code, which is devoted to family law (Civil Code art. 220, Civil Code art. 226). The claim therefore falls within the jurisdiction of the family court under Law No. 4787 art. 4:

Statutory Provision — Law on the Establishment, Duties and Procedures of Family Courts (Law No. 4787) art. 4 (Duties of family courts) (unofficial translation)

“Family courts hear the following actions and matters: 1. Actions and matters arising from family law under the Second Book, excluding the Third Part, of the Turkish Civil Code No. 4721 of 22.11.2001 and under Law No. 4722 of 3.12.2001 on the Entry into Force and Application of the Turkish Civil Code, …”

A jewellery claim may be brought together with the divorce action, for example in a counterclaim; most of the decisions cited here concern “mutual divorce and jewellery claim” cases. However, the jewellery claim is not an ancillary of the divorce and is subject to a proportional court fee. In a file where the court had held that “there is no need to decide” on a wife’s counterclaim for return in kind or value of her jewellery because no fee had been paid, the Court of Cassation ruled that the court should have given a proper time limit to pay the advance portion of the proportional fee calculated on the value of the jewellery claimed and, once the deficiency was remedied, examined the merits (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2022/7872, K. 2023/5441).

Claims against third parties: If the jewellery was taken by members of the spouse’s family, the claim may also be directed against them. In a case brought on the allegation that members of the former husband’s family had taken the jewellery one week after the wedding to buy land, the Court of Cassation’s 3rd Civil Chamber emphasised that a claim for chattels is an action for recovery of property and reversed the dismissal on limitation grounds that had been rendered without the evidence being properly gathered (3rd Civil Chamber, E. 2022/2972, K. 2022/4456).

Limitation: How Long Can a Jewellery Claim Be Brought?

The Court of Cassation determines limitation in jewellery claims by reference to their nature as actions for recovery of property: if the items are found to exist, the dispute is based on ownership and limitation cannot arise; if their existence cannot be established, the claim concerns compensation, so the ten-year limitation period under Code of Obligations art. 146 applies and, in actions between spouses, Code of Obligations art. 153 must be taken into account (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2021/5038, K. 2021/6749; see the decision box below). Under Code of Obligations art. 146, unless the law provides otherwise, every claim is subject to a ten-year limitation period; Code of Obligations art. 153 provides that, for as long as the marriage continues, limitation does not begin to run, or is suspended, for claims of one spouse against the other:

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 153 (Suspension of limitation) (unofficial translation)

“Limitation does not begin to run, and if it has begun, it is suspended, in the following cases: … 3. For claims of one spouse against the other, for as long as the marriage continues. … Limitation begins to run, or resumes running if it had begun before suspension, at the end of the day on which the grounds for suspension cease.”

In a decision applying this principle, the divorce had become final on 26.06.2014 and the jewellery claim was brought on 30.03.2017; noting that the ten-year period had not expired on the date of the action, the Court of Cassation reversed, in the interest of the law, the dismissal on limitation grounds (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2021/5038, K. 2021/6749). The same criteria were restated by the 3rd Civil Chamber in a claim against members of the spouse’s family (3rd Civil Chamber, E. 2022/2972, K. 2022/4456). The lapse of time since the divorce therefore does not by itself mean that the jewellery claim is time-barred; whether the items still exist and when the period began to run must be assessed in the specific case.

Court of Cassation Decisions

The decisions below have been verified against their full texts in the official case-law database; they were rendered by the chambers competent under the division of work in force at the time.

Ownership of wedding gifts: the Chamber’s new principled view

Court of Cassation Decision
2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2026/136, K. 2026/6720, 16.06.2026 (the Chamber’s reversal reasoning; reversal as regards the dismissed jewellery claim)

“The Chamber’s previous case law held that “unless there is an agreement or a rule of custom to the contrary, whatever is given at the wedding, by whomever and to whichever spouse, whatever is attached (jewellery, gold, foreign currency, Turkish lira, etc.), all of it is deemed to belong to the woman”. … If there is an agreement between the parties on the sharing of the jewellery, the sharing is carried out in accordance with that agreement. Where there is no agreement between the parties on the sharing of the jewellery, if the existence of a local custom is alleged and proven, the sharing is carried out in accordance with that rule. Otherwise, everything of economic value attached to or given to the man and to the woman belongs, as a rule, to each of them. However, if there is among the items something specific to the opposite sex (specific to women or to men), it is deemed to have been given to that sex. … In jewellery claims, too, the ordinary situation is that jewellery specific to women is kept under the wife’s protection. A wife who alleges the contrary bears the burden of proving her claim.” (translation)

Burden of proof: the wife must prove the existence of the jewellery and its taking by the husband

Court of Cassation Decision
2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2024/10485, K. 2025/11756, 23.12.2025 (the Chamber’s reversal reasoning; reversal as regards the jewellery claim after a hearing)

“According to common experience, what is ordinary is that such items are on the woman or kept and preserved at home. In other words, it runs contrary to the ordinary course of things for them to have been left in the possession and custody of the husband. Moreover, such items are of a kind that can easily be hidden, carried and taken away. It is therefore always possible for a woman planning to leave home to take or hide them beforehand. The wife bears the burden of proving the existence of the jewellery in dispute and that it was taken from her by the husband. The proceedings and the evidence gathered show that the statements of the wife’s witnesses were based on what they had heard from the wife and that the wife could not prove her claim regarding the jewellery by witness testimony or any other evidence. Since the wife did not rely on the oath either, the court should have dismissed the unproven jewellery claim; rendering judgment as written was not correct and required reversal.” (translation)

The burden of proving the “given not to be claimed back” defence lies on the husband

Court of Cassation Decision
2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2024/9098, K. 2025/5031, 14.05.2025 (the Chamber’s reversal reasoning; reversal in favour of the wife as regards the dismissed jewellery claim)

“Jewellery attached at the wedding is deemed donated to the woman and becomes her personal property; unless it is alleged and proven that the jewellery was given on the understanding that it would not be claimed back, the person who took the gold is obliged to return it. As also accepted by the first-instance court, the proceedings and the evidence gathered establish that the jewellery was sold and spent by the husband when a house was bought and was never returned to the wife; the husband’s defence that the jewellery had been given to him on condition that it would not be returned, the burden of proving which had shifted to him, cannot be accepted as proven by an abstract witness statement unsupported by the other evidence in the file.” (translation)

Return in kind suffices; the current value is taken into account at enforcement

Court of Cassation Decision
2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2025/8636, K. 2026/4113, 14.04.2026 (the Chamber’s reversal reasoning; reversal in favour of the claimant wife)

“However, the judge is bound by the parties’ claims and cannot award anything other than or more than what is claimed. Moreover, there is no need to award separately the value of the jewellery on the date of actual payment, because where return in kind is ordered and return proves impossible, the current value of the jewellery can be taken into account at the enforcement stage, and the claimant has no legal interest in requesting a separate judgment to that effect … Accordingly, the court should have confined itself to ordering return in kind of the jewellery and dower claims it accepted; ordering, beyond the claim, that the value on the date of the action be collected if return in kind proves impossible was not correct and required reversal.” (translation)

Limitation: none if the items exist, otherwise the ten-year period applies

Court of Cassation Decision
2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2021/5038, K. 2021/6749, 04.10.2021 (the Chamber’s reasoning in a reversal in the interest of the law; the judgment was reversed without affecting its outcome)

“In this sense, a claim for chattels is an action for recovery of property, and the claim takes the form of return in kind of the items in dispute or, failing that, payment of their value on the date of the action. If it is established that the items in dispute exist, the dispute is based on the right of ownership and limitation cannot arise. If the existence of the items cannot be established, the claim concerns compensation, so the ten-year limitation period laid down in article 146 of the Turkish Code of Obligations must be applied and, in actions between husband and wife, article 153 of the Turkish Code of Obligations must be taken into account.” (translation)

Frequently Asked Questions

Who keeps the gold given at the wedding after a divorce?

Under the 2nd Civil Chamber’s new principled view, the court first looks at any agreement between the parties and, absent an agreement, at a local custom that has been alleged and proven. Failing both, everything of economic value attached to the man and to the woman belongs, as a rule, to each of them; an item specific to women or to men is deemed given to that sex. Items placed in the jewellery chest or bag that suit both sexes are treated as jointly owned.

How can I prove that my spouse took my gold?

As a rule, you bear the burden of proving that the jewellery existed and that your spouse took it. Wedding photographs and footage, witness statements and expert examination are assessed together, but statements of witnesses who only repeat what they heard from you are not considered sufficient. Relying on the oath in your statement of claim matters if the other evidence proves insufficient.

Can I claim back gold that was sold to buy a house or a car?

Yes. According to the Court of Cassation, unless it is alleged and proven that the jewellery was given on the understanding that it would not be claimed back, the person who took it must return it, and what it was spent on does not affect the return. If the husband argues that it was given to him on condition that it would not be returned, he must prove this; an abstract witness statement unsupported by other evidence has not been considered sufficient.

Can a jewellery claim be brought together with the divorce action?

Yes; in practice it is usually raised together with the divorce action or as a counterclaim and heard by the family court. However, because the jewellery claim is not an ancillary of the divorce, a proportional fee must be paid on the value of the jewellery claimed; if the fee is short, the court must give a proper time limit.

Can a jewellery claim be brought years after the divorce?

According to the Court of Cassation, if the jewellery is found to exist, the dispute is based on ownership and limitation does not arise; if it no longer exists, the ten-year limitation period applies, and limitation does not run between spouses while the marriage continues (Code of Obligations art. 146 and art. 153). How the period is calculated in a particular case must be assessed separately.

On what date is the value of the gold calculated?

This depends on the relief sought. The Court of Cassation states that where return in kind is ordered and proves impossible, the current value of the jewellery can be taken into account at the enforcement stage; under Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law art. 24, if the value is not stated in the judgment or is disputed, the enforcement officer assesses it at the market value on the date of attachment. The court cannot award the value on the date of the action if it was not claimed.
