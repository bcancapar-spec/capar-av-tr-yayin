Limitation vs Preclusive Periods in Turkish Law: General and Five-Year Periods, Suspension and Interruption

Short answer: Under Turkish law, limitation does not extinguish the claim; it gives the debtor a defence (def’i) that prevents the claim from being enforced through an action or enforcement proceedings, and the judge cannot take it into account of its own motion unless the debtor raises it (Code of Obligations art. 161). Limitation is suspended in the cases listed in Code of Obligations art. 153, and it is interrupted, with a new period starting to run, where the debtor acknowledges the debt or the creditor pursues the claim by action, defence or enforcement proceedings (Code of Obligations art. 154).

What Are Limitation and Preclusive Periods?

The legal order attaches two different consequences to rights that are not exercised within a certain period. Limitation does not extinguish the claim; it gives the debtor a defence (def’i) that prevents the claim from being enforced through an action or enforcement proceedings. A preclusive period, by contrast, extinguishes the right itself if it is not exercised in time.

According to the Court of Cassation’s Assembly of Civil Chambers, limitation in Turkish law has the nature of a defence; it does not extinguish the claim but removes the possibility of collecting it through legal channels (Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2022/1205, K. 2023/1188). Preclusive periods, on the other hand, completely terminate the right; once the period expires, the right ends automatically without the other party having to take any step (Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2017/949, K. 2020/621).

The clearest sign that limitation does not extinguish the claim is the payment of a time-barred debt: enrichment resulting from the performance of a time-barred debt cannot be reclaimed (Code of Obligations art. 78/2). A time-barred claim may also be set off, provided that it was not yet time-barred at the moment when set-off became possible (Code of Obligations art. 139/3).

Differences Between Limitation and Preclusive Periods

The differences between the two institutions directly affect the outcome of a case:

  • Raising and consideration of the court’s own motion: Limitation is a defence; unless the debtor raises it, the judge cannot take it into account of its own motion (Code of Obligations art. 161). A preclusive period has the nature of an objection; it may be raised at every stage of the proceedings, even after the judgment has been reversed, and is taken into account by the judge of its own motion (Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2017/949, K. 2020/621).
  • Suspension and interruption: The statutory grounds of suspension and interruption are specific to limitation (Code of Obligations art. 153, Code of Obligations art. 154). According to the Assembly of Civil Chambers, preclusive periods are not suspended or interrupted as limitation periods are (Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2019/855, K. 2020/1001).
  • Effect of expiry: When the limitation period expires, the claim does not disappear. When a preclusive period expires, the right is extinguished entirely; it can no longer be claimed, sued for or enforced (Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2017/949, K. 2020/621).
  • Will of the parties: The limitation periods laid down in the relevant section of the Code cannot be changed by contract (Code of Obligations art. 148), and limitation cannot be waived in advance (Code of Obligations art. 160). According to the Assembly of Civil Chambers, preclusive periods likewise cannot be changed or abolished by contract.
  • Sixty-day additional period: If an action or defence has been dismissed because the court lacked venue or subject-matter jurisdiction, because of a correctable error or because it was brought prematurely, and the limitation or preclusive period has expired in the meantime, the creditor may exercise its rights within an additional period of sixty days (Code of Obligations art. 158). This provision applies to both types of period.
Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 161 (Raising limitation) (unofficial translation)

“Unless limitation is raised, the judge cannot take it into account of its own motion.”

How Is the Nature of a Period Determined? Statutory and Court of Cassation Criteria

The differences above (whether the right itself or only the possibility of collection ends, consideration of the court’s own motion, suspension and interruption, the will of the parties) are used as the criteria of the distinction in decisions of the Assembly of Civil Chambers. Which group a specific period belongs to is determined first by the wording of the law and, where the law is silent, by court decisions.

Express wording of the law. The legislature often indicates the nature of a period itself. The Turkish Civil Code regulates the periods for actions for annulment of a will and for abatement under the heading “Preclusive periods” and provides that the right of action “lapses” on expiry (Civil Code art. 559, Civil Code art. 571), whereas for the legatee’s right of action the same Code uses the words “becomes time-barred” (Civil Code art. 602). The Code of Obligations likewise expressly provides for limitation in respect of claims in tort, claims arising from unjust enrichment and actions arising from defects in the thing sold (Code of Obligations art. 72, Code of Obligations art. 82, Code of Obligations art. 231).

Where the law does not characterise the period. The provision on defects of consent uses neither “limitation” nor “preclusive period”; it merely provides that a party who does not, within one year of discovering the mistake or fraud or of the end of the effect of duress, declare that it is not bound by the contract or reclaim what it has given is deemed to have ratified the contract (Code of Obligations art. 39). The Court of Cassation’s 1st Civil Chamber characterised this period as a preclusive period, holding that the exercise of the right of avoidance is not subject to any form and may be effected within the one-year period by a declaration of intent addressed to the other party, by way of defence or by an action. In the case at hand, an action brought by the deceased within one year, which was later deemed not to have been filed, was treated as notice that the deceased was not bound by the contract, and the dismissal judgment that had equated the fact of withdrawal from the contract with “bringing an action” was reversed (1st Civil Chamber, E. 2020/824, K. 2021/2024; rendered by majority).

Both periods may run together. A preclusive period and a limitation period may coexist in the same dispute. A creditor whose request for enforcement has been objected to may bring an action for annulment of the objection “within one year” of service of the objection (Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law art. 67), and the Assembly of Civil Chambers characterises this period as preclusive. In one decision the majority of the Assembly held that, although the action had been brought within that period, the claim being enforced had become time-barred because time had passed without any valid step capable of interrupting limitation being taken in the enforcement file; it did not adopt the view that limitation does not run during the preclusive period (Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2019/855, K. 2020/1001). A creditor who misses the period for the action for annulment of the objection retains the right to sue for its claim under the general provisions (Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law art. 67).

The Ten-Year General Limitation Period and the Five-Year Limitation Period (arts. 146-147)

Unless a special period is provided by law, a claim is subject to the ten-year general limitation period:

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 146 (Ten-year limitation period) (unofficial translation)

“Unless the law provides otherwise, every claim is subject to a ten-year limitation period.”

The Assembly of Civil Chambers applied the ten-year period to an action for a claim arising from the matrimonial property regime between spouses; in a case where the divorce had been granted by a foreign court, it upheld the judgment on resistance that took the date on which the foreign judgment became final as the starting point and dismissed the action as time-barred (Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2022/1205, K. 2023/1188).

Certain claims, such as periodic payments like rents, interest and wages and claims arising from mandate and contracts for work, are subject to the five-year limitation period:

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 147 (Five-year limitation period) (unofficial translation)

“A five-year limitation period applies to the following claims: 1. Rents, interest on capital and other periodic payments such as wages. 2. Accommodation charges at places such as hotels, motels, guesthouses and holiday villages, and charges for food and drink at restaurants and similar places. 3. Claims arising from small crafts and small-scale retail sales. 4. Claims arising from the partnership agreement in a partnership, between the partners themselves or between the partners and the partnership; and claims between the managers, representatives or auditors of a partnership and the partnership or the partners. 5. Claims arising from contracts of mandate, commission and agency and, except for commercial brokerage fees, from brokerage contracts. 6. Claims arising from a contract for work, except where the contractor has, through gross fault, failed to perform its obligations at all or properly.”

Commencement and computation. Limitation begins to run when the claim falls due; where the claim falls due upon notice, the period runs from the day on which notice could have been given (Code of Obligations art. 149). In computing the period, the day on which limitation begins is not counted, and limitation is completed only when the last day has also passed without the right being exercised (Code of Obligations art. 151). Once the principal claim is time-barred, the interest and other claims attached to it are also time-barred (Code of Obligations art. 152).

Limitation in Tort and the Extended Criminal Limitation Period (art. 72)

For claims for compensation in tort the law provides two periods and an exception: two years from the date on which the injured party learned of the damage and of the person liable, and in any event ten years from the date on which the act was committed; if the compensation arises from an act requiring punishment for which the criminal laws provide a longer limitation period, that longer period applies:

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 72 (Limitation in tort) (unofficial translation)

“A claim for compensation becomes time-barred on the expiry of two years from the date on which the injured party learned of the damage and of the person liable to compensate, and in any event ten years from the date on which the act was committed. However, if the compensation arises from an act requiring punishment for which the criminal laws provide a longer limitation period, that limitation period applies. Where an obligation has arisen for the injured party by reason of the tortious act, the injured party may always refuse to perform that obligation even if its claim for compensation arising from the tortious act has become time-barred.”

According to the Assembly of Civil Chambers, whether the act is one requiring punishment is, as a rule, determined by the civil judge; with respect to the special limitation provision for traffic accidents, the Assembly stated that the application of the longer criminal limitation period does not require criminal proceedings to have been brought against the perpetrator or a conviction, and that it is sufficient for the tortious act to have the character of an offence (Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2017/1103, K. 2019/423; see the case box below).

A recourse claim against persons jointly liable becomes time-barred on the expiry of two years from the date on which the compensation was paid in full and the jointly liable person became known, and in any event ten years from the date of full payment (Code of Obligations art. 73/1).

Limitation of Unjust Enrichment Claims (art. 82)

The claim arising from unjust enrichment is also subject to a two-tier period: two years from the date on which the entitled person learned of its right to restitution, and in any event ten years from the date on which the enrichment occurred:

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 82 (Limitation of unjust enrichment claims) (unofficial translation)

“The claim arising from unjust enrichment becomes time-barred on the expiry of two years from the date on which the entitled person learned of its right to restitution, and in any event ten years from the date on which the enrichment occurred. Where the enrichment consists in the enriched party acquiring a claim, the other party may always refuse to perform that obligation even if its own claim has become time-barred.”

The knowledge required to start the short period is full knowledge. According to the Assembly of Civil Chambers, limitation runs from the date on which the entitled person fully learned of the transaction that caused the decrease in its assets and of the person unjustly enriched; incomplete information and estimates before that cannot serve as the starting point. For public institutions, what matters is the date on which the person or body authorised to bring an action (the approving authority) learned of it (Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2021/251, K. 2022/1430; the case concerned a claim that arose under the former Code of Obligations No. 818).

Suspension of Limitation (art. 153)

Suspension means that limitation does not begin to run or, if it has begun, stops, and resumes once the cause of suspension ceases. The law lists the grounds of suspension as follows:

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 153 (Suspension of limitation) (unofficial translation)

“Limitation does not begin to run, and if it has begun it is suspended, in the following cases: 1. For claims of children against their parents, for the duration of parental custody. 2. For claims of persons under guardianship against the guardian or against the State by reason of acts of guardianship, for the duration of the guardianship. 3. For claims of one spouse against the other, for as long as the marriage continues. 4. For claims of domestic workers against those who employ them, for the duration of the service relationship. 5. For as long as the debtor holds a usufruct over the claim. 6. For as long as it is impossible to assert the claim before the Turkish courts. 7. Where the capacities of creditor and debtor have merged in the same person and the merger is later set aside with retroactive effect, for the period until this occurs. Limitation begins to run at the end of the day on which the causes of suspension cease, or continues the running that had begun before the suspension.”

According to the Assembly of Civil Chambers, the impossibility of asserting a claim before the Turkish courts covers objective obstacles valid for everyone, such as war, civil war, insurrection or natural disaster; the creditor’s personal obstacles such as illness, lack of knowledge or incapacity do not suspend limitation. For claims between spouses, limitation begins to run from the moment the end of the marriage has been established by a final judgment (Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2022/1205, K. 2023/1188).

Interruption of Limitation and the New Period (arts. 154-157)

On interruption, the time that has run up to that point is disregarded and a new period begins to run. The grounds of interruption are the debtor’s acknowledgment of the debt and the creditor’s pursuit of its right by action, defence or enforcement proceedings:

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 154 (Interruption of limitation) (unofficial translation)

“Limitation is interrupted in the following cases: 1. If the debtor has acknowledged the debt, in particular by paying interest or making part performance, or by giving a pledge or providing a surety. 2. If the creditor has applied to a court or an arbitrator by way of action or defence, has initiated enforcement proceedings or has lodged a claim in bankruptcy.”

  • New period: On interruption a new period begins to run; if the debt has been acknowledged in a written instrument or established by a court or arbitral decision, the new period is always ten years (Code of Obligations art. 156).
  • During proceedings and enforcement: Limitation interrupted by an action or defence starts to run again, during the proceedings, after every procedural step of the parties or every decision of the judge; limitation interrupted by enforcement proceedings starts to run again after every step relating to the enforcement of the claim (Code of Obligations art. 157).
  • Joint debtors and sureties: Interruption against one of several joint and several debtors or debtors of an indivisible obligation also operates against the others; interruption against the principal debtor also operates against the surety, but interruption against the surety does not operate against the principal debtor (Code of Obligations art. 155).

According to the Assembly of Civil Chambers, limitation is interrupted automatically by a request for enforcement (see the case box below), whereas a request for enforcement annulled by the enforcement court upon complaint does not interrupt limitation. A request filed with an enforcement office lacking venue also interrupts limitation; however, if, despite an objection to venue, transfer of the file to the competent enforcement office is not requested, the enforcement is deemed not to have been initiated and the interruption becomes void (Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2019/855, K. 2020/1001). Since in the same decision the claim was held time-barred because time had passed without any valid step being taken in the enforcement file, enforcement files should not be left inactive for long periods.

Periods Frequently Encountered in Practice

The periods below are taken from the statutory texts; links are given to related articles where a detailed discussion is needed.

  • Ten years: every claim for which the law does not provide otherwise (Code of Obligations art. 146); the new period running after interruption where the debt has been acknowledged in a written instrument or established by a court or arbitral decision (Code of Obligations art. 156).
  • Five years: periodic payments such as rents, interest on capital and wages; claims arising from contracts of mandate, commission and agency and, except in the case of the contractor’s gross fault, from contracts for work (Code of Obligations art. 147). Annual leave pay arising from an employment contract, and severance pay, compensation for termination without observing the notice requirement, bad-faith compensation and compensation for termination in breach of the principle of equal treatment (Labour Law (Law No. 4857) additional art. 3). See Overtime Pay: Conditions, Calculation and Proof for wage claims and Severance Pay: Conditions and Calculation for severance pay.
  • Two years / ten years: claims for compensation in tort (Code of Obligations art. 72; a longer criminal limitation period for acts constituting offences), recourse claims (Code of Obligations art. 73) and claims arising from unjust enrichment (Code of Obligations art. 82).
  • Two years (defects in sales): unless the seller has undertaken liability for a longer period, every action concerning liability for defects in the thing sold becomes time-barred two years after delivery of the thing to the buyer, even if the defect appears later; a seller guilty of gross fault in delivering the thing in a defective condition cannot rely on this period (Code of Obligations art. 231). For practice, see Liability for Defects in the Sale of a Second-Hand Vehicle.
  • One year (defects of consent): declaring that one is not bound by the contract or reclaiming what one has given in cases of mistake, fraud or duress (Code of Obligations art. 39); according to the Court of Cassation this is a preclusive period.
  • One year / five years (exploitation): the injured party may exercise its right within one year from the date on which it became aware of its lack of judgment or inexperience or, in the case of distress, from the date on which the distress ended, and in any event within five years from the conclusion of the contract (Code of Obligations art. 28).
  • Negotiable instruments: claims on a bill of exchange against the acceptor become time-barred three years from maturity; the holder’s claims against the endorsers and the drawer one year from the date of a protest drawn up in time (or, if the bill bears the clause “to be returned without expenses”, from maturity); and an endorser’s claims against other endorsers and the drawer six months (Commercial Code art. 749). For promissory notes, see Validity Requirements of a Promissory Note (Bono) under Turkish Law.
  • One year (annulment of objection): a creditor whose request for enforcement has been objected to may bring an action for annulment of the objection within one year of service of the objection (Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law art. 67); according to the Assembly of Civil Chambers this period is preclusive. See Action for Annulment of Objection.
  • Inheritance law: the right to bring an action for annulment of a will lapses one year after the claimant learned of the disposition, the ground of annulment and its own entitlement and, in any event, counted from the opening of the will or, for other dispositions, from the passing of the estate, ten years as against defendants in good faith and twenty years as against defendants not in good faith (Civil Code art. 559); the right to bring an action for abatement lapses one year after the heirs learned that their reserved shares had been infringed and in any event after ten years (Civil Code art. 571); the legatee’s right of action becomes time-barred after ten years (Civil Code art. 602). See Action for Annulment of a Will and Abatement (Tenkis) Actions.

Court of Cassation Decisions

The decisions below have been verified against their full texts in the official case-law database. The quotations are taken from the Assembly of Civil Chambers’ own reasoning; the outcome and the voting are indicated in the note.

A preclusive period is an objection: it may be raised at any stage and is taken into account by the judge of its own motion

Court of Cassation Decision
Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2017/949, K. 2020/621, 16.09.2020 (reasoning of the Assembly of Civil Chambers; concerns the one-year period in an action for annulment of an objection; the judgment on resistance was reversed unanimously)

“As a rule, preclusive periods are regulated by law. It is impossible for the parties to set, change or abolish preclusive periods by contract. Preclusive periods are periods that completely terminate, extinguish and forfeit the right. … Preclusive periods have the nature of an objection. The parties may raise the preclusive period at every stage of the proceedings, even after the judgment has been reversed. Moreover, the examination of preclusive periods is not left to the will of the parties. They must be taken into account, investigated and examined by the judge of its own motion…” (translation)

Preclusive periods are not suspended or interrupted; limitation is interrupted by a request for enforcement

Court of Cassation Decision
Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2019/855, K. 2020/1001, 08.12.2020 (reasoning of the Assembly of Civil Chambers; in an action for annulment of an objection concerning the enforcement of a motor insurer’s recourse claim, the judgment on resistance was reversed by majority on different grounds)

“A preclusive period and a limitation period are not the same concepts. In preclusive periods there is no such thing as the suspension or interruption of periods as there is in limitation. … In enforcement law, limitation is interrupted automatically by the request for enforcement made by the creditor to the enforcement office. For this, there is no need for a payment order to be served on the debtor. In enforcement proceedings duly initiated by the creditor, limitation is still interrupted even if the file has remained inactive because of an error of the enforcement officer.” (translation)

Limitation does not extinguish the claim; the impossibility of asserting it must be objective

Court of Cassation Decision
Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2022/1205, K. 2023/1188, 29.11.2023 (reasoning of the Assembly of Civil Chambers; concerns a matrimonial property claim between spouses divorced by a foreign court judgment; the judgment on resistance dismissing the action as time-barred was upheld by majority)

“In Turkish law it is accepted that limitation has the nature of a defence and that it does not extinguish the claim but removes the possibility of collecting it through legal channels. … In essence, the inability to bring an action under this provision covers the situation of ‘being unable to bring an action for objective reasons valid for everyone, not for reasons personal to the individual’. … On the other hand, where it is impossible to bring an action for reasons arising from the creditor in the person of the claimant, such as illness, lack of knowledge or incapacity, that provision cannot be applied and limitation continues to run for those claims…” (translation)

The extended criminal limitation period for torts constituting offences

Court of Cassation Decision
Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2017/1103, K. 2019/423, 09.04.2019 (reasoning of the Assembly of Civil Chambers; concerns compensation for loss of support in a single-vehicle traffic accident and was assessed together with the limitation provision specific to traffic accidents; the judgment on resistance was reversed by majority on different grounds)

“Some tortious acts give rise to liability not only in private law but also under criminal law; as long as the perpetrator of the tortious act, that is, the person responsible, may be subject to criminal prosecution, which generally has heavier consequences, it cannot be accepted that the injured party loses its rights. For this reason, if the tortious act also constitutes an offence under the criminal laws and the criminal laws or other laws containing criminal provisions have set a longer limitation period for that act, the action for compensation is also subject to the limitation period applicable to the criminal case. … It should be noted here that whether the act constitutes an act requiring punishment is, as a rule, to be determined by the civil judge.” (translation)

In unjust enrichment the period starts with full knowledge

Court of Cassation Decision
Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2021/251, K. 2022/1430, 02.11.2022 (reasoning of the Assembly of Civil Chambers; concerns the recovery of a payment mistakenly made by a public institution into another enforcement file; the judgment on resistance was reversed by majority on different grounds)

“Limitation, defined as a way of acquiring a right or being released from an obligation upon the expiry of a period under the conditions laid down by law … begins to run from the date on which the entitled person fully learned of the transaction that caused the decrease in its assets and of the person unjustly enriched. Incomplete information and estimates prior to that cannot serve as the starting point of this period. … As regards public institutions and organisations, the start of the limitation period in actions based on unjust enrichment is … the date on which the persons or bodies of those institutions and organisations authorised to bring an action (the approving authority) learned that they had the right to take back what they had given.” (translation)

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the basic difference between limitation and a preclusive period?

Limitation does not extinguish the claim; it gives the debtor a defence preventing the claim from being enforced by action or enforcement proceedings, and it cannot be taken into account by the judge unless raised (Code of Obligations art. 161). A preclusive period extinguishes the right itself, is taken into account by the judge of its own motion and, according to the Assembly of Civil Chambers of the Court of Cassation, is not subject to suspension or interruption.

Can a person who paid a time-barred debt claim the money back?

No. Enrichment resulting from the performance of a time-barred debt cannot be reclaimed (Code of Obligations art. 78/2). Limitation does not extinguish the debt; it removes the possibility of collecting the claim through legal channels.

In which cases is limitation interrupted, and what happens then?

Limitation is interrupted if the debtor acknowledges the debt (in particular by paying interest, making part performance, giving a pledge or providing a surety) or if the creditor applies to a court or an arbitrator by way of action or defence, initiates enforcement proceedings or lodges a claim in bankruptcy (Code of Obligations art. 154). On interruption a new period begins to run; if the debt has been acknowledged in a written instrument or established by a court or arbitral decision, the new period is always ten years (Code of Obligations art. 156).

When does limitation start in tort, and when does the criminal limitation period apply?

A claim for compensation becomes time-barred two years after the injured party learned of the damage and of the person liable and in any event ten years after the act was committed; if the compensation arises from an act requiring punishment for which the criminal laws provide a longer limitation period, that period applies (Code of Obligations art. 72). According to the Assembly of Civil Chambers, whether the act requires punishment is, as a rule, determined by the civil judge.

Can the limitation period be extended or shortened by contract?

The periods laid down in the limitation section of the Turkish Code of Obligations cannot be changed by contract (Code of Obligations art. 148), and limitation cannot be waived in advance (Code of Obligations art. 160). The Assembly of Civil Chambers likewise considers it impossible for the parties to change or abolish preclusive periods by contract.
