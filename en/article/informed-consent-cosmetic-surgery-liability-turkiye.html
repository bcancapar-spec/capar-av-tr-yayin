Lack of Informed Consent in Cosmetic Surgery in Türkiye: Surgeon's Liability, Proof, Limitation (TBK Art. 147)

Short answer: Under Turkish law, before an intervention the physician must inform the patient, including about probable complications, other treatment options and the consequences of refusal (Patient Rights Regulation art. 15); for major surgical operations the law requires consent to be in writing (Law No. 1219 art. 70). According to a 2026 decision of the Court of Cassation’s 3rd Civil Chamber, the burden of proving that the patient was properly informed lies with the physician or hospital; however, the same Chamber treated a lack of consent raised later, without having been pleaded in the statement of claim, as an extension of the claim. Surgery performed for an aesthetic purpose (to achieve a better appearance) has been classified as a contract for work in decisions of the Court of Cassation Assembly of Civil Chambers and 6th Civil Chamber; claims arising from a contract for work are, as a rule, time-barred after five years, the contractor’s gross fault being excluded from that period (TBK art. 147); for defect-based claims, special two-year periods may also apply.

This article explains Turkish law. It is an English translation of the Turkish original; in case of any discrepancy, the Turkish text prevails.

In brief

Under Turkish law, a patient must be informed of the risks, complications and alternatives before a medical intervention; the Court of Cassation's 3rd Civil Chamber places the burden of proving this on the physician or hospital. The article explains the scope of disclosure, the consent form, the classification of cosmetic surgery as a contract for work, limitation periods and when a lack of consent must be raised in the action.

The claims under Turkish law that a patient in Türkiye who faces an unexpected outcome after cosmetic or therapeutic surgery may bring against the physician and a private hospital on the basis of a lack of informed consent: the scope of disclosure, the consent form, the burden of proof, the classification of cosmetic surgery as a contract for work, limitation periods and what to watch for in the statement of claim.

What Is Informed Consent? Its Basis in Statute and Regulation

Surgery, an injection or any other medical intervention is an interference with a person’s bodily integrity. One of the elements that makes it lawful is the patient’s consent. For consent to be valid, the patient must know what they are consenting to; hence the term “informed consent”.

The basic statutory provision is article 70 of Law No. 1219. It requires the physician to obtain in advance the consent of the patient (or of the parent or guardian if the patient is a minor or under guardianship) for every kind of procedure, and requires this consent to be in writing for major surgical operations. Sentences added by Law No. 7557 of 21.07.2025 now allow consent to be obtained electronically as well.

Statutory Provision — Law on the Practice of Medicine and Its Branches (Law No. 1219) art. 70 (Consent)

Physicians, dentists and dental practitioners shall first obtain the consent of the patient, or of the parent or guardian if the patient is a minor or under guardianship, for every kind of procedure they will perform. For major surgical operations this consent must be in writing. … Consent to be obtained under this paragraph may also be obtained electronically. … (Consent is not required where there is no parent or guardian or they are not present, or where the person to be operated on is unable to express themselves.) …

The text that regulates the content of disclosure and the limits of consent in detail is the Patient Rights Regulation. It is not a statute but a regulation of the Ministry of Health; the articles quoted below took their present form through the amendment of 08.05.2014. Under the Regulation, it is essential that the patient be informed about the subject and consequences of the intervention when consent is obtained, and the intervention must remain within the limits of the consent given.

Regulatory Provision — Patient Rights Regulation art. 31 (Scope of consent and cases where it is not required)

When consent is obtained, it is essential that the patient or legal representative be informed and enlightened about the subject and consequences of the medical intervention.

The consent given by the patient also covers routine procedures that are a continuation of the process required by the medical intervention and may be regarded as necessary.

The medical intervention must remain within the limits of the consent given by the patient.

Where, during a medical intervention, a need arises to extend the procedure and, if it is not extended, there is a medical necessity that may lead to the loss of an organ of the patient or to its inability to function, the intervention may be extended without seeking consent.

The exception in the last paragraph is narrow: consent is dispensed with only in a medical necessity threatening the loss of an organ or its function.

Scope of Disclosure and the Consent Form

Saying “you will have surgery, there are risks” is not disclosure. Article 15 of the Regulation lists what must be explained to the patient: the probable causes and course of the illness, who will perform the intervention, where and how, other diagnostic and treatment options, probable complications, the consequences of refusal, the medicines to be used and how to obtain help afterwards.

Regulatory Provision — Patient Rights Regulation art. 15 (Scope of information)

The patient shall be informed of: a) the probable causes of the illness and how it will progress, b) by whom, where, in what manner and how the medical intervention will be performed and its estimated duration, c) other diagnostic and treatment options, the benefits and risks of those options and their probable effects on the patient’s health, ç) probable complications, d) the probable benefits and risks that may arise in case of refusal, e) the important characteristics of the medicines to be used, f) lifestyle recommendations critical to health, g) where necessary, how medical help on the same matter can be reached.

In cosmetic surgery without medical necessity, not having the operation is a real option. This article’s inference is as follows: in such an intervention, a failure to explain complications and possible permanent scarring carries weight in a dispute, because it may directly affect the patient’s decision.

Article 26 of the Regulation requires that, for interventions medically likely to give rise to a dispute, a consent form containing the information in article 15 be prepared, that this information also be conveyed to the patient orally, and that the form be signed both by the patient and by the health professional who provides the information.

Regulatory Provision — Patient Rights Regulation art. 26 (Consent form)

For situations provided for in the legislation and for medical interventions that are medically likely to give rise to a dispute, the health institution prepares a consent form containing the information in article 15. The information in the consent form is conveyed to the patient orally and the consent form is signed by the patient or legal representative. … The health professional concerned is responsible for the accuracy of the information given.

Under this provision a form alone is not enough; the information must also be conveyed orally. The content of the form also matters: a general text that does not contain the complications specific to the intervention may not carry the information in article 15. Adequacy is assessed separately in each case.

Cosmetic Intervention: Contract for Work or Mandate?

The relationship between the patient and the physician or private hospital is usually a contract. According to the Assembly of Civil Chambers, in private hospitals there is, as a rule, a contract (a hospital admission contract) between the hospital and the patient rather than between the physician and the patient, and the rules on mandate apply to medical treatment, the principal performance under that contract. Under a mandate the physician owes careful effort, not a particular result.

The position is different for cosmetic intervention. In its decision of 02.11.2022, the Court of Cassation Assembly of Civil Chambers classified an agreement to have silicone implants placed in the hips as a contract for work, stating that providing an attractive appearance in line with the agreed purpose and bringing the process to a healthy conclusion is the subject of the contract. The 6th Civil Chamber, in its decision of 25.06.2025, classified cosmetic nose surgery in the same way, stating that disclosure and the management of complications are also the responsibility of the contractor (in that case, the hospital where the operation was performed). Under a contract for work, a result that does not meet the employer’s expectation may be regarded as defective, which opens the way for the patient to rely on the rules on defects.

However, not every nose or breast operation is cosmetic. In a regional court of appeal decision upheld by the Court of Cassation’s 3rd Civil Chamber on 21.05.2026, nose surgery performed because of breathing difficulty was treated as therapeutic and the relationship was assessed as a mandate. In its own reasoning the Chamber likewise described the dispute as a claim for breach of the duty of care under a contract of mandate, without discussing the classification further. In interventions with both therapeutic and aesthetic purposes, how the relationship is classified depends on the features of the case. Classification matters because it determines what the physician owes and the limitation period described below.

Burden of Proof: Who Proves Disclosure?

As a rule, everyone must prove their own allegation. In its 2021 decision, the Assembly of Civil Chambers stressed that in contractual liability the burden of proof as to fault is reversed against the debtor, but that the burden of proving elements such as the breach of contract, the damage and adequate causation remains with the creditor (the patient).

As regards disclosure, the Court of Cassation’s 3rd Civil Chamber stated clearly in its decision of 09.03.2026 that the burden of proof in informed consent lies with the doctor or the hospital. In that case the first-instance court had dismissed the action on, among other grounds, the ground that the duty to keep the consent form lay with a hospital not party to the action. The Chamber quashed the decision, stating that the burden of proving that the patient was properly informed lay both with the defendant doctors and with the non-party hospital, and that the court should have given the doctors an opportunity to prove this. The decision shows that proving disclosure is the task of the healthcare side, not of the patient; however, it is a reversal and the outcome of the case depends on the retrial.

Limitation, Competent Court and the Statement of Claim

The limitation period varies with the legal relationship on which the patient bases the claim. Under the Turkish Code of Obligations the general period is ten years; unless the law provides otherwise, every claim is subject to it.

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 146 (Ten-year limitation)

Unless the law provides otherwise, every claim is subject to a ten-year limitation period.

For claims arising from contracts of mandate and contracts for work, a five-year period is provided. The five-year period for contracts for work does not cover the case where the contractor fails to perform its obligations at all or properly through gross fault; according to this article’s inference, the general ten-year period then comes into play. If the claim is based on the work being defective, the special period in TBK art. 478 may also come into play: two years from delivery for works other than immovable structures, and twenty years where the contractor is grossly at fault. In a consumer relationship, liability for a defective service is likewise, as a rule, subject to a two-year limitation period from performance of the service, which does not apply if the defect was concealed through gross fault or fraud (Law No. 6502 art. 16). The decisions cited did not discuss these periods.

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 147 (Five-year limitation; items 5 and 6)

A five-year limitation period applies to the following claims:

… Claims arising from contracts of mandate, commission and agency, and from brokerage contracts other than claims for commercial brokerage fees.

… Claims arising from a contract for work, except where the contractor fails to perform its obligations at all or properly through gross fault.

Where the patient has no direct contract with the physician (for example, a physician working at the hospital who made no separate agreement with the patient), the claim may be based on tort. In that case a two-year period runs from learning of the damage and the person liable, and in any case a ten-year period from the act; if the act constitutes an offence subject to a longer criminal limitation period, that period applies.

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 72/1 (Limitation in tort)

A claim for damages is time-barred two years from the date on which the injured party learns of the damage and of the person liable, and in any case ten years from the date of the act. However, if the claim arises from an act requiring a penalty for which criminal laws provide a longer limitation period, that period applies.

Limitation is not taken into account by the court of its own motion; the defendant must raise it (TBK art. 161). In its decision of 12.01.2026, the Court of Cassation’s 6th Civil Chamber stated that, in an action brought jointly against the physician and the hospital, if only the physician raised the limitation defence in time, the hospital cannot benefit from it. In the same case the first-instance court had applied the five-year period in TBK art. 147/6 to the claim for pecuniary damages arising from cosmetic nose surgery; the Court of Cassation did not discuss that calculation separately.

Competent court. In the Chamber decisions cited in this article, the actions were heard by consumer courts or by a civil court of first instance acting as a consumer court; in one of them the process began with an application to a consumer arbitration committee. The two Assembly of Civil Chambers cases, filed in 2010 and 2011, were heard by civil courts of first instance. The route is different for interventions carried out in public hospitals; that subject is explained separately in the related article.

Statement of claim. A lack of consent should be pleaded expressly at the outset of the action. In its decision of 21.05.2026, the Court of Cassation’s 3rd Civil Chamber treated a lack of consent raised on appeal, without having been pleaded in the statement of claim, as an extension of the claim. In its 2021 decision, by contrast, the majority of the Assembly of Civil Chambers did not adopt the view that a lack of consent cannot be taken into account of the court’s own motion because it had not been pleaded as a fact; there, however, the Assembly gave no separate reasoning on this point and the issue had entered the proceedings through the Chamber’s reversal. In the 3rd Civil Chamber case, by contrast, the lower courts had found on the basis of the consent forms that disclosure had been given; only the new objection to the content of the form was not examined. This article’s inference: the safest course is to state concretely in the statement of claim which information was not given.

Informed Consent and Cosmetic Surgery in Court Decisions

Scope of disclosure. In a case where a painkiller injection given after haemorrhoid surgery at a private hospital caused permanent damage to the leg, the Assembly of Civil Chambers found the reports obtained inadequate and quashed the lower court’s decision to persist. The Assembly stated that the injection fell within the scope of the surgical intervention and that whether the patient was informed about the possible risks must also be considered. The decision was given by majority, with rectification of the decision left open.

Court of Cassation Assembly of Civil Chambers Decision
Court of Cassation, Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2018/849, K. 2021/1385, 11.11.2021 (the Assembly’s reasoning; the decision to persist was quashed by majority)

“It should be noted here that the physician’s duty of disclosure denotes an obligation that includes informed consent but is broader than it. In other words, the scope of the duty of disclosure covers not only informed consent but also the physician informing the patient about what needs to be done after the treatment applied. … in addition, since the injection given in the concrete case fell within the scope of the surgical intervention performed on the claimant, the court must also take into account whether the claimant was informed about the possible risks.”

The burden of proof is on the physician. In the case of a patient who suffered a perforation during surgery for vaginal agenesis, the Court of Cassation’s 3rd Civil Chamber held that the consent form being kept at another hospital does not shift the burden of proof to the patient.

Court of Cassation Decision
3rd Civil Chamber, E. 2025/531, K. 2026/1285, 09.03.2026 (the Chamber’s reasoning for reversal)

“Under article 70 of Law No. 1219 on the Practice of Medicine and Its Branches and article 26 of the Rules of Medical Professional Ethics, the burden of proof in informed consent lies with the doctor or the hospital. … whereas the defendant doctors should have been given an opportunity to prove their duty of disclosure and a decision given according to the result, the court’s deciding as written without assessing this matter is contrary to procedure and law and requires reversal.”

Lack of consent must be raised in time. In the case of a patient left with a permanent scar after two nose operations for breathing difficulty, the Court of Cassation’s 3rd Civil Chamber upheld the regional court decision based on the expert report finding that the outcome was a complication. The objection that the consent form contained incomplete information was not examined because it had not been raised in the statement of claim.

Court of Cassation Decision
3rd Civil Chamber, E. 2025/6073, K. 2026/3293, 21.05.2026 (the Chamber’s reasoning for upholding)

“as regards the appeal grounds raised concerning informed consent; since it is understood that this matter, not raised by the claimant in the statement of claim, being raised later falls within the scope of an extension of the claim, it was decided to dismiss the appeal objections of the claimant’s counsel and to uphold the decision, which was found to be in accordance with procedure and law.”

Cosmetic surgery is a contract for work. In the case of a patient who had silicone implants placed in her hips and had to undergo a second operation because of an infection that developed after surgery, the Assembly of Civil Chambers, although the expert reports stated that the complication did not result from the fault of the physician and hospital, accepted that the defendants had not properly performed their obligations under the contract for work and quashed the decision to persist on the ground that a higher award of non-pecuniary damages was required. The decision was given by majority, with rectification of the decision left open.

Court of Cassation Assembly of Civil Chambers Decision
Court of Cassation, Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2020/284, K. 2022/1423, 02.11.2022 (the Assembly’s reasoning; the decision to persist was quashed by majority)

“providing an attractive appearance in line with the desired and agreed purpose through aesthetic surgery and bringing the process to a healthy conclusion constitutes the subject of the contract for work. … As for complications, the duty of disclosure and the proper management of complications are likewise the contractor’s responsibility.”

The same classification appears in a more recent decision of the 6th Civil Chamber. A consumer arbitration committee decision obtained by a patient who underwent two further operations after cosmetic nose surgery had been annulled by the consumer court in an action brought by the hospital. On an appeal in the interest of the law, the Chamber quashed the decision without affecting the outcome of the case, stating that the later operations showed the undertaken result had not been achieved and that the expert panel should include a physician from the field of plastic, reconstructive and aesthetic surgery.

Court of Cassation Decision
6th Civil Chamber, E. 2025/1714, K. 2025/2538, 25.06.2025 (reasoning for reversal in the interest of the law; without affecting the outcome)

“Since the claimant applied to the defendant for an aesthetic purpose, it is clear that providing an attractive appearance in line with the desired and agreed purpose through aesthetic surgery and bringing the process to a healthy conclusion are the subject of the contract for work between the parties. Here the contractor is under an obligation to produce the work in the interest of the employer and without causing any harm to them. As for complications, the duty of disclosure and the proper management of complications are likewise the contractor’s responsibility.”

The limitation defence is personal. In a cosmetic nose surgery case, non-pecuniary damages had been awarded and the joined action for pecuniary damages dismissed as time-barred. The Chamber upheld the main action and, because only the physician had raised limitation, quashed the decision on the joined action as regards the hospital.

Court of Cassation Decision
6th Civil Chamber, E. 2025/674, K. 2026/108, 12.01.2026 (the Chamber’s reasoning for reversal as to the joined action)

“In this context, since only the party raising it can benefit from the limitation defence, a personal right that gives the debtor-defendant the possibility of avoiding performance of a debt, an optional co-defendant who did not raise it cannot benefit from it.”

Practical Points

  • Collect documents early. Your copy of the consent form, the discharge summary, the operation note, photographs and payment records are the foundation of the case. The Regulation gives the patient the right to inspect their records and obtain a copy (art. 16).
  • Check the content of the form. Are the complication that occurred, the alternatives and the consequences of not having surgery written in the form?
  • Plead the lack of consent in the statement of claim. State concretely which information was not given; an allegation raised later may be treated as an extension of the claim.
  • Join all those responsible. The physician and the hospital may be liable separately; defences such as limitation have separate effects for each defendant.
  • Do not miss the time limit. The five-year period may apply to contractual claims and two-year periods to defect-based and tort-based claims; where it is disputed which applies, acting on the basis of the shortest period is the safe course.

Frequently Asked Questions

Under Turkish law, who must prove that the patient was properly informed when informed consent is disputed?

In its decision of 09.03.2026, the Court of Cassation’s 3rd Civil Chamber, relying on Law No. 1219 art. 70 and article 26 of the Rules of Medical Professional Ethics, stated that the burden of proof for informed consent lies with the doctor or the hospital, and quashed a dismissal given without allowing the defendant doctors to prove it. The patient, as a rule, must still prove the breach of contract, the damage and the causal link; the Assembly of Civil Chambers stressed that the presumption of fault concerns fault only.

Under Turkish law, is a consent form signed by the patient sufficient on its own for informed consent?

As a rule, no. Under art. 26 of the Patient Rights Regulation, the consent form must contain the information listed in art. 15 (such as complications, alternatives and the consequences of refusal), and that information must also be conveyed to the patient orally. Whether the form contains this information and whether it was actually explained is assessed in each case; the existence of a signed form does not by itself end the dispute over disclosure (this is the article’s own inference).

Is cosmetic surgery treated as a contract for work or as a contract of mandate in Turkish Court of Cassation decisions?

Surgery performed for an aesthetic purpose (to achieve a better appearance) was classified as a contract for work in the decisions of the Court of Cassation Assembly of Civil Chambers of 02.11.2022 and of the 6th Civil Chamber of 25.06.2025; the contractor (physician or hospital) was held bound to achieve the agreed result, and disclosure and management of complications were also treated as the contractor’s responsibility. Where the operation is for treatment (for example nose surgery for a breathing problem), the relationship was treated as a mandate both in a regional court decision and in the reasoning of the 3rd Civil Chamber upholding it.

What is the limitation period for a damages claim arising from cosmetic surgery in Türkiye?

Claims arising from a contract for work are, as a rule, subject to a five-year limitation period; however, the case where the contractor fails to perform its obligations at all or properly through gross fault is excluded from that five-year period (TBK art. 147). Unless the law provides otherwise, the general period is ten years (TBK art. 146). If the claim is based on defective performance, the two-year periods in TBK art. 478 and Law No. 6502 art. 16 may also apply. Tort-based claims are subject to two- and ten-year periods (TBK art. 72). Which period applies depends on the nature of the relationship and the defendant; limitation is considered only if the defendant raises it.

Under Turkish law, can a lack of informed consent be raised for the first time on appeal in a damages action?

It is not safe to do so. In its decision of 21.05.2026, the Court of Cassation’s 3rd Civil Chamber treated a lack of consent raised later, without having been pleaded in the statement of claim, as an extension of the claim and dismissed the appeal ground. Although the Assembly of Civil Chambers, in a 2021 decision concerning a different procedural situation, required disclosure to be taken into account, the safest course is to plead the lack of consent expressly in the statement of claim.
