Fair Trial and Press Freedom for Journalists in Türkiye: Detention, Sources, Access Bans, AYM and ECtHR

Short answer: A journalist’s news report, commentary or interview cannot by itself be treated as evidence of a crime; detention may be ordered only on concrete evidence of strong suspicion and after a proportionality review. The Constitutional Court and the European Court of Human Rights have found that detentions resting solely on published articles violate both the right to liberty and press freedom, and the Court of Cassation has rejected collective punishment for press offences. A journalist cannot be compelled to disclose a source, and reporting on an investigation within the limits of news reporting is not a crime.

This article explains Turkish law. It is an English translation of the Turkish original; in case of any discrepancy, the Turkish text prevails.

Legal Framework: the Constitution, the Convention and Press Legislation

In a case against a journalist, freedom of expression and of the press and the right to a fair trial are intertwined. Because a measure such as custody, detention, a search or an access ban is at the same time an interference with press freedom, the reasons for a detention order, for example, are reviewed separately under the right to liberty and under press freedom.

The Constitution

The general standard for restricting fundamental rights is Constitution art. 13: restrictions must be imposed by law, for the reasons set out in the Constitution, without impairing the essence of the right and in line with proportionality. Constitution art. 26 protects freedom of expression, including the freedom to receive and impart news and ideas without interference by official authorities, while the special position of the press is regulated in the provision below. The right to periodical and non-periodical publication and the protection of printing facilities are found in Constitution arts. 29-30: a lawfully established printing house and its press equipment may not be seized, confiscated or barred from operation on the ground that they are “instruments of crime”.

Statutory Provision — Constitution of the Republic of Türkiye (Law No. 2709) art. 28 (Freedom of the press) (unofficial translation)

“The press is free, and shall not be censored. The establishment of a printing house shall not be subject to prior permission or the deposit of a financial guarantee. … The State shall take the necessary measures to ensure freedom of the press and information. In the limitation of freedom of the press, the provisions of Articles 26 and 27 of the Constitution shall apply. … No ban shall be placed on the reporting of events, except by the decision of a judge issued within the limits to be specified by law, to ensure proper functioning of the judiciary.”

As regards procedural guarantees, Constitution art. 19 (“strong indications” required for detention, trial within a reasonable time, right to request release), Constitution art. 36 (right to a fair trial) and Constitution art. 38 (legality of crimes and penalties, presumption of innocence, exclusion of unlawfully obtained evidence) apply.

The Convention, the ICCPR and the primacy of human rights treaties

Article Five (liberty), Article Six (fair trial) and Article Ten (freedom of expression) of the European Convention on Human Rights are the core standards in cases involving journalists, while Article Eighteen prohibits restrictions on rights for purposes not prescribed by the Convention. Similar guarantees appear in the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights. Under Constitution art. 90/5, international treaties on fundamental rights prevail in the event of conflict with statutes; the case-law of the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) therefore serves as a direct standard in interpreting criminal provisions.

Press Law No. 5187

Since the amendment made by Law No. 7418 of 13.10.2022, the Press Law covers not only printed works but also news websites (Press Law No. 5187 arts. 1-2). A news website is a periodical publication set up and operated to present written, visual or audio content in the nature of news or commentary at regular intervals. The most important consequence is that the short time limits for prosecution explained below now also apply to news websites.

Statutory Provision — Press Law (Law No. 5187) art. 3 (Freedom of the press) (unofficial translation)

“The press is free. This freedom includes the rights to obtain and impart information, to criticise, to interpret and to create works. The exercise of press freedom may be restricted only in accordance with the requirements of a democratic society, for the purpose of protecting the reputation and rights of others, public health and morals, national security, public order, public safety and territorial integrity, preventing the disclosure of State secrets or the commission of crimes, and ensuring the authority and impartiality of the judiciary.”

An offence committed through the press is completed at the moment of publication; in periodicals the author is liable as a rule, and the responsible editor and the persons to whom he or she reports are liable only in the cases listed in the Law, such as where the author is unknown or abroad (Press Law No. 5187 art. 11). A correction and reply must be sent within two months of publication and must be published within three days in daily periodicals and within one day, with a URL link, on news websites; otherwise the criminal judgeship of peace may be applied to (Press Law No. 5187 art. 14).

The same amendment placed press cards on a statutory footing: the card is an official identity document issued upon application to the Directorate of Communications; the eligibility conditions include not having been convicted of terrorist offences, and the Press Card Commission may issue a warning or cancel the card for breaches of the principles of press ethics (Press Law No. 5187 additional arts. 1-6). Details are set out in the Press Card Regulation published in the Official Gazette of 10.04.2023. The Law does not make journalistic activity or the publication of a periodical conditional on holding a press card.

Internet and broadcasting legislation

The main statute for internet content is Law No. 5651. In its current text, the “President” and the “Presidency” are defined, following Law No. 7590 of 24.07.2026, as the President and the Presidency of Cyber Security (Law No. 5651 art. 2), so that the administrative access-blocking powers under the Law have passed to that body. In radio and television, freedom of expression and information remains the rule even in times of crisis; however, where national security clearly so requires or a serious disturbance of public order is highly likely, the President of the Republic or a vice-president or minister designated by the President may impose a temporary broadcasting ban, against which an action for annulment lies directly before the Council of State (Law No. 6112 art. 7).

Scope and Limits of Press Freedom

Press freedom protects the right to impart information and ideas on matters of public concern; in the case-law of the ECtHR and the Constitutional Court the press acts as a public “watchdog”. The intensity of protection is proportionate to the expression’s contribution to public debate: reporting and criticism concerning politicians, public officials and the functioning of the judiciary enjoy the widest protection, whereas protection narrows for private-life stories that serve no public interest.

Facts and value judgments: factual statements are susceptible of proof, value judgments are not, but a value judgment must still have a sufficient factual basis. Good faith and diligence: according to the ECtHR, the protection afforded to journalists is subject to the proviso that they act in good faith and on an accurate factual basis and provide “reliable and precise” information in accordance with the ethics of journalism (Bédat v. Switzerland [GC], no. 56925/08, 29.03.2016, § 58). Criteria for the right to report: in actions for infringement of personality rights by the press, the Civil General Assembly of the Court of Cassation looks at whether the report is true, whether there is a public interest and public concern, whether the subject is current, and whether a balance between substance and form is maintained; if a report breaches any of these rules, the balance between public interest and personality rights is upset. The General Assembly upheld the finding that verbatim quotation from another newspaper did not by itself remove liability (Civil General Assembly, E. 2017/1361, K. 2017/1447, 29.11.2017).

The chilling effect is the idea that heavy sanctions against journalists may push the whole press towards self-censorship; the severity of the sanction is therefore part of the proportionality assessment, and according to the ECtHR the fact of a conviction may sometimes matter more than the minor nature of the penalty (same judgment, § 79). Prior restraints (publication bans, seizure, access bans) are specifically limited by the Constitution: a ban on reporting events may be imposed only by a judge, within limits set by law, to ensure the proper functioning of the judiciary.

Offences Most Frequently Charged Against Journalists

Terrorist propaganda and organisation offences

The charge most frequently brought against journalists is the propaganda offence under the Anti-Terror Law. A 2013 amendment added the requirement that violent methods be legitimised, praised or encouraged, and Law No. 7188 of 17.10.2019 added an express sentence on news reporting and criticism.

Statutory Provision — Anti-Terror Law (Law No. 3713) art. 7/2 (Terrorist propaganda) (unofficial translation)

“A person who makes propaganda for a terrorist organisation in a manner that legitimises or praises its methods involving force, violence or threats, or encourages recourse to such methods, shall be punished by imprisonment of one to five years. If this offence is committed through the press and broadcasting, the penalty shall be increased by one half. In addition, a judicial fine of one thousand to five thousand days shall be imposed on the publication managers of press and broadcasting outlets who did not participate in the offence. … Expressions of opinion that do not exceed the limits of news reporting or that are made for the purpose of criticism shall not constitute an offence.”

Printing or publishing statements or declarations of terrorist organisations that legitimise, praise or encourage violent methods is a separate offence (Law No. 3713 art. 6/2); this provision becomes decisive for interviews with members of such organisations. Charges of membership (Law No. 5237 art. 314) and of knowingly and wilfully aiding an organisation without being part of its hierarchy (Law No. 5237 art. 220/7) are usually built on a newspaper’s editorial line, contacts with sources or social-media posts. The Court of Cassation’s criteria on these charges are set out in the decisions section below.

Publicly disseminating misleading information (Penal Code art. 217/A)

The provision added by Law No. 7418 of 13.10.2022, commonly called the “disinformation offence”, is among the most debated rules for news content.

Statutory Provision — Turkish Penal Code (Law No. 5237) art. 217/A (Publicly disseminating information misleading the public) (unofficial translation)

“(1) A person who, solely with the motive of creating anxiety, fear or panic among the public, publicly disseminates untrue information concerning the internal and external security, public order and public health of the country in a manner capable of disturbing public peace shall be punished by imprisonment of one to three years. (2) If the offender commits the offence by concealing his or her real identity or within the framework of the activities of an organisation, the penalty imposed under the first paragraph shall be increased by one half.”

The Constitutional Court dismissed the request for annulment by a majority, with six dissenting members (Constitutional Court, E.2022/129, K.2023/189, 08.11.2023; Official Gazette 23.02.2024). The reasoning contains important limits for the defence: the capacity of the information to disturb public peace is a material element that must be established with evidence and facts, and the offence is committed only where information known by the offender to be untrue is disseminated solely with the motive of creating anxiety, fear or panic. For a journalist who relays a disputed claim and names the source, knowledge and the specific motive must therefore be examined separately.

Secrecy, influencing the judiciary and State secrets

Breach of investigative secrecy is discussed separately below. Making public oral or written statements in order unlawfully to influence a judge, expert or witness so as to obtain an unlawful decision or act, or a false statement, in pending proceedings is the offence of “attempting to influence a fair trial”, punishable by a judicial fine (Law No. 5237 art. 288); since this specific aim is required, reporting on or criticising a case does not in itself fall within its scope. Disclosing information that by its nature must remain secret for the security or political interests of the State carries five to ten years’ imprisonment, and disclosure for the purpose of espionage carries life imprisonment (Law No. 5237 arts. 329-330). Decisive questions are whether the information is genuinely secret, whether it had already become public, and whether an espionage purpose is shown by concrete facts.

Insult and insulting the President

For insult directed at a public official because of his or her duties, the minimum penalty may not be less than one year (Law No. 5237 art. 125/3-a); for insulting the President, prosecution requires the permission of the Minister of Justice (Law No. 5237 art. 299). On the line between criticism and insult, see our article listed under Related Articles.

Detention and Lengthy Detention: Can a News Report Be Evidence of a Crime?

In cases against journalists the most serious interference is often pre-trial detention. The Constitutional Court and the ECtHR carry out a two-stage review: first, whether there are strong indications and a ground for detention; second, whether the measure is a proportionate interference with press freedom.

Statutory Provision — Code of Criminal Procedure (Law No. 5271) art. 100/1 (Grounds for detention) (unofficial translation)

“(1) Where there is concrete evidence showing the existence of strong suspicion of an offence and a ground for detention, a detention order may be issued against the suspect or accused. A detention order may not be issued if it would be disproportionate to the importance of the case and to the expected penalty or security measure.”

The words “concrete evidence” replaced the word “facts” in 2014. Decisions ordering or continuing detention, or rejecting a request for release, must state with concrete facts the strong suspicion, the ground for detention, proportionality and the inadequacy of judicial supervision (Law No. 5271 art. 101/2); stereotyped reasoning does not satisfy this requirement. In cases within the jurisdiction of assize courts, detention may last up to two years and may be extended where necessary, but the extension may not exceed three years in total, or five years for terrorist offences and the State security offences listed in the Code; in such offences detention during the investigation stage may not exceed one year and six months, extendable by six months (Law No. 5271 art. 102).

In the detention of a newspaper’s editor-in-chief and its Ankara bureau chief, the Plenary of the Constitutional Court found that the main basis for detention was the publication of two news reports, that no other concrete evidence was shown, that a similar report had been published months earlier in another newspaper and that the need to detain them some six months after the investigation began had not been explained; it held that the right to liberty and the freedoms of expression and of the press had been violated (Erdem Gül and Can Dündar [Plenary], no. 2015/18567, 25.02.2016; see the principle box below). By contrast, the Court declared inadmissible as manifestly ill-founded the application of an investigative journalist detained on account of articles, interviews and posts (Ahmet Şık [Plenary], no. 2017/5375, 02.05.2019), whereas the ECtHR, finding that the material contributed to public debate, contained no incitement to violence and could not ground a reasonable suspicion, held unanimously that Article Five § 1 and by a majority that Article Ten had been violated (Şık v. Turkey (No. 2), no. 36493/17, 24.11.2020).

Proportionality is also assessed separately for reports on State secrets. In respect of a journalist detained for about five months over six articles on the military operation in Libya, the ECtHR accepted that only one article contained confidential information (the identity of the commander of the operation) capable of causing considerable damage to national security, but held unanimously that the detention was excessive and had a chilling effect and therefore violated Article Ten; the Constitutional Court Plenary had found no violation in 2022 (Müyesser Uğur v. Türkiye, no. 45738/22, 06.10.2026; the Chamber judgment is not yet final).

Fair Trial Guarantees Specific to Journalists

Lawfulness, an independent and impartial tribunal

The application of a criminal provision to journalistic activity must be foreseeable. The ECtHR Grand Chamber regarded treating civil-society activities as the material element of a grave offence, and attributing liability for violence by others without direct participation or incitement, as an unforeseeable extension, held that the interference was not “prescribed by law” and had a serious chilling effect, and found by fifteen votes to two that shortcomings affecting the independence and impartiality of the courts breached Article Six § 1 as well (Kavala v. Türkiye (No. 2) [GC], no. 2170/24, 25.08.2026). Although the case concerned a human-rights defender, it offers guidance for organisation charges built on a newspaper’s editorial line.

Equality of arms and restricted access to the case file

Access to the investigation file is a precondition for effectively challenging detention. A restriction may be ordered only in the offences listed in the Code; since terrorist and State-secret offences are on the list, it is common in cases involving journalists.

Statutory Provision — Code of Criminal Procedure (Law No. 5271) art. 153 (Defence counsel’s right to examine the file) (unofficial translation)

“(2) Defence counsel’s right to examine the content of the file or to take copies of documents may be restricted by a decision of the judge, upon the request of the public prosecutor, if it could jeopardise the purpose of the investigation. Such a decision may be issued only in investigations concerning the following offences: … (3) The second paragraph shall not apply to records containing the statements of the arrested person or the suspect, to expert reports, or to records of other judicial acts at which those persons are entitled to be present. (4) From the date on which the indictment is accepted by the court, defence counsel may examine the content of the file and the evidence held in custody and may obtain copies of all records and documents free of charge.”

A restriction must not completely prevent access to the evidence on which detention is based. Not every restriction, however, automatically leads to a violation: according to the Constitutional Court, where the essential evidence was disclosed to the person during questioning and could be addressed in the objection to detention, the mere existence of a restriction order is not a violation, and the Court accordingly declared the complaint about access to the file manifestly ill-founded in the Erdem Gül and Can Dündar judgment cited above. An objection should therefore show concretely which evidence could not be accessed and how this affected the defence.

Public hearings and the right to follow trials

Hearings are public; they may be held wholly or partly in private where public morals or public safety make this strictly necessary, and the reasoned decision on this and the judgment are pronounced in open court (Law No. 5271 art. 182). Journalists may follow and report on public hearings, but devices allowing sound or image recording or transmission may not be used in the courthouse or in the courtroom once the hearing has begun (Law No. 5271 art. 183). Publicly breaching the confidentiality of statements or images from a closed hearing is an offence (Law No. 5237 art. 285/3).

Balancing investigative secrecy and reporting

Subject to other statutory provisions and without prejudice to the rights of the defence, procedural acts during the investigation are confidential (Law No. 5271 art. 157). This general confidentiality does not prohibit reporting on an investigation; criminal liability arises only under the specific conditions below.

Statutory Provision — Turkish Penal Code (Law No. 5237) art. 285 (Breach of confidentiality) (unofficial translation)

“(1) A person who publicly breaches the confidentiality of an investigation shall be punished by imprisonment of one to three years or a judicial fine. For this offence to be committed: a) the right to be presumed innocent, the confidentiality of communications or the privacy of private life must be violated by disclosing the content of an act carried out during the investigation, or b) the disclosure concerning the content of an act carried out during the investigation must be capable of preventing the discovery of the material truth. … (6) Reporting on investigative and trial proceedings without exceeding the limits of news reporting shall not constitute an offence.”

The ECtHR Grand Chamber found compatible with the Convention a fine imposed on a journalist who published, in sensationalist terms, confidential interview records and letters written by the accused to the investigating judge, listing as criteria the manner in which the information was obtained, the content of the article, its contribution to public debate, the risk of influencing the proceedings, the accused’s private life and the proportionality of the penalty (Bédat v. Switzerland [GC], no. 56925/08, 29.03.2016).

Presumption of innocence, unlawful evidence and reasonable time

No one may be considered guilty until proven guilty by a court, and findings obtained unlawfully may not be admitted as evidence (Constitution art. 38). The presumption of innocence protects both the journalist on trial and the persons reported on: publishing images that lead persons to be perceived as guilty during investigation or trial is punishable (Law No. 5237 art. 285/5). Trial within a reasonable time is a separate guarantee covering both detention and the proceedings as a whole.

Protection of Sources, Searches and Digital Material

Confidentiality of journalistic sources is a basic condition of press freedom: if sources are not protected, information of public concern does not reach the press. Turkish law guarantees it in an express statutory provision.

Statutory Provision — Press Law (Law No. 5187) art. 12 (News sources) (unofficial translation)

“The owner of a periodical, the responsible editor and the author cannot be compelled to disclose any news sources, including information and documents, or to testify on this matter.”

A journalist may rely on this right when asked about a source during questioning or as a witness, and non-disclosure cannot be held against him or her. The guarantee must not be circumvented either: seizing a journalist’s computer or phone or a media outlet’s servers is an indirect interference with source protection, since it may reveal a source’s identity.

Searching, copying and seizing computers requires strong suspicion based on concrete evidence and the absence of any other means of obtaining evidence; the order is given as a rule by a judge, or in urgent cases by the public prosecutor, whose order must be submitted for judicial approval within twenty-four hours; on seizure all data are backed up and a copy is given to the suspect or his or her lawyer (Law No. 5271 art. 134). The scope of the search should be limited in the order to the charge. As for printed works, no more than three copies may be seized as evidence for the investigation; seizure of the entire print run is possible only for listed offences and by a judge’s decision (Press Law No. 5187 art. 25).

Access Bans, Content Removal and Publication Bans

The most common form of interference with news websites is blocking access and removing content. For news content Law No. 5651 provided three main routes; one of them is no longer in force.

Catalogue offences: for publications in respect of which there is sufficient suspicion that their content constitutes offences listed in the Law, such as obscenity, sexual abuse of children, incitement to suicide or gambling, the judge decides during the investigation and the court during the trial, and this protective measure may be challenged under the Code of Criminal Procedure (Law No. 5651 art. 8). As a rule, blocking is ordered at the level of the infringing content (URL); blocking the entire website is possible only where the infringement cannot otherwise be prevented.

Urgent cases: on one of the grounds of the right to life, safety of life and property, national security, public order, prevention of crime or public health, a judge or, in urgent cases, the President of Cyber Security at the request of the Presidency of the Republic or the relevant ministries may order removal and/or blocking; the President’s decision must be submitted to a criminal judgeship of peace within twenty-four hours and lapses automatically if the judge does not decide within forty-eight hours (Law No. 5651 art. 8/A). For news content the reasoning should identify the concrete danger prevented and explain why the ban could not be limited to specific URLs; an objection should raise the public interest, the factual basis and the indefinite duration of the ban.

Blocking on the ground of personality rights: most bans on news reports relied on this route. In a pilot judgment concerning access bans on 129 news reports on news websites, the Plenary of the Constitutional Court found that the violation stemmed directly from the law and notified the Grand National Assembly (Keskin Kalem Yayıncılık ve Ticaret A.Ş. and Others [Plenary], no. 2018/14884, 27.10.2021; see the principle box below), and then annulled the provision in its entirety, with the annulment taking effect nine months after publication in the Official Gazette (Constitutional Court, E.2020/76, K.2023/172, 11.10.2023; Official Gazette 10.01.2024). The annulment took effect on 10.10.2024; as of 25.10.2026 the text in the Official Legislation Information System shows the provision as “annulled”, with no replacement (Law No. 5651 art. 9). Blocking on the ground of privacy remains in force under a separate provision (Law No. 5651 art. 9/A).

Constitutional Court, ECtHR and Court of Cassation Decisions

Detention based solely on news reports violates liberty and press freedom

Constitutional Court Decision
Constitutional Court (Plenary), Erdem Gül and Can Dündar, no. 2015/18567, 25.02.2016 (the Court’s own reasoning, §§ 97 and 99; violation of Constitution arts. 19/3, 26 and 28 by a majority; Official Gazette 10.03.2016)

Summary of the holding: since the only fact relied on for the charges was the publication of the news reports, a detention that did not satisfy the requirement of lawfulness could not be regarded as a necessary and proportionate interference with the freedoms of expression and of the press in a democratic society, and detaining the applicants without any concrete fact other than the published reports was liable to have a chilling effect on those freedoms. (The judgment is available in Turkish; no official English text was used.)

Acts in the normal course of journalistic activity do not ground a reasonable suspicion

ECtHR Judgment
ECtHR, Sabuncu and Others v. Turkey, no. 23199/17, 10.11.2020 (Chamber judgment, §§ 159 and 179; violation of Article Five § 1 and Article Ten)

“Given the complete absence of any incriminating content, the telephone calls made by Mehmet Murat Sabuncu and Akın Atalay to public figures against whom criminal proceedings were later brought constitute acts consistent with the normal course of journalistic activity and cannot be regarded as grounding a reasonable suspicion that the applicants had committed the criminal offences of which they were accused. … In the Court’s view, such an interpretation of the criminal law is not only difficult to reconcile with the domestic legislation recognising public freedoms, but also posed a considerable risk to the Convention system, resulting in any person expressing a view at odds with the views advocated by the government and the official authorities being characterised as a terrorist or a person assisting terrorists.”

In this case on the detention of a newspaper’s writers and executives, the ECtHR found violations in respect of all applicants other than the two for whom the Constitutional Court had already found violations, and found no violation of Article Eighteen.

There is no collective liability for press offences; aiding requires direct intent

Court of Cassation Decision
16th Criminal Chamber, E. 2019/7004, K. 2019/5220, 12.09.2019 (the Chamber’s own reasoning; the convictions of the newspaper’s executives and writers were quashed for most defendants)

“But in democracies such protection never justifies the punishment of ideas. … As in Turkish Penal Code No. 5237, no principle of objective liability has been adopted for offences allegedly committed through periodical and non-periodical publications under the press law. The liability arising from such publications is the direct liability of the author and, where the conditions are met, of the editorial managers. A notion of collective punishment for press offences has not been adopted.” (translation)

The Chamber held that aiding an organisation requires direct and specific intent, that defendants who were neither authors nor editors-in-chief could not be convicted without concrete evidence, free from any doubt, that they acted to aid the organisation, and stressed the binding effect of the Constitutional Court’s violation judgment concerning one defendant. It considered, however, that one defendant’s publication, during an armed attack, of the attackers’ statements legitimising violence after a telephone conversation with them could constitute the offence under Law No. 3713 art. 6/2. At the time the Chamber regarded internet publications as outside the Press Law; Law No. 7418 changed this for news websites.

Propaganda requires that violent methods be legitimised or praised

Court of Cassation Decision
3rd Criminal Chamber, E. 2021/13232, K. 2023/7589, 19.10.2023 (the Chamber’s reasons for quashing; defence counsel argued that the defendant was a journalist and that the posts remained within the limits of news reporting)

“For the offence of making propaganda for a terrorist organisation to be committed, in addition to being carried out with the aim of introducing, instilling or spreading a doctrine, idea or belief relating to the terrorist organisation, it must be carried out in a manner that legitimises the terrorist organisation’s methods involving force, violence and threats, praises those methods or encourages recourse to them.” (translation)

The Chamber found that the posts on which the conviction was based did not legitimise or praise violent methods, held that the defendant should have been acquitted, and unanimously quashed the judgment.

Reporting on an investigation within the limits of news reporting is not an offence

Court of Cassation Decision
12th Criminal Chamber, E. 2020/11238, K. 2022/9312, 30.11.2022 (the Chamber’s own reasoning; the acquittal for breach of confidentiality was upheld)

“As expressly emphasised by the provision in paragraph 6 of the article, ‘Reporting on investigative and trial proceedings without exceeding the limits of news reporting shall not constitute an offence’, this offence does not abolish press freedom but merely restricts it. The legislative reasoning likewise states that, provided the right of persons to benefit from the presumption of innocence is not violated, the discovery of the material truth is not prevented and the privacy of private life or of communications is not violated, reporting on the content of acts carried out during the investigation must be assessed within the framework of the right to report.” (translation)

In that case it had been alleged that information from an investigation file was passed to the press and reported. The Chamber upheld the trial court’s finding that general information, not capable of putting pressure on the complainant or of preventing the discovery of the material truth, had been reported within the limits of news reporting.

The personality-rights blocking procedure created a structural problem (pilot judgment)

Constitutional Court Decision
Constitutional Court (Plenary), Keskin Kalem Yayıncılık ve Ticaret A.Ş. and Others, no. 2018/14884, 27.10.2021 (the Court’s own reasoning, §§ 157 and 159; unanimous violation of Constitution arts. 26, 28 and 40, pilot judgment; Official Gazette 07.01.2022)

Summary of the holding: the violation stemmed directly from the law, which lacked the basic safeguards for protecting the freedoms of expression and of the press, and the provision giving rise to the violation therefore had to be reviewed in order to remedy the violation and prevent similar new violations. (The judgment is available in Turkish; no official English text was used.)

The Court found that the procedure in Law No. 5651 art. 9 as it stood before annulment was a non-adversarial route without a hearing and with indefinite effects, that it should therefore have been limited to exceptional cases where an infringement of personality rights was apparent at first sight (prima facie), and that the criminal judgeships of peace had not respected this limit. Examination of similar applications was postponed for one year, and retrial and non-pecuniary damages were ordered.

Implementing Constitutional Court and ECtHR Judgments: Retrial

A violation judgment of the Constitutional Court also determines what must be done to remedy the violation and its consequences.

Statutory Provision — Law on the Establishment and Rules of Procedure of the Constitutional Court (Law No. 6216) art. 50/2 (Decisions) (unofficial translation)

“(2) If the violation found arises from a court decision, the file shall be sent to the relevant court for retrial in order to remedy the violation and its consequences. Where there is no legal interest in a retrial, compensation may be awarded to the applicant or the applicant may be referred to the general courts. The court obliged to conduct the retrial shall, where possible on the basis of the file, deliver a decision that remedies the violation and its consequences as explained by the Constitutional Court in its violation judgment.”

In its pilot judgment the Constitutional Court stated that, in a retrial following a violation judgment, the trial court has no discretion as to whether there is a ground for retrial, and must, without waiting for a request, set aside the decision that caused the violation and issue a new decision consistent with the finding. In journalists’ cases this may mean ending detention, lifting an access ban or setting aside a conviction.

A final ECtHR judgment finding that a criminal judgment was given in violation of the Convention and was based on that violation, or a strike-out decision following a friendly settlement or unilateral declaration, is a ground for reopening; the request must be made within one year of the ECtHR judgment becoming final (Law No. 5271 art. 311/1-f). Execution of ECtHR judgments is supervised by the Committee of Ministers of the Council of Europe under Article Forty-Six of the Convention, and the Committee may bring “infringement proceedings” before the Court if it considers a judgment is not being complied with. In Kavala (No. 2) the Grand Chamber, taking into account the failure to execute earlier judgments including the one in the infringement proceedings, held that the applicant must be released at the earliest possible date, that the consequences of his conviction must be eliminated and that general measures must be taken.

Practical Guide: From Police Custody to Strasbourg

Custody and questioning

Police custody depends on its being necessary for the investigation and on concrete evidence of suspicion; it may not as a rule exceed twenty-four hours and, for collective offences, may be extended by written order of the public prosecutor by one day at a time up to three days (Law No. 5271 art. 91). During questioning the person is reminded of the right to remain silent and the right to request that concrete evidence be gathered to dispel suspicion (Law No. 5271 art. 147). A journalist can explain which public debate a report concerns and which documents it relied on without revealing the source.

Objecting to detention and to a restriction order

Detention orders (Law No. 5271 art. 101/5) and other judges’ decisions such as an order restricting access to the file (Law No. 5271 art. 267) may be challenged by objection. Since the amendment by Law No. 7499 the time limit for an objection is two weeks from learning of the decision, and objections to detention and judicial-supervision decisions of a criminal judgeship of peace are examined by a criminal court of first instance judge (Law No. 5271 art. 268). The suspect or accused may request release at any stage (Law No. 5271 art. 104), and during the investigation detention is reviewed at intervals of no more than thirty days (Law No. 5271 art. 108). An objection should show concretely why each publication stays within the limits of news reporting or criticism and why judicial supervision would suffice.

Time limit for prosecuting press offences

Statutory Provision — Press Law (Law No. 5187) art. 26 (Time limits for prosecution) (unofficial translation)

“As a procedural condition, criminal proceedings concerning offences committed through printed works or news websites or other offences provided for in this Law must be brought within four months for daily periodicals and news websites and within six months for other printed works. These periods begin on the date on which the printed works are delivered to the Chief Public Prosecutor’s Office and, for news websites, on the date on which the criminal complaint concerning the news report is made. … However, these periods may not exceed the periods of limitation for prosecution provided for in the Turkish Penal Code.”

The time limit is a procedural condition: a case not brought in time cannot be examined on the merits. For offences prosecuted on complaint, the period runs from the date on which the commission of the offence became known; where prosecution requires permission, the period is suspended upon the application for permission, but the suspension may not exceed four months. These cases are treated as urgent (Press Law No. 5187 art. 27). Whether the publication appeared on a news website or on a social-media account is therefore the first procedural question to examine.

Individual application to the Constitutional Court and interim measures

Anyone whose right guaranteed by the Constitution and falling within the Convention has been violated by public authority may apply to the Constitutional Court after exhausting ordinary remedies (Constitution art. 148/3). The application must be lodged within thirty days of exhaustion of remedies or, where no remedy is available, of learning of the violation; in case of a valid excuse it may be lodged within fifteen days of the excuse ceasing (Law No. 6216 art. 47/5). The Sections may order interim measures they consider necessary to protect fundamental rights, of their own motion or upon request; if a measure is ordered, the decision on the merits must be given within six months at the latest (Law No. 6216 art. 49/5). According to the Constitutional Court, a complaint that detention violated press freedom does not require the trial to be completed; exhausting the objection against detention is sufficient (Erdem Gül and Can Dündar, § 58). The application form should set out the liberty and press-freedom complaints under separate headings.

Applying to the ECtHR

Protocol No. 15 reduced the time limit for applying to the ECtHR from six to four months; the new limit applies to final domestic decisions given after 01.02.2022. An individual application to the Constitutional Court is as a rule a remedy that must be exhausted; in Kavala (No. 2) the ECtHR held that the applicant could not be criticised for not awaiting its outcome because that remedy had become manifestly excessive in length in the particular circumstances. That exceptional assessment is not a general rule.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a journalist be compelled to disclose a source?

No. The owner of a periodical, the responsible editor and the author cannot be compelled to disclose any news source, including information and documents, or to testify on this matter; non-disclosure cannot be held against the journalist.

Can a journalist be detained solely because of a news report?

Detention requires concrete evidence of strong suspicion and a ground for detention. The Constitutional Court and the ECtHR have held that detentions ordered without any concrete fact other than published reports violate the right to liberty and press freedom.

Is it a crime to report on an ongoing investigation?

As a rule, no. Reporting on investigative and trial proceedings without exceeding the limits of news reporting is not an offence (Law No. 5237 art. 285/6). The offence of breach of confidentiality requires that the disclosure violate the presumption of innocence, confidentiality of communications or privacy, or be capable of preventing the discovery of the material truth.

What is the time limit for prosecuting press offences?

Four months for daily periodicals and news websites and six months for other printed works; for news websites the period runs from the criminal complaint concerning the report and may not exceed the limitation period for prosecution.

How can an access ban on a news report be challenged?

Decisions concerning catalogue offences may be challenged under the Code of Criminal Procedure (Law No. 5651 art. 8) within two weeks of learning of the decision (Law No. 5271 art. 268). The provision on blocking for personality rights was annulled, with effect from 10.10.2024. After the objection is rejected, an individual application to the Constitutional Court may be lodged within thirty days.

What are the time limits for applying to the Constitutional Court and the ECtHR?

An individual application to the Constitutional Court must be lodged within thirty days of exhaustion of remedies (Law No. 6216 art. 47/5). The time limit for applying to the ECtHR is four months for final domestic decisions given after 01.02.2022.
