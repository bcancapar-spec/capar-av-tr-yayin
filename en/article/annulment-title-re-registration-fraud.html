Annulment of Title and Re-Registration for Fraud in Türkiye: Elements, One-Year Period, Proof, Third Parties

Short answer: An owner who sold immovable property as a result of deceit (fraud) is not bound by the contract even if the error is not fundamental (Code of Obligations art. 36), and may sue for cancellation of the title entry and re-registration in their name. To do so, the owner must, within one year of discovering the deceit, declare that they are not bound by the contract or reclaim what they performed; otherwise the contract is deemed ratified (Code of Obligations art. 39), and the declaration may also be made by filing suit. Fraud may be proved by any evidence, including witnesses; if the property has passed to someone else, a third party who relied on the land register in good faith is protected, whereas one who knew or ought to have known of the wrongful registration is not (Civil Code arts. 1023-1024). It is important to seek an interim injunction annotated in the land register against a transfer during the proceedings; mediation is not a precondition for this action.

This article explains Turkish law. It is an English translation of the Turkish original; in case of any discrepancy, the Turkish text prevails.

In brief

An owner who sold immovable property as a result of deceit (fraud) is not bound by the contract under art. 36 of the Turkish Code of Obligations; within one year of discovering the deceit, the owner must declare this or reclaim what was performed, and the declaration may also be made by filing suit. What Is Fraud (Deceit) and What Are Its Elements?

The Turkish Code of Obligations calls this defect of consent “deceit” (aldatma); Court of Cassation decisions also use the term “fraud” (hile) with the same meaning. The Court of Cassation defines fraud as intentionally creating a false belief in a person, or preserving or prolonging an existing false belief, in order to induce that person to conclude a contract. Its elements are: (1) an act of deceit — misleading conduct such as presenting what does not exist as existing or concealing what exists, or silence on a matter that must be disclosed under the rules of good faith (there is, however, no general duty of disclosure); (2) intent to deceive — deliberately misleading the other party in order to induce it to contract; (3) causation — the contract must have been concluded “as a result of” the deceit. The error need not be fundamental.

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 36 (Deceit) (unofficial translation)

“Where one party has concluded a contract as a result of deceit by the other, that party is not bound by the contract even if its error is not fundamental. A party who has concluded a contract as a result of deceit by a third person is not bound by the contract if, at the time the contract was concluded, the other party knew or was in a position to know of the deceit.”

The act of deceit: false information, deliberate concealment and promises

The Grand Civil Chamber describes the act of deceit as the deceiver giving untrue information on certain matters during the conclusion of the contract, and especially during negotiations, or deliberately concealing matters which the rules of good faith required it to disclose (Grand Civil Chamber, E. 2024/46, K. 2025/282). This formula also covers deceit by silence; silence, however, amounts to deceit only on a matter subject to a duty of disclosure. That duty is determined in the concrete case according to the parties’ relationship, the information gap between them and the weight of the concealed information in the other party’s decision. Promises concerning future conduct may also constitute deceit: in the same decision, a defendant who secured the transfer by saying that, if the property were transferred to him, he would cover the seller’s need for money with a loan he would take out was held to have committed fraudulent acts through promising words and reassuring conduct.

Intent to deceive and causation

Intent to deceive means knowingly and willingly acting contrary to the truth in order to induce the other party to contract. According to the same Grand Civil Chamber decision, there is no intent to deceive where a person made a statement without knowing the true position, even if that ignorance amounts to gross fault; in such a case the other party may escape the contract only if the conditions of fundamental error are met. For causation, it is sufficient that, without the deceit, the deceived party would either not have concluded the contract at all or would have concluded it on better terms. Even if the seller was already thinking of selling, causation may be established if the deceit led to a lower price.

The deceit must precede or accompany the conclusion of the contract

As a defect of consent is a defect in the formation of the will, deceit must have occurred before or at the conclusion of the contract; a promise broken afterwards is in itself a breach of contract. Later conduct may, however, indicate earlier deceit: the Grand Civil Chamber assessed the high-value mortgage created immediately after the transfer and the sale of the share to an acquaintance shortly afterwards together with the transfer, and concluded that the seller’s consent had been vitiated before the transfer (Grand Civil Chamber, E. 2024/46, K. 2025/282).

Difference from exploitation (gabin): in deceit intentional misleading suffices and no imbalance between the performances is required; exploitation requires a clear imbalance between the performances obtained by taking advantage of the injured party’s distress, thoughtlessness or inexperience, and the periods for exercising the right differ (Code of Obligations art. 28). Details are in our article on annulment of title for exploitation (gabin).

How Does Fraud Differ from Mistake, Duress, Simulation and Fiduciary Transactions?

Mistake (error): in mistake a person errs on his own, and the mistake must be fundamental for the person to escape the contract (Code of Obligations art. 30). Code of Obligations art. 31 lists examples of fundamental mistake, one being where the mistaken party declared its will for a contract other than the one it intended to conclude. In the case of an elderly claimant who alleged that she had been taken to the land registry to let another plot and that a gift of the adjoining plot had been procured instead, the Grand Civil Chamber required both the mistake and the deceit allegations to be examined on the merits (Grand Civil Chamber, E. 2018/889, K. 2018/1939). In deceit, the error need not be fundamental.

Duress: in duress consent is obtained not by misleading but by pressure. Duress exists where the party threatened was justified, in the circumstances, in believing that a serious and imminent danger had arisen to the personality rights or property of himself or of a person close to him (Code of Obligations art. 38). The two institutions diverge where a third person acts: a party under duress by a third person is not bound even if the other party did not know of it, and pays compensation to the other party in good faith only where equity so requires (Code of Obligations art. 37). In deceit by a third person, by contrast, the other party must have known or been in a position to know of the deceit (Code of Obligations art. 36). The one-year period also runs from discovery in deceit and from the cessation of the effect of the threat in duress (Code of Obligations art. 39).

Simulation and the decedent’s simulated transactions: in simulation the parties do not in fact want the apparent transaction and create, by common intention, an appearance designed to mislead third parties; the type and content of a contract are determined according to the parties’ true and common intention (Code of Obligations art. 19). In deceit there is no common hidden intention; one party’s consent has been vitiated by the other’s intentional conduct. Sham transfers intended to deprive heirs of their inheritance fall under decedent’s simulated transactions. Classification directly affects the outcome: the Court of Cassation reversed a judgment in which the court of first instance had treated the dispute as a decedent’s simulated transaction although the action was based on fraud; it required the court first to determine whether the action had been brought within the preclusive period and then, once all necessary parties were joined, to examine the fraud allegation (1st Civil Chamber, E. 2014/8891, K. 2015/9631).

Fiduciary transaction: where it is alleged that the property was transferred “temporarily” as security for a debt or to obtain a loan, the other party often argues that a valid fiduciary agreement exists. In a transfer obtained with the promise that a loan would be taken out and passed on to the claimant, the Grand Civil Chamber held that the regional court of appeal had wrongly treated the dispute as a fiduciary transaction and accepted that the promise was part of the fraudulent conduct. According to the Grand Chamber, legal characterisation is for the judge (Code of Civil Procedure art. 33), a mere change of legal ground not affecting the facts does not fall foul of the prohibition on extending claims, and the preliminary examination record is the roadmap of the proceedings (Grand Civil Chamber, E. 2024/46, K. 2025/282).

What if a Third Party Deceived the Seller?

Where the deceiver is a third person, the seller is released only if the buyer, at the time of contracting, knew or was in a position to know of the deceit. The Court of Cassation has held that the buyer need not have known of the deceit, being in a position to know is sufficient; in the case of an owner who transferred his home after being told that his property would be confiscated, it considered a buyer who purchased without any advertisement, below the value determined on site inspection and through an agent without speaking to the seller living on the same housing estate to be in a position to know. Who counts as a “third person” also matters: the Grand Civil Chamber held that the fraud of a person who personally concluded the contract on behalf of the other party under a power of attorney is treated as the contracting party’s own fraud, without any requirement that the latter knew or was in a position to know of it; in the same decision, the fraud allegation was not sufficiently proved by the witness statements and the judgment of dismissal was upheld by majority (Grand Civil Chamber, E. 2017/1216, K. 2021/60). Compensation may also be claimed from a deceiving third person under the rules on torts (Code of Obligations art. 49).

Is a Sale Induced by Fraud Automatically Void?

No. The deceived party is “not bound” by the contract, but whether to rely on this is left to that party. According to the Court of Cassation, by exercising this right the deceived party may set aside the legal relationship retroactively and reclaim what it has performed; if it does not exercise the right in time, it is deemed to have ratified the contract. The Grand Civil Chamber states expressly that the sanction attached to a defect of consent is not absolute nullity and that the law grants the person whose consent was vitiated a right of avoidance exercisable within a certain period (Grand Civil Chamber, E. 2018/889, K. 2018/1939).

Within What Period Must a Fraud Claim Be Brought?

The deceived party must, within one year of discovering the deceit, declare that it is not bound by the contract or reclaim what it performed; the period runs from discovery, not from the date of sale, and Court of Cassation decisions classify it as a preclusive period. Since an action also serves as this declaration, the safest course is to file suit within that year. Even if the period is missed, the right to compensation is not extinguished. For the difference between preclusive and limitation periods, see this article.

Statutory Provision — Turkish Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) art. 39 (Removal of the defect of consent) (unofficial translation)

“A party who has concluded a contract by reason of error or deceit, or as a result of duress, shall be deemed to have ratified the contract if, within one year from the moment the error or deceit was discovered or the effect of the duress ceased, that party does not declare that it is not bound by the contract or does not reclaim what it has performed. The deemed ratification of a contract that is not binding because of deceit or duress does not extinguish the right to compensation.”

The starting point is not the date of the official deed: in the case of a claimant who alleged that she had learned in 2009, from a letter of the municipality, of a gift made in 2006, the Grand Civil Chamber reversed the insistence ruling that started the period from the date of the official deed (Grand Civil Chamber, E. 2018/889, K. 2018/1939). The claimant must explain and prove concretely what was learned, when and how. In an affirmed finding, the date of the seller’s application to the Chief Public Prosecutor’s Office was taken as the date of discovery (1st Civil Chamber, E. 2025/1276, K. 2026/2682).

There is no absolute long-stop period: Code of Obligations art. 39 provides no separate maximum period running from the date of the contract for deceit; there is no counterpart to the five-year upper limit for exploitation (Code of Obligations art. 28). Compensation that may be claimed even where the contract is deemed ratified is, however, subject to limitation (for torts, Code of Obligations art. 72).

Is a Notice of Avoidance Required, or Is Filing Suit Enough?

The exercise of the right of avoidance is not subject to any form: within the one-year period it may be exercised by a declaration of intent addressed to the other party, by way of defence or by way of action, so an action filed without any prior notice also serves as the declaration. The 1st Civil Chamber treated an action brought in time by the deceased owner, later deemed not to have been filed because it was not pursued (that ruling not being final), as a declaration as well, and found the heirs’ subsequent action to be in time (by majority). A notice served through a notary is a suitable means of preserving the period; however, since the time limit for an action brought after the notice may be disputed, filing the action within the year is the safest course.

How Is the Title Recovered Once Fraud Is Established?

A registration based on a sale that does not bind the deceived party is wrongful. The action is brought for cancellation of the title entry and re-registration in the owner’s name; in case the property cannot be recovered, a claim for “the price, failing that” or for compensation may be added in the alternative.

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code (Law No. 4721) art. 1024 (Against third parties not in good faith) (unofficial translation)

“Where a right in rem has been registered wrongfully, a third party who knew or ought to have known this cannot rely on that registration. A registration based on a legal transaction that is not binding, or lacking a legal ground, is wrongful. A person whose right in rem is impaired by such a registration may assert the wrongfulness of the registration directly against third parties not in good faith.”

The right defendant must be chosen: the action is brought against the registered owner at the date of the action. The court may not examine the subsidiary claim unless it dismisses the principal claim on the merits (Code of Civil Procedure art. 111); if the property changed hands before the action, an alternative action against the first buyer of the type “annulment of title, failing that the price” is therefore risky. In an affirmed finding of a regional court of appeal, the annulment claim in such an action was dismissed for lack of standing and it was held that there was no need to decide the claim for the price (1st Civil Chamber, E. 2026/1388, K. 2026/2071). An up-to-date land register extract should be obtained; the annulment claim should be directed against the new owner and the claim for the price or compensation against the deceiving first buyer as a principal claim. If the property is transferred after the action is filed, the claimant may continue against the transferee or convert the action into one for compensation against the transferor (Code of Civil Procedure art. 125).

The general rules on standing, jurisdiction and wrongful registration are explained in our article on the action for annulment of title and re-registration.

Can the Heirs Sue if the Seller Has Died?

Yes, but the way the action is constituted matters. The estate is held jointly by the heirs; at the request of one heir the civil court of peace may appoint a representative for the community of heirs (Civil Code art. 640). The 1st Civil Chamber stated that, apart from actions such as those based on a decedent’s simulated transactions, which are torts against the estate, all heirs must bring the action together or the consent of the other heirs must be obtained for a return to the estate, and held that the fraud action of a claimant who limited his claim to registration in proportion to his own share could not be heard (1st Civil Chamber, E. 2015/18476, K. 2016/3023). Another decision likewise stressed that the consent of heirs not party to the action must be obtained or the action continued through a representative (1st Civil Chamber, E. 2014/8891, K. 2015/9631). An action brought in time by the deceased may also count as the declaration for the heirs (see the 2021 decision below).

How Is Fraud Proved?

Fraud may be proved by any evidence, and the burden of proof lies on the party alleging deceit (Civil Code art. 6). Although the sale is made by official deed, witnesses may be heard on allegations of defective consent:

Statutory Provision — Code of Civil Procedure (Law No. 6100) art. 203/1(ç) (Exceptions to the requirement of documentary proof) (unofficial translation)

“(1) Witnesses may be heard in the following cases: … ç) Allegations of defective consent and of exploitation in legal transactions.”

Official registers and deeds constitute evidence of the truth of the facts they record; however, proof that their content is untrue is not subject to any form unless the law provides otherwise (Civil Code art. 7). For this reason the Grand Civil Chamber accepts that mistake, deceit and duress cannot be put in writing and that, even where the contract was made by official deed, deceit may be proved by any evidence including witnesses (Grand Civil Chamber, E. 2024/46, K. 2025/282). The fact that proof by witnesses is possible does not mean that any witness statement will suffice: in one case the Grand Civil Chamber did not consider the witness statements sufficient to accept that fraud had occurred (Grand Civil Chamber, E. 2017/1216, K. 2021/60), whereas in the 2025 decision it treated the deceit as proved by assessing the witness accounts together with documentary facts such as the mortgage created immediately after the transfer, the low price shown in the second sale and the parties’ acquaintance with each other.

Elderly, ill or illiterate sellers: medical records and Forensic Medicine

Fraud allegations are concentrated in transfers made by owners who are elderly, ill, living alone or unable to read and write. Hospital and doctors’ records, medication, bank movements relating to the price and statements of those present are the principal evidence. Lack of capacity of discernment (incapacity) is a ground separate from fraud. In a case where incapacity, a decedent’s simulated transaction, fraud and exploitation had been pleaded together but only simulation had been examined, the Court of Cassation reversed the judgment; it held that each legal ground must be investigated in order of importance, that incapacity must be examined first because it concerns public order, that for this purpose the deceased’s medical reports, prescriptions and hospital observation records should be obtained and a report sought from the Fourth Specialised Board of the Forensic Medicine Institute, and that, if the deceased was found to have had capacity, the fraud and exploitation allegations should then be assessed (1st Civil Chamber, E. 2018/2713, K. 2020/2953). Forensic examination is also used for handwriting: in the case of a seller who claimed to be illiterate, the Forensic Medicine Institute examined whose handwriting the words “I have read” on the official deed were; according to the affirmed finding the allegation was not proved, and it was noted that the oath had not been expressly relied on as evidence in the statement of claim (1st Civil Chamber, E. 2025/1276, K. 2026/2682). Where no other evidence exists, it is therefore important to rely expressly on the oath in the petition.

What Happens if the Property Has Been Sold On?

The position of a later owner is governed by the principle of reliance on the land register: the acquisition of a third party who acquired a right relying on the registration in good faith is protected (Civil Code art. 1023), whereas one who knew or ought to have known of the wrongful registration is not. Good faith is presumed, but a person who fails to exercise the care required by the circumstances cannot claim good faith (Civil Code art. 3). The Court of Cassation has required that, once fraud is established, it be separately assessed whether later owners can benefit from this protection, and has held that the good faith of a buyer acting together with the deceiver cannot be spoken of. Where the property remains with a person in good faith, all claims for compensation are reserved (Civil Code art. 1025).

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code (Law No. 4721) art. 1023 (Against third parties in good faith) (unofficial translation)

“The acquisition of a third party who, relying in good faith on the entry in the land register, acquires ownership or another right in rem is protected.”

Order of examination: the Chamber required that the deceit allegation be examined first in respect of the initial transfer and, if deceit is established, that it be clarified whether the defendant in the position of second-hand acquirer can benefit from the protection of Civil Code art. 1023 (that reversal by the Chamber is quoted in Grand Civil Chamber, E. 2022/862, K. 2023/283; the Grand Chamber sent the file back to the Chamber for review of the new judgment). Facts that negate good faith: the Grand Civil Chamber considered together that the share was sold about three and a half months after the first transfer, with the TRY 550,000 mortgage on it and a price of TRY 12,200 in the official deed (real value TRY 255,235 according to the expert), that the defendants knew each other beforehand and that the second buyer purchased without seeing the building, and held that the second buyer could not benefit from the protection of Civil Code art. 1023 (decision below).

Switching to compensation: where the property cannot be recovered from a third party in good faith, the deceived party claims compensation from the deceiving first buyer (Civil Code art. 1025). Such claims were often brought as actions for an undetermined claim. By Law No. 7589, published in the Official Gazette of 31.07.2026, Code of Civil Procedure art. 107 governing actions for an undetermined claim was repealed; under the transitional provision, that article continues to apply to actions filed before its repeal. Under the paragraph added to Code of Civil Procedure art. 109 by the same Law, where only part of a claim is sued for, the claim may be increased once in the same action until the end of the taking of evidence without being subject to the prohibition on extending the claim, and limitation is deemed interrupted from the date of the action also for the increased part.

Can the Price Paid Be Recovered, and Can Damages Be Claimed?

Once the contract is set aside retroactively, the price paid no longer rests on a valid ground and may be reclaimed under the rules on unjust enrichment (Code of Obligations art. 77); this claim is subject to the limitation periods in Code of Obligations art. 82. Compensation for loss caused by the deceit may also be claimed; even deemed ratification of the contract does not extinguish the right to compensation (Code of Obligations art. 39/2).

Mutual restitution and adjustment: restitution works both ways. A seller who recovers the title must return any price actually paid; in a significant proportion of fraud cases, however, no price was paid at all, and this fact is itself a main pillar of the claim. A person who parted with the enrichment not in good faith, or who ought to have reckoned when parting with it that he might have to return it, must return the whole enrichment (Code of Obligations art. 79). As to expenditure on the property, an enriched party in good faith may claim necessary and useful expenses, whereas one not in good faith may claim only necessary expenses and, of the useful expenses, only the increase in value existing at the time of restitution (Code of Obligations art. 80). If a price is proved to have been paid, a statement by the claimant at the outset that he is ready to return it makes it easier to resolve the dispute in a single action.

Interim Injunction and Annotation in the Land Register

If the defendant transfers the property during the proceedings to a third party in good faith, recovery of the property itself may become impossible (Civil Code art. 1023); an interim injunction is therefore often the first step when filing the action:

Statutory Provision — Code of Civil Procedure (Law No. 6100) art. 389/1 (Conditions of an interim injunction) (unofficial translation)

“(1) Where it is feared that, owing to a change that may occur in the existing situation, obtaining the right will become considerably more difficult or entirely impossible, or that delay will cause a drawback or serious damage, an interim injunction may be ordered concerning the subject matter of the dispute.”

An injunction prohibiting transfer and disposition is annotated in the land register and may be asserted against those who acquire rights after the annotation (provision below). The applicant must as a rule provide security; if the request is based on an official document or other conclusive evidence, or if the circumstances so require, the court may, stating its reasons, dispense with security, and a person receiving legal aid need not provide it (Code of Civil Procedure art. 392).

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code (Law No. 4721) art. 1010 (Restriction of the power of disposition) (unofficial translation)

“Restrictions on the power of disposition based on the following grounds may be annotated in the land register: 1. Court orders for the protection of disputed rights, … Restrictions on the power of disposition, once annotated, may be asserted against holders of rights subsequently acquired in the immovable.”

Which Court Hears the Case, and How Is the Court Fee Assessed?

Unless otherwise provided, the competent court is the civil court of first instance (Code of Civil Procedure art. 2); in actions concerning a right in rem in immovable property, the court of the place where the property is located has exclusive jurisdiction (Code of Civil Procedure art. 12). In actions concerning the immovable itself, such as cancellation of a title entry, the court fee is calculated on the value of the property, which must be stated in the statement of claim (art. 16 of Law No. 492 on Fees).

Possible commercial court jurisdiction between merchants: civil actions arising from matters relating to the commercial enterprises of both parties are commercial cases (Commercial Code art. 4), which are heard by the commercial court of first instance. In a judicial district without a commercial court, failure to rely on the rule of subject-matter jurisdiction does not require a ruling of lack of jurisdiction, and the civil court of first instance continues with the case (Commercial Code art. 5). Exclusive jurisdiction: no jurisdiction agreement may be made in cases of exclusive jurisdiction (Code of Civil Procedure art. 18), and the court must examine of its own motion until the end of the case whether it has jurisdiction (Code of Civil Procedure art. 19); where the action concerns more than one property, it may also be brought in respect of the others at the place where one of them is located (Code of Civil Procedure art. 12/3). Fee and value: if compensation is also claimed in an action concerning the immovable itself, the fee is charged on the value of the property plus the compensation claimed; where the value can be determined it must be stated in the petition, failing which the claimant is required to state it (Law No. 492 art. 16).

The chamber on appeal: most of the decisions in this article were rendered by the 1st Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation. Which chamber hears an appeal on points of law is determined by the division-of-work decisions of the Court of Cassation’s Grand General Assembly and may change over time; the current division of work should be checked at the time of filing and of appeal.

Is Mediation Mandatory?

No. Law No. 6325 art. 18/B, added by Law No. 7445 in 2023, exhaustively lists the disputes in which mediation is a precondition: lease, partition and termination of co-ownership, condominium and neighbour-law disputes. A claim for annulment of title and re-registration based on fraud is not among them. In a commercial case in which a sum of money or compensation is claimed, however, mediation is a precondition (Commercial Code art. 5/A).

Voluntary mediation is possible: disputes over the transfer of property are suitable for mediation; by the parties’ written agreement an annotation may be entered in the land register for the duration of the process, for a maximum of three months, the enforceability annotation on the settlement agreement is obtained from the civil court of peace where the property is located, and under the paragraph added by Law No. 7531 in 2024 registration may be carried out by the land registry without an official deed being drawn up (Law No. 6325 art. 17/B).

Court of Cassation Decisions

Deceit may also be committed by silence

Court of Cassation Decision
3rd Civil Chamber, E. 2023/1848, K. 2023/2597, 09.10.2023 (the Chamber’s own reasoning; affirmance in a case concerning the transfer of a vehicle)

“The act of deceit may be a positive act, an act of doing, or a negative act, an act of not doing, an abstention or silence. … Silence on a matter about which information must be given under the rules of good faith is also an act of deceit. There is no general duty of disclosure in law.” (translation)

Deliberate concealment is deceit; a second buyer connected with the deceiver cannot rely on the land register

Court of Cassation Decision
Grand Civil Chamber, E. 2024/46, K. 2025/282, 30.04.2025 (the Grand Chamber’s own reasoning; the insistence ruling was reversed by majority)

“The deceiver gives untrue information on certain subjects and matters during the conclusion of the contract and especially during negotiations, or deliberately conceals certain matters which it was required to disclose under the rule of good faith. … In the light of these findings, it has been concluded that defendant ... cannot benefit from the protection of article 1023 of Law No. 4721.” (translation)

In deceit by a third party it is sufficient that the buyer was in a position to know

Court of Cassation Decision
1st Civil Chamber, E. 2024/5666, K. 2025/5062, 17.11.2025 (the Chamber’s reasoning for reversal; unanimous)

“that it is not necessary for the defendant to have known of the deceit at the time the contract was concluded, and that the claimant is likewise not bound by the contract if the defendant was in a position to know of the deceit” (translation)

An action brought in time counts as the declaration; the right of avoidance is not subject to any form

Court of Cassation Decision
1st Civil Chamber, E. 2020/824, K. 2021/2024, 05.04.2021 (the Chamber’s reasoning for reversal; by majority)

“that by this action the deceased owner ... notified the first-hand defendant ..., within the one-year period provided in article 39 of the TBK, that she was not bound by the contract on grounds of deceit (fraud) … the exercise of the right of avoidance is not subject to any form. It may also be exercised, within the one-year preclusive period from the discovery of the deceit, by a declaration of intent addressed to the other party, by way of defence or by way of action.” (translation)

A buyer acting together with the deceiver is not in good faith

Court of Cassation Decision
1st Civil Chamber, E. 2022/455, K. 2022/2838, 06.04.2022 (quoted from the Chamber’s earlier reversal in the same case; the judgment granting the claim in line with it was affirmed)

“having regard to the price shown in the official deed and the market value of the property at the date of transfer, it is concluded that the transfer of the property took place through fraud. Since the defendants are partners in the same company and, according to the witness statements, their dealings with the claimant carried an intent to deceive aimed at the transfer of the property, it is clear that the good faith of defendant ... cannot be spoken of” (translation)

Advanced age, an unproven payment of the price and a defence contrary to the ordinary course of life may together prove deceit (Assembly of Civil Chambers)

Court of Cassation, Assembly of Civil Chambers (the Assembly’s reasoning)
Yargıtay Hukuk Genel Kurulu, E. 2021/482, K. 2023/992, T. 25.10.2023

“Accordingly, taking into account, besides their education and their social and economic circumstances, the advanced age of the claimant and his late sibling, it must be accepted that the likelihood of their will having been vitiated during the transfers of the properties in dispute was high. … it cannot be said that payment of the sale price in instalments at intervals over six months, starting months before the transfer at the land registry, and in cash in a parcel, is consistent with the ordinary course of life.” (unofficial translation)

Two siblings aged 86 and 87 transferred the bare ownership of their shares in two flats to the defendant while reserving usufruct; the claimant alleged that the defendant’s spouse, having gained their trust by helping with earlier land registry transactions, procured the transfer by persuading them that a signature was missing at the registry. The Chamber had reversed, finding that the siblings were educated and aware persons and that the witnesses had no first-hand knowledge, so that deceit was not proved. The Assembly of Civil Chambers instead assessed together the advanced age, witness evidence that there was no intention to transfer, the lack of sufficient evidence that the price was paid, and the implausibility of the defence of cash payments in parcels, held that the will had been vitiated by deceit, and upheld by majority the insistence judgment granting the claim. Although the action also relied on unfair exploitation (gabin), the Assembly based its conclusion on deceit and made no separate assessment of exploitation. The transfer dates from 2010, under the former Code of Obligations no. 818; the decision was rendered under the former procedural code with rectification of decision still available.

Where the claim rests only on incapacity, vitiated consent and abuse of agency cannot be raised for the first time on appeal (Assembly of Civil Chambers)

Court of Cassation, Assembly of Civil Chambers (the Assembly’s reasoning)
Yargıtay Hukuk Genel Kurulu, E. 2022/859, K. 2023/990, T. 25.10.2023

“…at the later stages of the proceedings (appeal and cassation), the assertion that there was in reality no genuine sale, that the sale was in fact made as security, that there was a defect of consent … and seeking cancellation of title and registration on these grounds, must now be regarded as an extension of the claim.” (unofficial translation)

The claimant sought cancellation of title on the ground that, owing to a long-treated psychiatric illness, he lacked capacity when signing the power of attorney and at the sale; after the Forensic Medicine Institute found him capable, he argued on appeal and in cassation that the sale was a security arrangement, that he had been deceived and that the agency had been abused. The Chamber reversed for an inquiry into abuse of agency; the Assembly of Civil Chambers held that, although the pleadings mentioned simulation, the claim ultimately rested solely on incapacity, that the claimant could have extended his claim at the preliminary hearing under Article 141 of the Code of Civil Procedure as then in force but did not, and that the later allegations could not be heard; it upheld the insistence judgment dismissing the action by majority, with no further appeal. The dissent considered that the statement of claim had described the deception and that an allegation of a power of attorney obtained by fraud also covers abuse of agency. The decision shows the importance of pleading the facts of deceit, and a claim on that legal ground, clearly from the outset at the written pleadings stage.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a title transfer obtained through fraud be cancelled?

Yes. An owner who sold as a result of deceit by the other party, or by a third person whose deceit the other party knew or was in a position to know of, is not bound by the contract; as the registration based on it is wrongful, cancellation of the title entry and re-registration in the owner’s name may be claimed (Civil Code art. 1024). If a price was paid, the seller returns it.

Within what period must a fraud action be filed?

Within one year of discovering the deceit, the deceived party must declare that it is not bound by the contract or reclaim what it performed; otherwise the contract is deemed ratified (Code of Obligations art. 39). The period runs from discovery, not from the date of sale, and no separate upper limit running from the date of the contract is provided for deceit. As the declaration may also be made by filing suit, filing within that year is the safest course.

How is fraud proved?

By any evidence, including witnesses; witnesses may be heard on allegations of defective consent as an exception to the requirement of documentary proof (Code of Civil Procedure art. 203). Witness statements are assessed together with the gap between the price and the market value, the parties’ relationship, transactions after the transfer, medical records and, where necessary, a report of the Forensic Medicine Institute. If there is no other evidence, the oath should be relied on expressly.

What happens if the property has been sold to someone else?

A third party who acquired the property relying on the land register in good faith is protected (Civil Code art. 1023); a person who knew or ought to have known of the wrongful registration is not, and cancellation may also be sought against that person. Where the property remains with a person in good faith, compensation is claimed from the deceiver. To prevent a sale during the proceedings, an interim injunction should be sought and annotated in the land register.

Can the heirs bring a fraud action if the seller has died?

Yes, but the action must be brought on behalf of the estate and, as a rule, by all the heirs together or with the consent of the other heirs; if the heirs cannot agree, the civil court of peace may be asked to appoint a representative for the community of heirs (Civil Code art. 640). The Court of Cassation held that an action based on fraud and brought only in proportion to the claimant’s inheritance share could not be heard.

Is it mandatory to apply to a mediator before filing suit?

No. An action for annulment of title and re-registration based on fraud is not among the disputes in which mediation is a precondition (Law No. 6325 art. 18/B). However, mediation is a precondition if a sum of money or compensation is claimed in a commercial case between merchants; the parties may also choose to resolve a dispute over the transfer of property through voluntary mediation.
