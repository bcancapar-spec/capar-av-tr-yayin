Recourse in Turkish Traffic Insurance: Claims Against Insured and Driver, Exclusive Causation, Limitation

Short answer: Under Turkish law, a traffic insurer cannot raise against the injured party circumstances arising from the insurance contract that remove or reduce the compensation; it pays and may then turn to the policyholder to the extent that it could have had the compensation removed or reduced (Highway Traffic Law art. 95). The recourse grounds are listed in clause B.4 of the General Conditions, and the version in force on the policy date applies; an intoxicated or unlicensed driver and unlicensed passenger carriage are among them. For alcohol, the Court of Cassation requires the insurer to prove that the accident was caused solely by the effect of alcohol, with no other factor; for unlicensed driving, the decisions examined did not require such exclusive causation, and the recoverable amount was limited to the driver’s fault ratio and the injured party’s actual loss. A claim against a driver who is not a party to the policy does not rest on the insurance contract; subrogation (Commercial Code art. 1472) comes into play, and the Court of Cassation based the driver’s liability on tort. The right of recourse becomes time-barred two years from the day the insurer fully performed its obligation and learned whom to claim against (Highway Traffic Law art. 109/4).

This article explains Turkish law. It is an English translation of the Turkish original; in case of any discrepancy, the Turkish text prevails.

In brief

Recourse in Turkish compulsory motor third-party liability (traffic) insurance: the paying insurer’s claim against the policyholder under Highway Traffic Law art. 95, matters outside cover (art. 92), the recourse grounds in clause B.4 of the General Conditions and the version in force on the policy date, exclusive causation and the burden of proof for alcohol and unlicensed passenger carriage, unlicensed drivers, fault ratio and actual loss, claims against the driver and subrogation (Commercial Code art. 1472), the competent court and the two-year limitation period for recourse (Highway Traffic Law art. 109), with decisions of the Court of Cassation’s General Assembly of Civil Chambers, 4th Civil Chamber and former 17th Civil Chamber.

A guide to the insurer’s right of recourse in Turkish compulsory motor third-party liability insurance (traffic insurance, ZMSS): why defences arising from the insurance contract cannot be raised against the injured party and how the paying insurer may turn to the policyholder under Highway Traffic Law art. 95, matters outside cover (Highway Traffic Law art. 92), the recourse grounds in clause B.4 of the General Conditions (alcohol, unlicensed driving, unlicensed passenger carriage, leaving the scene) and the importance of the version in force on the policy date, exclusive (sole) causation and the burden of proof, recourse limited to the fault ratio and actual loss, claims against the driver and subrogation (Commercial Code art. 1472), the competent court, the two-year limitation period for recourse (Highway Traffic Law art. 109), and verified decisions of the Court of Cassation’s General Assembly of Civil Chambers, 4th Civil Chamber and former 17th Civil Chamber.

What Is Recourse in Traffic Insurance?

Operators of motor vehicles must take out compulsory financial liability insurance (commonly called traffic insurance) to cover their legal liability arising from the operation of their vehicles (Highway Traffic Law art. 91/1). The purpose of this insurance is to secure compensation for third parties injured in an accident.

Statutory Provision — Highway Traffic Law (Law No. 2918) art. 91/1 (Obligation to take out financial liability insurance) (unofficial translation)

“Operators are obliged to take out financial liability insurance in order to ensure that their liability under the first paragraph of article 85 of this Law is covered.”

This protection has two sides. Circumstances arising from the insurance contract or related statutory provisions that remove or reduce the compensation — for example, the driver being intoxicated or unlicensed — cannot be raised against the injured party; the insurer pays. An insurer that has paid may, however, turn to the policyholder in accordance with the contract and the relevant statutory provisions, to the extent that it could have obtained the removal or reduction of the compensation (Highway Traffic Law art. 95). In traffic insurance, recourse is the insurer’s right of claim in this internal relationship.

Statutory Provision — Highway Traffic Law (Law No. 2918) art. 95 (Circumstances resulting in the reduction or removal of compensation) (unofficial translation)

“Circumstances arising from the insurance contract or from statutory provisions relating to the insurance contract which result in the removal of the obligation to pay compensation or in the reduction of its amount cannot be raised against the injured party. An insurer that has made payment may have recourse against the policyholder, in accordance with the insurance contract and the statutory provisions relating to that contract, to the extent that it could have obtained the removal or reduction of the compensation.”

The Court of Cassation’s General Assembly of Civil Chambers reads Highway Traffic Law art. 95 in the same way: the insurer cannot raise circumstances removing or reducing the compensation against the injured party but, after payment, may have recourse against the policyholder under the contract and the statutory provisions (Court of Cassation General Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2017/1713, K. 2021/1042). The right of recourse is therefore conditional on payment: the insurer first pays the injured party and then turns to its insured. Nothing changes for the injured party; the economic burden of the recourse ground is shifted to the policyholder in the internal relationship.

Matters Outside Cover (Highway Traffic Law art. 92) vs. Recourse Grounds

Recourse must not be confused with matters outside the cover. Claims listed in Highway Traffic Law art. 92 fall outside the scope of compulsory financial liability insurance and are not met by traffic insurance. Highway Traffic Law art. 95, by contrast, governs defences arising from the contract or related statutory provisions in respect of a loss that is within the cover.

Statutory Provision — Highway Traffic Law (Law No. 2918) art. 92 (Matters outside compulsory financial liability insurance) (unofficial translation)

“The following matters are outside the scope of compulsory financial liability insurance: a) claims that the operator may bring against persons for whose acts it is held liable under this Law, … e) damage to goods carried in the motor vehicle, f) claims for non-pecuniary damages. g) claims of the right-holder corresponding to his or her own fault, h) claims of interested parties that do not fall within the insured’s liability risk, …”

The first question in any dispute is therefore whether the claim falls within the cover. If it does, the insurer cannot rely on the recourse grounds of the General Conditions against the injured party; those grounds become relevant only in a recourse claim against the policyholder after payment.

Recourse Grounds: Clause B.4 of the General Conditions and the Policy Date

Highway Traffic Law art. 95/2 ties the measure of recourse to “the insurance contract and the statutory provisions relating to that contract”. According to the General Assembly of Civil Chambers, the cases in which the insurer may have recourse against its insured are listed in clause B.4 of the General Conditions of Compulsory Financial Liability Insurance for Motor Vehicles in force on the date the policy was issued and the action was filed (Court of Cassation General Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2017/1713, K. 2021/1042). Because the General Conditions are published in the Official Gazette and have been replaced over time, they are not reproduced here as statutory text; the recourse grounds are described only as quoted in Court of Cassation decisions.

Important: the applicable text is the version of the General Conditions in force on the policy date. In a decision upheld by the Court of Cassation, the first-instance court and the regional court of appeal held that the General Conditions that entered into force on 01.06.2015 could not be applied to a 2006 policy and that the 2003 text applicable on the policy date did not list a missing alcohol report as a recourse ground; only the defendant insured appealed against that judgment, on jurisdiction and attorney’s fees (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2022/5871, K. 2024/10214). Clause letters also change from version to version: the alcohol ground appears as B.4.d in an older decision (General Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2013/626, K. 2014/146), while a 2024 decision places unlicensed driving under B.4/b and alcohol under B.4/c (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2021/26622, K. 2024/2700); in the General Assembly’s 2021 decision, unlicensed driving rests on B.4-c (General Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2017/1713, K. 2021/1042). Unlicensed passenger carriage likewise appears as B.4.e in the text in force on the accident date and as B.4.ç in the text relied on by the insurer (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2024/10751, K. 2025/17101). The text in force on the policy date must therefore be examined in every file.

Driver under the influence of alcohol

Driving under the influence of alcohol is one of the recourse grounds in the General Conditions; in this case, however, the Court of Cassation requires the insurer to prove that the accident occurred solely under the influence of alcohol (General Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2013/626, K. 2014/146). This is discussed further below.

Unlicensed driver

Use of the vehicle by a person without the required driving licence is also a recourse ground. In a first-instance judgment upheld by the Court of Cassation in 2024, clause B.4/b was described as covering the vehicle being driven by persons who do not hold the required licence, who hold a driver’s certificate that has lost its validity, or whose licence has been temporarily or permanently confiscated (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2022/4679, K. 2024/7627).

Unlicensed passenger carriage and load limits

The General Conditions also provide a recourse ground where the event giving rise to compensation results from carrying passengers in a vehicle not licensed for passenger transport, or from carrying passengers or goods in excess of the permitted capacity. The 4th Civil Chamber requires, in this case too, that the accident occurred exclusively as a result of the unlicensed carriage of passengers (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2024/10751, K. 2025/17101).

Leaving the scene and the duty to obtain documents

Clause B.4(f) of the General Conditions that entered into force on 01.06.2015 listed as a recourse ground, in accidents causing bodily injury, leaving the scene other than in cases of necessity such as medical treatment or personal safety, or breaching the duty to have documents such as the accident report and the alcohol report drawn up; in the decision upheld by the Court of Cassation, the lower courts held this clause inapplicable to a 2006 policy (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2022/5871, K. 2024/10214). The former 17th Civil Chamber, in a decision concerning a 2009 accident, held with respect to an insured who later gave a statement to the police stating that he was fully at fault that “merely not being present at the scene does not give the insurer a right of recourse against its own insured” (translation) (former 17th Civil Chamber, E. 2016/5264, K. 2019/1876).

Exclusive (Sole) Causation and the Burden of Proof

A decisive question in recourse actions is the causal link between the recourse ground and the accident. For alcohol, the General Assembly of Civil Chambers held that, for the insurer to recover from its insured the compensation it paid, the accident must have occurred solely under the influence of alcohol, without any other contributing factor. The Chamber judgment it endorsed stated that the burden of proof lies with the insurer and that the blood-alcohol level is not directly decisive, and required a report from an expert panel including a neurologist and a traffic expert to determine whether another vehicle striking the insured vehicle had contributed to the accident (Court of Cassation General Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2013/626, K. 2014/146).

The exclusivity requirement is not limited to alcohol. In a recourse enforcement based on unlicensed passenger carriage, the Forensic Medicine Institute report had found the insured driver primarily at fault, the other driver secondarily at fault and the passenger slightly at fault for his own injury; the 4th Civil Chamber held that the accident did not result exclusively from the unlicensed carriage of passengers and reversed the judgment dismissing the insured’s action for a declaration of non-liability (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2024/10751, K. 2025/17101). In practice, the insurer must show through technical expert reports that factors such as the other vehicle’s fault or a collision by another vehicle played no part in the accident.

Causation in unlicensed driving

In the decisions examined here, exclusivity was not required for unlicensed driving. In the file before the General Assembly of Civil Chambers, the lower court’s judgment of resistance, recalling that the Court of Cassation requires a causal link in drink-driving cases, argued that a certificate holder’s failure to obtain a licence could not be treated as fault; the Assembly did not accept that reasoning and held that the insurer has a right of recourse where the vehicle is driven without a driving licence (Court of Cassation General Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2017/1713, K. 2021/1042). Although the heirs of the insured owner argued that there was no causal link between the lack of a licence and the accident, a first-instance judgment holding that recourse requires the driver to be unlicensed and may then be claimed in proportion to fault, and allowing recourse in respect of a fully at-fault driver, was likewise upheld (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2022/4679, K. 2024/7627). In unlicensed-driving cases too, the recoverable amount is limited to the driver’s fault ratio.

The Recoverable Amount: Fault Ratio and Actual Loss

Highway Traffic Law art. 95/2 allows the insurer to claim to the extent that it could have obtained the removal or reduction of the compensation. The 4th Civil Chamber applies this standard by determining the recoverable amount according to the fault ratio of the insured vehicle’s driver and the actual loss of the injured parties. In an accident involving a driver who was both intoxicated and unlicensed, the regional court of appeal had dismissed on the merits the appeal against a first-instance dismissal, on the ground that it could not be established whether the accident was caused solely by alcohol; the Chamber held that, since it was established that the driver was intoxicated and unlicensed, the scope of recourse had to be determined, and reversed the judgment (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2021/26622, K. 2024/2700).

This standard means that the amount the insurer actually paid to the injured party is not automatically the measure of the recourse claim. The former 17th Civil Chamber likewise required that, in the recourse of medical expenses, the actual loss be calculated on the basis of a report from a medical expert, and held it wrong to award enforcement-denial compensation against the defendant because the claim was not liquidated, its amount requiring adjudication. The same decision stressed that, where the driver has no licence at all or an inadequate one, compensation should be awarded in proportion to the driver’s fault (former 17th Civil Chamber, E. 2016/5264, K. 2019/1876).

Recourse Against the Driver and Subrogation (Commercial Code art. 1472)

Highway Traffic Law art. 95/2 grants the right of recourse against the policyholder. Where the vehicle was driven by a person who is not a party to the policy, the insurer’s claim against that driver rests not on the insurance contract but on a different legal basis. The Commercial Code provides that an insurer that pays the indemnity is subrogated, up to the amount paid, to the insured’s rights against those responsible (Commercial Code art. 1472/1).

Statutory Provision — Turkish Commercial Code (Law No. 6102) art. 1472/1 (Subrogation) (unofficial translation)

“(1) When the insurer pays the insurance indemnity, it legally takes the place of the insured. If the insured has a right of action against those responsible for the loss that occurred, that right passes to the insurer up to the amount indemnified. Where an action or enforcement proceeding has been commenced against those responsible, the insurer may, without the approval of the court or of the other party, continue the action or proceeding from where it stands under the rule of subrogation by proving the payment made to its insured.”

In a negative declaratory action concerning enforcement proceedings that the insurer had commenced “as successor of its insured” against both the insured owner of the vehicle and the person alleged to have been the driver, for loss-of-support compensation paid after a fatal accident, the 4th Civil Chamber stated that the driver was not the insurer’s insured, that there was no insurance contract between them and that the driver’s liability rested on tort. According to the Chamber, since this relationship is not commercial, the mandatory mediation precondition for commercial cases (Commercial Code art. 5/A) cannot apply to that action (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2025/10883, K. 2025/15619).

In the same file, the first-instance court had taken the view that Highway Traffic Law art. 95/2 allows the insurer to claim only against the party to the insurance contract; the Chamber, however, confined its reversal to the mediation issue and did not examine the other grounds of appeal (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2025/10883, K. 2025/15619). The merits of a claim against the driver must therefore be assessed separately according to the circumstances of the case and the driver’s fault. For the calculation of loss-of-support compensation in fatal accidents, see our article on damages in fatal traffic accidents.

Competent Court and Procedural Preconditions

In a recourse action brought by the insurer against the insured, the competent court may depend on whether the insured qualifies as a consumer. The 4th Civil Chamber found it correct that a recourse action was heard by a consumer court where the defendant insured qualified as a consumer under Law No. 6502 on Consumer Protection and the insurance contract was a consumer transaction, and stressed that that Law’s provisions on jurisdiction and venue apply even where other laws contain provisions. The assessment of jurisdiction was based on the rules in force on the date the action was filed (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2022/5871, K. 2024/10214).

If the insured is not a consumer, the competent court is determined under the general rules; the recourse actions underlying the General Assembly decisions cited here were heard by commercial courts of first instance (General Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2013/626, K. 2014/146; General Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2017/1713, K. 2021/1042). For a claim against a driver who is not a party to the policy, the Court of Cassation characterised the relationship as tort-based and non-commercial (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2025/10883, K. 2025/15619). Before filing suit, it should also be checked whether a mediation precondition applies, depending on the nature of the dispute.

Limitation in Recourse Actions (Highway Traffic Law art. 109)

Alongside its general limitation rule for claims for pecuniary damage arising from motor vehicle accidents, the Highway Traffic Law lays down a separate rule for recourse: the rights of recourse of persons liable to pay compensation in motor vehicle accidents against one another are time-barred two years from the day on which they fully performed their own obligations and learned of the person against whom recourse is to be had (Highway Traffic Law art. 109/4).

Statutory Provision — Highway Traffic Law (Law No. 2918) art. 109 (Limitation) (unofficial translation)

“Claims for compensation of pecuniary damage arising from motor vehicle accidents are time-barred two years from the date on which the injured party learned of the damage and of the person liable to pay compensation and, in any event, ten years from the day of the accident. If the action arises from an act requiring criminal punishment and the criminal law prescribes a longer limitation period for that act, that period also applies to claims for pecuniary damages. … In motor vehicle accidents, the rights of recourse of persons liable to pay compensation against one another are time-barred two years from the day on which they fully performed their own obligations and learned of the person against whom recourse is to be had.”

For claims arising from an insurance contract, the Commercial Code provides a general two-year period running from the date the receivable becomes due, while reserving provisions in other laws (Commercial Code art. 1420).

Statutory Provision — Turkish Commercial Code (Law No. 6102) art. 1420 (Limitation) (unofficial translation)

“(1) All claims arising from an insurance contract are time-barred two years from the date the receivable becomes due and, subject to article 1482, claims relating to the insurance indemnity and the sum insured are in any event time-barred six years from the date on which the risk materialised. (2) Provisions in other laws are reserved.”

In an action concerning a traffic insurer’s recourse against its insured because the driver was intoxicated, the regional court of appeal held that limitation in recourse actions starts with payment and that the limitation defence raised in time had to be accepted under Highway Traffic Law art. 109/4 for an action filed more than two years after payment, and dismissed the appeal. The insurer appealed on points of law, arguing that the extended criminal limitation period should apply because the accident was fatal; the 4th Civil Chamber found the limitation assessment appropriate and upheld the judgment (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2022/12393, K. 2025/8608). For the insurer, the practical consequence is that recourse should be pursued without delay after payment to the injured party; for the insured, the payment date is the starting point of the limitation defence, which must be raised in time.

Court of Cassation Decisions

In the General Assembly decisions cited here, the Chamber that issued the reversal, as well as the 2019 Chamber decision, was the 17th Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation; the 2024–2025 Chamber decisions are decisions of the 4th Civil Chamber. Recent decision texts refer to the former as the “Court of Cassation (Abolished) 17th Civil Chamber” (General Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2017/1713, K. 2021/1042). In one recourse file, the 2020 reversal was issued by the (abolished) 17th Civil Chamber and the judgment rendered after the reversal was reviewed in 2025 by the 4th Civil Chamber (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2024/10751, K. 2025/17101). The case law of the former 17th Civil Chamber and the current decisions of the 4th Civil Chamber should therefore be read together; the details of the allocation of work are outside the scope of this article.

Recourse for alcohol requires the accident to have occurred solely under the influence of alcohol; the burden of proof is on the insurer

Court of Cassation Decision
General Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2013/626, K. 2014/146, 26.02.2014 (the Chamber’s reversal reasoning was endorsed by the General Assembly and the judgment of resistance was reversed; majority vote)

“Under clause B.4.d of the ZMSS general conditions and clause 4.c of the İMSS general conditions applicable between the parties, for the insurer to be able to claim from its own insured the compensation it paid for the other vehicle involved in the accident, the insured or its driver must have caused the accident solely under the influence of alcohol. The burden of proving such a situation lies with the insurer. For this reason, the level of alcohol consumed by the driver is not, in itself, directly decisive of the outcome. … for the plaintiff insurer to be able to recover from its insured the compensation it paid, the accident must have occurred solely under the influence of alcohol, without any other contributing factor” (translation)

A certificate holder who has not obtained a driving licence counts as unlicensed; the insurer has a right of recourse

Court of Cassation Decision
General Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2017/1713, K. 2021/1042, 21.09.2021 (reasoning of the General Assembly of Civil Chambers; judgment of resistance reversed; unanimous)

“clause B.4, headed ‘Preservation of the rights of injured parties and the insurer’s recourse against the operator’, of the General Conditions of Compulsory Financial Liability Insurance for Motor Vehicles (Traffic Insurance) in force on the date the policy was issued and the action was filed lists the cases in which the insurer may have recourse against its insured … In that case, even if the driver holds a certificate and is entitled to obtain a licence, a driver who drives during the period before obtaining the licence is in the position of a person driving without a driving licence, and the insurer has a right of recourse against its insured for the damage paid to third parties as a result of an accident occurring at that time.” (translation)

The recoverable amount depends on the driver’s fault ratio and the injured party’s actual loss

Court of Cassation Decision
4th Civil Chamber, E. 2021/26622, K. 2024/2700, 12.03.2024 (the Chamber’s reversal reasoning)

“Under clauses B.4/b and c of the Compulsory Financial Liability Insurance General Conditions, compensation paid to a third party as a result of an accident occurring where the driver does not hold a valid and adequate driving licence and where the vehicle is driven by persons who have consumed alcohol may be claimed from the insured by way of recourse. The amount of compensation for which the insurer may have recourse is determined according to the fault ratio of the insured vehicle’s driver and the actual loss of the injured parties. In the present case it is established that on 22.04.2008 the driver of the vehicle insured with the plaintiff was intoxicated and unlicensed. In that situation, the court should have determined the scope of the recourse and ordered the defendant to pay compensation; deciding as it did was not found correct” (translation)

A recourse action against an insured who qualifies as a consumer is heard by the consumer court

Court of Cassation Decision
4th Civil Chamber, E. 2022/5871, K. 2024/10214, 24.10.2024 (the Chamber’s reasoning in upholding the judgment)

“under article 3/l of Law No. 6502 on Consumer Protection in force on the date the action was filed, the defendant qualifies as a consumer and the contract between the parties is a consumer transaction; under article 73/1 of the same Law, actions arising from consumer transactions are heard by consumer courts; and article 83/2 of the Law provides that the provisions of Law No. 6502 on jurisdiction and venue apply even where other laws contain provisions” (translation)

The limitation period for recourse starts with payment and is two years (Highway Traffic Law art. 109/4)

Court of Cassation Decision
4th Civil Chamber, E. 2022/12393, K. 2025/8608, 27.05.2025 (finding of the regional court of appeal; upheld by the Chamber)

“since in recourse actions limitation starts with payment … the plaintiff insurer made the payment to the third party who suffered bodily injury in the accident on 06.05.2013 and filed this recourse action on 29.02.2016, the defendant raised the limitation defence in time, and, having regard to article 109/4 of the KTK, the two-year limitation period had expired so that the defendant’s limitation defence had to be accepted … the assessment made on limitation being appropriate, the decision is in accordance with procedure and law” (translation)

Frequently Asked Questions

When can a traffic insurer claim back from its insured the compensation it paid?

The insurer cannot raise against the injured party circumstances arising from the contract that remove or reduce the compensation, and it pays; after payment it may claim against the policyholder to the extent that it could have obtained the removal or reduction of the compensation (Highway Traffic Law art. 95). The recourse grounds are listed in clause B.4 of the General Conditions (General Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2017/1713, K. 2021/1042); in a decision upheld by the Court of Cassation, the lower courts applied the version of the General Conditions in force on the policy date (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2022/5871, K. 2024/10214). An intoxicated or unlicensed driver and unlicensed passenger carriage are among these grounds.

If the driver was intoxicated, can the insurer always claim recourse?

No. According to the General Assembly of Civil Chambers, recourse requires that the accident occurred solely (exclusively) under the influence of alcohol, without any other contributing factor; the burden of proof is on the insurer and the blood-alcohol level alone does not decide the outcome. A report from an expert panel including a neurologist and a traffic expert was required for this determination (General Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2013/626, K. 2014/146).

In recourse for an unlicensed driver, must it also be proven that the accident resulted from the lack of a licence?

In the decisions examined here, unlike the alcohol ground, the Court of Cassation did not require exclusivity for unlicensed driving. The General Assembly of Civil Chambers recognised the insurer’s right of recourse for an accident caused with a vehicle driven by a certificate holder who had not obtained a driving licence (General Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2017/1713, K. 2021/1042). The recoverable amount is, however, limited to the driver’s fault ratio and the injured party’s actual loss (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2021/26622, K. 2024/2700).

Is leaving the scene of the accident a recourse ground in itself?

The former 17th Civil Chamber held that, for an insured who later gave a statement to the police admitting fault, merely not being present at the scene does not give the insurer a right of recourse (former 17th Civil Chamber, E. 2016/5264, K. 2019/1876). The General Conditions that entered into force on 01.06.2015 listed leaving the scene other than in cases of necessity, and breaching the duty to have documents such as the accident report and the alcohol report drawn up, as a separate recourse ground; in a decision upheld by the Court of Cassation, the lower courts held that this provision could not be applied to a 2006 policy (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2022/5871, K. 2024/10214).

Which court hears a recourse action?

Where the insured qualifies as a consumer under Law No. 6502, the Court of Cassation found it correct that the insurer’s recourse action was heard by a consumer court (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2022/5871, K. 2024/10214). If the insured is not a consumer, jurisdiction is determined under the general rules. For a claim against a driver who is not a party to the policy, the Court of Cassation based the driver’s liability on tort and did not treat the relationship as commercial (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2025/10883, K. 2025/15619).

What is the limitation period for a recourse action?

Under Highway Traffic Law art. 109/4, the rights of recourse of persons liable to pay compensation in motor vehicle accidents against one another are time-barred two years from the day on which they fully performed their own obligations and learned of the person against whom recourse is to be had. In a decision upheld by the Court of Cassation, it was accepted that, in an insurer’s recourse action against its insured, the period starts with payment to the injured party, and the request to apply the extended criminal limitation period did not affect the outcome (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2022/12393, K. 2025/8608).
