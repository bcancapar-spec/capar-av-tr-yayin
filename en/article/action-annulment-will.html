Annulment of a Will in Türkiye: Requirements, Formal Rules and Court of Cassation 7th Civil Chamber Precedents

Short answer: Under Turkish law, a will can be annulled only on one of the four grounds exhaustively listed in TCC Art. 557: lack of testamentary capacity; mistake, fraud, threat or duress; illegality or immorality of its content, conditions or burdens; and failure to observe the statutory form. These defects do not render the will void automatically; an heir or legatee with an interest in its annulment must bring an action for annulment before the civil court of first instance at the decedent’s last domicile (TCC Art. 558, TCC Art. 576). The right of action lapses one year after the claimant learns of the disposition, the ground for annulment and their own entitlement, and in any event ten years after the opening of the will against defendants in good faith and twenty years against defendants in bad faith; invalidity may, however, always be raised as a defence (TCC Art. 559). According to the Grand General Assembly of Civil Chambers of the Court of Cassation, formal defects in an official will that stem from the acts of the official may be assessed under the principle of interpretation in favour of the will, but a will not drawn up by an official or made without the participation of two witnesses cannot be saved in this way.

This article explains Turkish law. It is an English translation of the Turkish original; in case of any discrepancy, the Turkish text prevails.

In brief

An action for annulment of a will seeks to set aside a testamentary disposition that reflects the decedent's final wishes where it is tainted by incapacity, a defect of intent, illegality or immorality, or a formal defect. Drawing on Articles 502, 531, 532, 536, 538, 557, 558, 559 and 576 of the Turkish Civil Code, this article examines testamentary capacity, the forms of a will, the statutory grounds for annulment, standing, the competent court, the preclusive periods, and recent leading decisions of the Grand General Assembly of Civil Chambers and the 7th Civil Chamber of the Court of Cassation.

Requirements for annulling a will: testamentary capacity (TCC Art. 502), the official, holographic and oral forms of a will (Arts. 531, 532, 536, 538), the four statutory grounds for annulment under TCC Art. 557 (incapacity, defect of intent, illegality or immorality, and formal defect), standing (Art. 558), the competent court and the Court of Cassation's chamber assignment (Art. 576), the preclusive periods (Art. 559), proof, and the distinction from an abatement action.

What Is a Will (Testamentary Disposition)?

A will is a testamentary disposition by which a person (the decedent) makes a unilateral declaration of intent, to take effect after death, concerning property and certain personal matters. The law divides testamentary dispositions into two categories: the will, made unilaterally and freely revocable at any time (TCC Art. 531 et seq.), and the inheritance contract, a binding bilateral instrument (TCC Art. 545 et seq.). A will may be altered or revoked at any time during the decedent's lifetime without any need to state a reason; it becomes binding only upon death.

Because a will reflects the decedent's final wishes, the law attaches great importance to its validity: it requires a specific capacity in the testator and strict compliance with formal rules for the formation of the testator's intent. A defect in either respect does not render the will void automatically; it must be set aside by an action for annulment brought by an heir or legatee with an interest in doing so.

Testamentary Capacity

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code (Law No. 4721) Art. 502 (unofficial translation)

“To make a will, a person must have the capacity of discernment and have completed the age of fifteen.”

Capacity of discernment is the ability to act reasonably and to appreciate the meaning and consequences of one's acts; its absence — whether from youth, mental illness, mental deficiency, intoxication or similar causes — gives rise to incapacity. It should be stressed that, under the Court of Cassation's settled case law, a will made by an incapable person is not automatically void; it loses effect only through an annulment action brought by an interested party.

Forms of a Will

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code Art. 531 (unofficial translation)

“A will may be made in official form, in the testator's own handwriting, or orally.”

Official will

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code Art. 532 (unofficial translation)

“An official will is drawn up by an official in the presence of two witnesses. The official may be a magistrate, a notary, or another officer empowered by law for this purpose.”

In practice, official wills are most often executed before a notary. The law provides separate procedures for literate and illiterate testators; as emphasised in a Grand General Assembly of Civil Chambers decision examined below, for an illiterate testator the officer must personally read the will aloud before the witnesses, and the witnesses must state in the will that they personally witnessed that reading. The law also strictly limits who may act as officer or witness, and to whom a benefit may be conferred:

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code Art. 536 (unofficial translation)

“Persons lacking capacity to act, persons banned from public service by a criminal judgment, illiterate persons, and the testator's spouse, ascendants, descendants, siblings and their spouses, may not take part in the execution of an official will as officer or witness. No benefit under that will may be conferred on the officer or witnesses who took part in its execution, or on their ascendants, descendants, siblings or spouses.”

This is commonly referred to in practice as the absolute participation bar; its breach — a disqualified person acting as witness or officer, or receiving a benefit through the will — is a formal defect frequently invoked in litigation.

Holographic (handwritten) will

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code Art. 538 (unofficial translation)

“A holographic will must state the year, month and day of its making and be written and signed, from beginning to end, entirely in the testator's own hand. It may be left, sealed or unsealed, with a notary, a magistrate, or a competent officer for safekeeping.”

The most common dispute concerning a holographic will is whether the handwriting and signature genuinely belong to the decedent; as shown in one of the decisions examined below, this is resolved through a forensic handwriting and signature examination by the Forensic Medicine Institute.

Oral will

Where extraordinary circumstances — imminent danger of death, severed communications, illness, war and the like — prevent the decedent from making an official or holographic will, he or she may resort to an oral will: relating final wishes to two witnesses and charging them with recording those wishes in writing. This is an exceptional remedy that lapses a certain time after the extraordinary circumstance ends.

Grounds for Annulment

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code Art. 557 (unofficial translation)

“An action for annulment of a testamentary disposition may be brought on the following grounds: 1. The disposition was made while the decedent lacked testamentary capacity; 2. The disposition resulted from mistake, fraud, duress or undue influence; 3. The content of the disposition, or the conditions or burdens attached to it, is unlawful or immoral; 4. The disposition was made without complying with the forms prescribed by law.”

The law confines the grounds for annulment to four, on a numerus clausus basis:

1. Testamentary incapacity

The decedent lacked capacity of discernment when the will was executed (Art. 502). This ground is usually invoked by reference to advanced age, dementia, impaired consciousness or mental illness, and is most often proved through a Forensic Medicine Institute report.

2. Defect of intent

The will resulted from mistake, fraud, duress or undue influence. Here the testator's capacity is intact, but the will was formed under an external vitiating influence.

3. Illegality or immorality

The content of the disposition, or the conditions or burdens attached to it, is unlawful or immoral.

4. Formal defect

The will was not made in compliance with the official, holographic or oral form prescribed by law. The Grand General Assembly decision examined below explains in detail when formal defects in an official will require annulment, and the limiting role played in that assessment by the principle of interpretation in favour of the will (favor testamenti).

An action for annulment may target the disposition in whole or only in part; where the defect stems from the participation, in drawing up the disposition, of persons to whom (or to whose spouses or relatives) a benefit is conferred, only that benefit — not the entire disposition — is annulled (Art. 558(3), below).

Standing: Who May Sue?

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code Art. 558 (unofficial translation)

“An action for annulment may be brought by an heir or legatee having an interest in the annulment of the disposition. The action may seek the annulment of the whole or only part of the testamentary disposition. Where the action rests on a defect arising from the participation, in drawing up the disposition, of persons to whom, to whose spouses or to whose relatives a benefit was conferred by the disposition, only that benefit — not the whole disposition — is annulled.”

Standing belongs to every heir (statutory or instituted) and every legatee with a legal interest in the annulment; the defendant is the person or persons to whom a benefit was conferred by the will. The claimant need not hold a reserved share: any heir whose statutory share would increase upon annulment has an interest in suing.

Competent Court and the Court of Cassation Chamber

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code Art. 576 (unofficial translation)

“Succession opens, for the entirety of the estate, at the decedent's domicile. Actions for the annulment or abatement of the decedent's dispositions, for partition of the estate, and for recovery of an inheritance shall be heard at the court of that domicile.”

The action for annulment of a will is heard by the civil court of first instance; venue lies exclusively with the court of the decedent's last domicile — a mandatory rule of venue under the Code.

The specialised chamber of the Court of Cassation has changed over time. In older decisions examined in this article, dating from 2014 to 2020, such actions were heard by the 3rd Civil Chamber; between 2020 and 2022 some files — particularly those combined with a claim for annulment of title based on a simulated transaction — came before the 1st Civil Chamber. Under the Grand General Assembly's decision of 26 June 2025, No. 2025/1, on the division of business among the chambers, jurisdiction over actions for annulment of a will and for abatement now lies with the 7th Civil Chamber; indeed, all of the 2025–2026 decisions on this subject were rendered by that Chamber. In one file, even after it emerged that the claim was in truth one for abatement rather than annulment, jurisdiction was held to remain with the 7th Civil Chamber. Where a claim for annulment of a will is combined with a claim for annulment of title based on a simulated transaction, however, the entire file may still come before the 1st Civil Chamber; which chamber has jurisdiction in a given file therefore depends on the nature of the claims asserted.

Preclusive Periods

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code Art. 559 (unofficial translation)

“The right to bring an action for annulment lapses one year from the date the claimant learns of the disposition, of the ground of annulment, and of his or her own entitlement, and in any event ten years — as against a defendant in good faith — or twenty years — as against a defendant not in good faith — from the date a will is opened, or, for other dispositions, from the date succession opens. Invalidity may nonetheless be raised at any time by way of defence.”

The period operates on two levels: a relative period of one year from the date the claimant learns of the disposition, the ground of annulment and his or her own entitlement; and an absolute (maximum) period — for wills, from the date the will is opened and read, and for other dispositions, from the date succession opens (death) — of ten years against a good-faith defendant and twenty years against a defendant not in good faith. In one 7th Civil Chamber decision examined below, the Chamber stated that the one-year period runs from the date the claimant learns of the will, the ground of annulment and his or her own entitlement, and in that case relied on the date on which the will was served on the claimant together with the claimant's statement at the hearing. In an earlier reversal in the same case, the same Chamber had run the period from the finality of the decision that the will had been “opened and read”, but it then affirmed the first-instance court's resisting judgment; the starting date should therefore be assessed on the particular facts of each case. By contrast, a plea of invalidity may be raised at any time by way of defence, regardless of the period — so even where the right to sue has lapsed, the same objection may still be asserted defensively in another action.

Proof

Under the general rule, the burden of proof lies on the claimant seeking annulment. In claims of incapacity, the principal evidence is usually a medical board report from the Forensic Medicine Institute; courts weigh the medical records closest in time to the will, witness testimony and, where necessary, a consolidated opinion of the Institute's plenary board. In holographic-will disputes, whether the handwriting and signature belong to the decedent is examined by the Forensic Medicine Institute's Physical Sciences Department against comparison samples of the decedent's handwriting and signature; as one decision below shows, relying solely on a signature examination without also investigating handwriting samples has been held to constitute an incomplete inquiry. In formal-defect claims, the text of the will itself — the officer's manner of drawing it up and the witnesses' statements — is decisive.

Court of Cassation Precedents

All decisions below were verified against their full texts in the official database; quotations are verbatim translations, omissions marked with …

Formal requirements for an illiterate testator's official will, and the principle of interpretation in favour of the will

Court of Cassation Decision
Grand General Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2025/218, K. 2026/290, 06.05.2026 (translation)

“…The principle of interpretation in favour of the will… This principle allows a will at risk of annulment to be upheld through interpretation. Its aim is to give effect to the decedent's final wishes… For this principle to apply, there must together exist: the decedent's genuine intention to make a will, the possibility of interpreting the will in different ways, and the ascertainability of the decedent's true intent… A formal defect arising because the official will was not drawn up by an official, or because two witnesses did not take part in the process, cannot undoubtedly be cured under the principle of favourable interpretation… That said, there is no obstacle whatsoever to assessing, under the principle of interpretation in favour of the will, formal defects in official wills that arise specifically from the acts of the official or his assistants.”

The Grand General Assembly held, by majority, that the absence in the will of a separate statement — required by Art. 535(2) for an illiterate testator — that the witnesses were themselves present when the testator made his declaration, does not invalidate the will where the whole of the instrument makes this clear, and that the claim for annulment should therefore be dismissed; it accordingly reversed the resisting judgment below.

Incapacity claim: affirmance of a dismissal based on consistent Forensic Medicine Board reports

Court of Cassation Decision (affirmed first-instance reasoning)
7th Civil Chamber, E. 2026/936, K. 2026/1107, 27.02.2026 (affirming the first-instance reasoning — translation)

“… annulment of the will was sought on the allegation that the testator lacked capacity to perform legal transactions on the date the will was drawn up, and the Forensic Medicine Board reports obtained before and after the Court of Cassation's reversal ruling consistently opined that, as of 14/05/2009, the date of the will, the deceased possessed capacity to act …”

The Chamber had earlier reversed because the first-instance court ruled without hearing the parties' witnesses and gathering the evidence; on remand, the first-instance court, complying with the reversal, again dismissed the action on the basis of the consistent Forensic Medicine Board reports obtained before and after the reversal, and the Chamber affirmed that judgment as consistent with its reversal ruling.

Whether the handwriting and signature on a holographic will belong to the decedent: incomplete inquiry and a dissent

Court of Cassation Decision (Chamber's earlier reversal, summarised)
7th Civil Chamber, E. 2026/431, K. 2026/1067, 26.02.2026 (affirmed by majority; dissenting opinion — translation)

“…since the claimants in the main and consolidated actions had stated that it was necessary to determine whether the holographic will of 15 July 2014 was in the decedent's own hand, the court should have obtained the decedent's comparison signature and handwriting samples from the relevant institutions and ruled accordingly; instead, relying solely on the Forensic Medicine Institute's Physical Sciences Department report — which examined only the signature — to conclude that the will was in the decedent's hand was an incomplete inquiry and was not correct…”

After the handwriting samples were also gathered on remand, the will was found to be in the decedent's hand and the decedent to have had capacity; the judgment was affirmed by majority, with a dissent taking the view that authorship of the handwriting had not been proved.

Commencement of the preclusive period: affirmance of a resisting judgment that ran the period from learning of the will through service

Court of Cassation Decision
7th Civil Chamber, E. 2026/304, K. 2026/694, 09.02.2026 (translation)

“Under the one-year preclusive period set out in Article 559 of Law No. 4721 for actions for annulment of a will and for abatement, in annulment actions the period runs from the date the claimant learns of the will, of the ground of annulment and of their own entitlement, and in abatement actions from the date the claimant learns that their reserved share was infringed; in the present case the will was served on the claimant on 5 August 2021, and the claimant having stated at the hearing that they became aware of the will upon its service and did not accept it, the preclusive period is found to have lapsed…”

In its earlier reversal, the Chamber had held that the one-year period ran from the finality of the magistrate's court decision that the will had been “opened and read”, and had directed the court to examine the merits. The first-instance court did not comply but resisted: holding that the will had been served on the claimant on 5 August 2021 and that the claimant had learned of its content on that date, as he also confirmed in his statement at the hearing, it again dismissed the action as time-barred. Relying on the date of service and the claimant's statement, the Chamber held that the period had lapsed, found the resisting judgment justified and unanimously affirmed it.

Grounds of annulment are exhaustive; infringement of the reserved share is a ground for abatement, not annulment (Assembly of Civil Chambers, 2026)

Court of Cassation, Assembly of Civil Chambers (the Assembly’s reasoning)
Yargıtay Hukuk Genel Kurulu, 2025/247 E., 2026/282 K., 29.04.2026

“Where the grounds listed in Article 557 of the Turkish Civil Code exist, the will must be annulled. Annulment of a disposition upon death cannot be sought on other grounds, but heirs with a reserved share may claim abatement where their reserved shares are infringed.” (unofficial translation)

In the same decision the Assembly characterised the action of claimants who alleged infringement of their reserved shares and also paid a proportional fee as one that included, alongside annulment of the will, an alternative claim for abatement, and reversed by majority the regional court’s insistence judgment that had examined the annulment claim only.

Annulment or Abatement?

In practice, claimants often seek annulment of a will and, in the alternative, its abatement. The two actions differ: an annulment action seeks to set aside a will, wholly or in part, for incapacity, defect of intent, illegality/immorality or formal defect, and may be brought by any interested heir, whether or not entitled to a reserved share; an abatement (tenkis) action, by contrast, reduces a formally and substantively valid will or lifetime gift only to the extent it infringes a reserved share, and may be brought only by heirs entitled to such a share. If an annulment action succeeds, the will (or the relevant part) is void from the outset; in an abatement action the will remains valid, and only the amount of the gift is reduced so as not to exceed the disposable portion. For a detailed treatment of the requirements, the reserved-share ratios and the method of calculation, see our article on Abatement (Tenkis) Actions.

Frequently Asked Questions

On what grounds may a will be annulled?

Article 557 lists four exhaustive grounds: testamentary incapacity; a defect of intent from mistake, fraud, duress or undue influence; unlawful or immoral content or burdens; and non-compliance with the statutory form.

Who may bring an action to annul a will?

Any heir with a legal interest in the annulment (a reserved share is not required) or any legatee. The defendant is the person or persons who benefited under the will.

Within what time must the action be brought?

One year from when the claimant learns of the will, the ground for annulment and his own entitlement; in any event ten years (good-faith defendant) or twenty years (bad-faith defendant) from the will's opening or the succession's opening. Invalidity may still be raised at any time as a defence.

Which court and Court of Cassation chamber hear the case?

The civil court of first instance at the decedent's last domicile. Since the Grand General Assembly's decision of 26 June 2025 (No. 2025/1), jurisdiction lies with the 7th Civil Chamber; files combined with a title-annulment/simulated-transaction claim may still reach the 1st Civil Chamber.

What makes a holographic will valid?

It must state the year, month and day, and be written and signed entirely in the decedent's own hand. Disputes over authorship are resolved by a Forensic Medicine Institute examination.

What is the difference between annulment and abatement?

Annulment sets aside a defective will in whole or part and may be sought by any interested heir. Abatement reduces only the portion of a valid disposition that exceeds the reserved share, and may be sought only by heirs entitled to that share.
