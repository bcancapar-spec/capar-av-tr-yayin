Action for Denial of Paternity in Turkish Law: Presumption, the Mother’s Right, Time Limits, DNA Testing

Short answer: Under Turkish law, the action for denial of paternity is the means of rebutting the presumption that the husband is the father of a child born during the marriage (Civil Code art. 285). Under Civil Code art. 286 as amended by Law No. 7531, it may be brought by the husband, the mother or the child against the other persons entitled to bring the action. The time limit is one year for each right-holder and runs for the husband from learning, for the mother from the birth and for the child from reaching majority; if the delay is based on a justified reason, the period starts when that reason ceases (Civil Code art. 289). According to the Court of Cassation, mere suspicion that the child is not his does not amount to learning; the period starts when learning occurs through a paternity test obtained outside the proceedings, and the judge takes the period into account ex officio. The judge investigates the facts ex officio and the parties and third persons must submit to DNA testing; in a paternity action, the Assembly of Civil Chambers held that coercive measures under Code of Civil Procedure art. 292 should be applied to a defendant who avoids testing so that a report can be obtained. The action is heard by the family court, and the defendant’s acceptance of the claim does not by itself produce a result.

This article explains Turkish law. It is an English translation of the Turkish original; in case of any discrepancy, the Turkish text prevails.

In brief

Action for denial of paternity under the Turkish Civil Code (arts. 282-291): the right of action granted to the husband, the mother and the child by Law No. 7531, the presumption of paternity and the burden of proof, the one-year time limits and the moment of “learning”, actions by other interested persons, the duty to submit to DNA testing (Civil Code art. 284, Code of Civil Procedure art. 292), appointment of a curator for the child, the limits of acceptance and waiver and the competent family court, with recent decisions of the Court of Cassation.

What Is the Action for Denial of Paternity? The Presumption of Paternity (art. 285)

Under the Turkish Civil Code, descent between a child and the mother is established by birth; descent between a child and the father is established by marriage to the mother, acknowledgment or a court judgment, and descent may also be established by adoption (art. 282). The father of a child born during the marriage is the husband by virtue of the statutory presumption of paternity. Where this legal tie contradicts biological reality, the means of removing it is the action for denial of paternity.

According to the Court of Cassation’s 2nd Civil Chamber, descent in the narrow sense is the biological tie between a person and his or her parents only; descent legally established through birth in wedlock and the presumption of paternity can be removed only by denial of paternity, and once the action succeeds the child acquires the status of a child without descent on the father’s side. Referring to a Constitutional Court decision, the Chamber also stressed that the right to repudiate a child whose genetic-biological origin is not one’s own is among a person’s most fundamental rights (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2022/1620, K. 2022/2754; rendered under the pre-amendment text).

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code (Law No. 4721) art. 285 (Presumption of paternity) (unofficial translation)

“The husband is the father of a child born during the marriage or within three hundred days of its termination. A child born after this period may be attributed to the husband only by proof that the mother became pregnant during the marriage. Where the husband has been declared absent, the three-hundred-day period begins to run from the date of the danger of death or of the last news.”

Conflict of presumptions: If the child is born within three hundred days of the end of the marriage and the mother has remarried in the meantime, the husband in the second marriage is deemed the father; if this presumption is rebutted, the husband in the first marriage is deemed the father (art. 290).

Right of Action: Husband, Mother and Child (art. 286) and the 2024 Amendment

Under art. 286, as amended by Law No. 7531 of 7 November 2024, the action for denial of paternity may be brought by the husband, the mother or the child, and it is brought against the other persons entitled to bring the action. Accordingly, the husband’s action is directed at the mother and the child, the mother’s action at the husband and the child, and the child’s action at the husband and the mother.

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code (Law No. 4721) art. 286 (Right of action) (unofficial translation)

“The husband, the mother or the child may rebut the presumption of paternity by bringing an action for denial of paternity. This action is brought against the other persons entitled to bring the action.”

Background to the amendment: The former text granted the right of action only to the husband and the child, providing that the husband’s action was brought against the mother and the child, and the child’s action against the mother and the husband. On a referral made in an action for denial of paternity brought by a mother, the Constitutional Court annulled the first paragraph and ruled that the annulment would enter into force nine months after its publication in the Official Gazette (Constitutional Court, E.2023/37, K.2023/140, 26.07.2023). In the Court’s view, a tie of descent between the child and a husband who is not the biological father also concerns the mother’s right to respect for private life, so the mother’s access to court to rebut the presumption of paternity is a requirement of the right to an effective remedy. Law No. 7531 then re-enacted art. 286 and added the mother’s time limit (one year from the birth) to the second paragraph of art. 289.

After the amendment, the 2nd Civil Chamber held that the mother’s earlier action of 2019, dismissed for lack of standing, does not constitute res judicata for the new action and that the merits must be examined after a representative curator has been appointed for the child (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2026/2257, K. 2026/4224; principle box below).

Right of Action of Other Interested Persons (art. 291)

If the husband dies, is declared absent or permanently loses the capacity to discern before his time limit expires, the right of action is granted to the person claiming to be the father and the husband’s descendants, mother or father; for a minor child, the action is brought by the curator appointed for the child.

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code (Law No. 4721) art. 291 (Right of action of other interested persons) (unofficial translation)

“Where, before the period for bringing the action expires, the husband dies, is declared absent or permanently loses the capacity to discern, the person claiming to be the father, the husband’s descendants, mother or father may bring an action for denial of paternity within one year of learning of the birth and of the husband’s death, permanent loss of capacity to discern or declaration of absence. The curator to be appointed for a minor child shall bring the action for denial of paternity within one year, …, of service of the appointment decision on him or her. The provisions on the action for denial of paternity brought by the husband apply by analogy.”

The gap marked … in the second paragraph corresponds to the “(…)” in the official text, where the phrase “and in any event five years from the birth” was annulled by the Constitutional Court (Constitutional Court, E.2013/62, K.2013/115, 10.10.2013). Despite the cross-reference by analogy in the third paragraph, the Court of Cassation held that, since a separate time limit and starting point are laid down for interested persons, the husband’s time-limit rules cannot be applied by analogy, and that the action of a descendant who failed to sue within one year of learning of his father’s death had to be dismissed (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2024/2993, K. 2024/2934; rendered under the pre-amendment text). The current art. 291/1 likewise contains its own one-year period and starting criterion for interested persons.

Time Limits and the Moment of Learning (art. 289)

The time limit is one year for every holder of the right, but the starting point differs: learning for the husband, the birth for the mother, and reaching majority for the child. If the delay rests on a justified reason, the period begins when that reason ceases to exist.

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code (Law No. 4721) art. 289 (Time limits) (unofficial translation)

“The husband must bring the action within one year, …, of learning of the birth and that he is not the father or that the mother had sexual relations with another man at the time she became pregnant. The mother must bring the action within one year at the latest from the birth, and the child within one year at the latest from reaching majority. … If the delay is based on a justified reason, the one-year period begins to run from the date on which that reason ceases to exist.”

The gap marked … in the first paragraph corresponds to the “(…)” in the official text, where the phrase “and in any event five years from the birth” applicable to the husband was annulled; in the same decision the Constitutional Court held that the remainder of the first paragraph and the third paragraph on justified reasons are not unconstitutional (Constitutional Court, E.2008/30, K.2009/96, 25.06.2009). The mother’s time limit in the second paragraph was added by Law No. 7531.

When does learning occur? This is the crux of the husband’s action. According to the Court of Cassation, mere suspicion is not sufficient for learning; where learning occurs through an out-of-court paternity test, the period starts at that moment. The action of a husband who voiced his suspicion in divorce proceedings but in which no DNA examination had been carried out was therefore treated as filed in time (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2025/1001, K. 2025/1163; 2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2022/1620, K. 2022/2754). Along the same lines, the date of a DNA report obtained privately by the claimant was taken as the moment of learning (earlier reversal in the file of 2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2023/1987, K. 2023/2378). The one-year period should therefore not be missed once a private test result is known.

If the period has expired and there is no justified reason, the action is dismissed. The Court of Cassation has stated that this period is a peremptory time limit (hak düşürücü süre) to be taken into account ex officio by the judge whether or not it is raised, and that, for a claimant alleging that he learned he was not the father one week before filing, the burden of proving whether a justified reason for the delay exists lies with the claimant (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2010/17813, K. 2010/19935; rendered under the pre-amendment text).

Burden of Proof: Time of Conception (arts. 287-288)

The claimant’s burden of proof depends on when the child was conceived. If the child was conceived during the marriage, the claimant must prove that the husband is not the father:

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code (Law No. 4721) art. 287 (Conception during marriage) (unofficial translation)

“If the child was conceived during the marriage, the claimant must prove that the husband is not the father. A child born at least one hundred and eighty days after the marriage and at most three hundred days after its termination is deemed to have been conceived during the marriage.”

If the child was conceived before the marriage or while the spouses were living apart, the claimant need not produce further evidence; but the presumption survives if there is convincing evidence of sexual relations between the spouses during the period of conception:

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code (Law No. 4721) art. 288 (Conception before marriage or during separation) (unofficial translation)

“If the child was conceived before the marriage or while the spouses were living apart, the claimant need not produce any other evidence. However, if there is convincing evidence that the husband had sexual relations with his wife during the period of conception, the presumption of the husband’s paternity remains valid.”

In practice the decisive evidence is genetic (DNA) examination. The Court of Cassation held that the court may not rely on a private DNA report submitted by the claimant but must refer the persons concerned to give samples at the same time and obtain a report from the Forensic Medicine Institute (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2023/1987, K. 2023/2378; rendered under the pre-amendment text).

Procedure: Ex Officio Investigation and the Duty to Submit to DNA Testing

In actions concerning descent, the procedural code applies subject to two special rules in art. 284: the judge investigates the material facts ex officio, and the parties and third persons are obliged to consent to necessary examinations that pose no danger to their health.

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code (Law No. 4721) art. 284 (Procedure) (unofficial translation)

“In actions concerning descent, the Code of Civil Procedure applies subject to the following rules: 1. The judge investigates the material facts ex officio and evaluates the evidence freely. 2. The parties and third persons are obliged to consent to investigations and examinations that are necessary to establish descent and pose no danger to their health. If the defendant does not consent to the investigation and examination ordered by the judge, the judge may, depending on the situation and circumstances, deem the expected result to have arisen against him or her.”

Art. 292 of the Code of Civil Procedure, in force since 01.10.2011, goes further: it imposes a duty to submit to the taking of blood or tissue and provides that, absent a justified reason, the examination is carried out by force:

Statutory Provision — Code of Civil Procedure (Law No. 6100) art. 292 (Examination for determining descent) (unofficial translation)

“(1) Provided that it is necessary for resolving the dispute, consistent with scientific data and poses no danger to health, everyone must submit to the taking of blood or tissue from his or her body for the purpose of determining descent. If this obligation is not complied with without a justified reason, the judge orders that the examination be carried out by force. (2) A third person may not avoid this obligation by claiming a right to refuse to testify.”

The relationship between the two provisions was addressed by the Assembly of Civil Chambers: the first-instance court had applied Civil Code art. 284/2 and deemed the result to have arisen against a defendant who avoided testing; the Assembly held by majority that, under the principle of immediate application, the later art. 292 of the Code of Civil Procedure governs and a report must be obtained by applying, where necessary, coercive measures including arrest, compulsory appearance and placement under observation (Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2022/33, K. 2023/377; rendered in a paternity action). Earlier, the Court of Cassation had reversed a judgment granted because the mother refused samples from the child, treating it as incomplete examination since the curator appointed for the children had not also been warned (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2011/17288, K. 2012/29701; rendered under the pre-amendment text).

Constitution of the Parties and Appointment of a Curator for the Child

The child is the defendant in an action brought by the husband or the mother and the claimant in his or her own action, and is often in a conflict of interest with the legal representative; under art. 426 of the Civil Code, the guardianship authority then appoints a representative curator. The Court of Cassation holds that the proper constitution of the parties is a matter of public order, that because of the conflict of interest between the minors and their mother as legal representative appointing a curator under art. 426/2 is mandatory, and that a judgment rendered against incomplete parties without the curator’s participation will be reversed (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2011/14358, K. 2012/28420; rendered under the pre-amendment text).

In an action brought by the mother on behalf of the child in her capacity as holder of custody, the Court of Cassation likewise held that an “error in representation” does not require dismissal; the parties must be completed on the claimant side by appointing a curator for the child and on the defendant side by joining the mother as defendant (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2022/3647, K. 2022/5385; rendered under the pre-amendment text). In the mother’s own action, too, the parties must be constituted by appointing a representative curator for the child (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2026/2257, K. 2026/4224). A minor child’s own action is brought by the curator, whose time limit runs from service of the appointment decision (art. 291/2).

Public Order: Limits of Acceptance and Waiver

Because descent concerns not only the parties but also public order, the effect of the parties’ procedural acts is limited:

Statutory Provision — Code of Civil Procedure (Law No. 6100) art. 308 (Acceptance of the claim) (unofficial translation)

“(1) Acceptance is the defendant’s consent, in part or in full, to the claimant’s request for relief. (2) Acceptance has effect only in actions concerning matters over which the parties may freely dispose.”

The Court of Cassation has likewise stated that in these actions the defendant’s acceptance of the claim does not by itself produce legal effect and that the judge investigates the material facts ex officio (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2008/5455, K. 2009/10721), and it reversed a judgment based solely on acceptance without investigation of blood and genetic findings (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2010/17813, K. 2010/19935). Both decisions were rendered under the repealed Code of Civil Procedure No. 1086 and the pre-amendment text.

Waiver, by contrast, is the claimant’s abandonment of the request for relief and produces effects like a final judgment (Code of Civil Procedure arts. 307 and 311). In an action for denial of paternity combined with a paternity action, the regional appellate court found that, while these actions concern public order, in the present action, which is strictly personal to the claimant, the waiver by the claimant’s attorney amounted to complete abandonment of the request for relief and dismissal on grounds of waiver was correct; the Court of Cassation upheld that decision (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2023/5633, K. 2023/5227; finding upheld on appeal).

Competent Court and Venue

Since descent is regulated in Book Two of the Civil Code (art. 282 et seq.), the action for denial of paternity falls within the jurisdiction of the family court under art. 4/1 of Law No. 4787.

Statutory Provision — Law No. 4787 on the Establishment, Duties and Procedures of Family Courts art. 4 (Duties of family courts) (unofficial translation)

“Family courts hear the following actions and matters: 1. Actions and matters arising from family law under Book Two, excluding Part Three, of the Turkish Civil Code No. 4721 of 22.11.2001 and under Law No. 4722 of 3.12.2001 on the Entry into Force and Application of the Turkish Civil Code, …”

The Court of Cassation holds that subject-matter jurisdiction is a matter of public order to be observed ex officio at every stage, and that where there is no family court the action must be heard by the civil court of first instance acting as a family court (2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2023/1978, K. 2023/2560). Venue lies with the court of the domicile of either party at the time of the action or of the birth (Civil Code art. 283).

Court of Cassation Decisions

The decisions below have been verified against their full texts in the official case-law database; they were rendered by the chambers competent under the division of work in force at the time. Decisions rendered before the amendment made by Law No. 7531 are flagged in the note.

The mother’s right of action: an earlier dismissal for lack of standing is not res judicata; a representative curator is appointed for the child

Court of Cassation Decision
2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2026/2257, K. 2026/4224, 15.04.2026 (the Chamber’s reversal reasoning; rendered after the amendment made by Law No. 7531)

“By the Constitutional Court’s decision of 26.07.2023 in file no. 2023/37, the first paragraph of Article 286 of Law No. 4721, which provided that an action for denial of paternity could not be brought by the mother, was annulled. Considering procedural economy, the right to a fair trial and the Constitutional Court’s annulment decision as a whole, it must be accepted that the action for denial of paternity brought by the claimant mother on 25.10.2019 and dismissed for lack of standing does not constitute res judicata for the present file and that the mother may bring this action. That being so, whereas the court should have ensured the appointment of a representative curator for the child, constituted the parties and then collected the evidence and decided on the merits, dismissing the action on the stated grounds was not found correct…” (translation)

Start of the time limit: suspicion is not learning

Court of Cassation Decision
2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2025/1001, K. 2025/1163, 10.02.2025 (the Chamber’s reversal reasoning; concerns the husband’s action, art. 289/1 was not amended by Law No. 7531)

“Settled Court of Cassation practice shows that paternity reports obtained outside the proceedings are taken into account, in particular for the period for bringing the action, which is a peremptory time limit, to start from the date of learning; that is, it is accepted that the peremptory time limit starts at the moment learning occurs through an out-of-court paternity test. Consequently, a suspicion that a child is not one’s own cannot be accepted as sufficient for learning.” (translation)

Action by other interested persons: the husband’s time-limit rules do not apply by analogy (art. 291)

Court of Cassation Decision
2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2024/2993, K. 2024/2934, 29.04.2024 (the Chamber’s reversal reasoning; rendered under the pre-amendment text)

“For a provision to be applied by analogy, there must be no express regulation on the matter. Since a separate peremptory time limit and starting date are provided for the action for denial of paternity to be brought by interested persons, the provisions on the peremptory time limit for the action brought by the husband cannot be applied by analogy.” (translation)

Defendant avoiding DNA testing: coercive measures under Code of Civil Procedure art. 292

Court of Cassation Decision
Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2022/33, K. 2023/377, 26.04.2023 (reasoning of the Assembly of Civil Chambers; rendered in a paternity action, by majority and before the 2024 amendment)

“Descent between a child and a father is a fundamental right and also concerns public order. Therefore, in actions concerning descent, the judge must investigate all material facts ex officio and make use of every means made available by scientific research in order to reach the correct result. … under the principle of immediate application, in order to reach the correct result, it is beyond doubt that, under Article 292 of the Code of Civil Procedure as the later law, a decision must be rendered on the basis of the report to be obtained by applying, where necessary, all coercive measures, including arrest, compulsory appearance and placement under observation, against a defendant who avoids giving blood and tissue samples, bearing in mind also that criminal complaints should be filed against officials who neglect the duty to use force.” (translation)

A private DNA report is not sufficient; a report must be obtained from the Forensic Medicine Institute

Court of Cassation Decision
2nd Civil Chamber, E. 2023/1987, K. 2023/2378, 11.05.2023 (the Chamber’s reversal reasoning; rendered under the pre-amendment text)

“Bearing in mind that in actions concerning descent the courts are obliged to create ... records without giving rise to any doubt or hesitation, the court should have referred the mother, the child whose descent is to be determined and the defendant alleged to be the father to give DNA samples together at the same time and obtained a report from the Forensic Medicine Institute; rendering judgment on incomplete examination by contenting itself with the report relied on as evidence by the claimant was not found ... .” (translation)

Frequently Asked Questions

Who can bring an action for denial of paternity?

Under art. 286 of the Civil Code, as amended by Law No. 7531, the husband, the mother or the child; the action is directed at the other persons entitled to bring it. If the husband dies, is declared absent or permanently loses the capacity to discern before his time limit expires, the person claiming to be the father and the husband’s descendants, mother or father may also sue (art. 291).

Within what time must the action be brought?

The husband must sue within one year of learning of the birth and that he is not the father or that the mother had relations with another man at the time of conception; the mother within one year from the birth; the child within one year from reaching majority. If the delay rests on a justified reason, the period starts when that reason ceases (art. 289). The Court of Cassation treats this period as a peremptory time limit observed by the judge ex officio.

Does my suspicion that the child is not mine start the time limit?

No. According to the Court of Cassation, suspicion is not sufficient for learning; where learning occurs through an out-of-court paternity test, the period starts at that moment. The one-year period should therefore not be missed once a private DNA result is known.

What happens if the other party does not attend the DNA test?

Under art. 284/2 of the Civil Code the judge may draw an adverse inference against a defendant who refuses the examination, while art. 292 of the Code of Civil Procedure provides that the examination is carried out by force if blood or tissue is refused without a justified reason. The Assembly of Civil Chambers held that a report must be obtained by applying coercive measures, including arrest and compulsory appearance where necessary, against a defendant who avoids testing.

Is a DNA report from a private laboratory sufficient for the case?

A private report may be important in fixing the moment of learning, but the Court of Cassation holds that judgment cannot rest solely on a report submitted by the claimant and that a report must be obtained from the Forensic Medicine Institute by referring the persons concerned at the same time.

If the defendant accepts the claim, will the court simply grant it?

No. Because descent concerns public order, acceptance does not by itself produce effect; the judge investigates the facts ex officio and orders a genetic examination (Civil Code art. 284; Code of Civil Procedure art. 308/2). A claimant’s waiver, by contrast, led to dismissal of the action in a decision upheld by the Court of Cassation.
