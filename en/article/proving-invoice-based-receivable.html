Proving an Invoice-Based Receivable in Türkiye: Is the Claim Real? (Delivery, Commercial Books, BA-BS Forms)

Short answer: An invoice is not the source of a receivable; the receivable arises from a sale, work or service contract, and the invoice alone does not prove it. For an invoice claim between commercial companies to be a real claim, the seller must prove that the goods were delivered or the service was performed. In decisions upheld by the Court of Cassation, delivery was accepted where the invoice was recorded in the buyer’s commercial books or notified by the buyer to the tax office on the BA form, whereas the seller’s reliance solely on its own books and BS notification was not found sufficient.

This article explains Turkish law. It is an English translation of the Turkish original; in case of any discrepancy, the Turkish text prevails.

Is the Invoice the Source of the Receivable?

No. The source of the receivable is the sale, work or service contract between the parties; the invoice is the document showing the price arising from that contract. In a sale, the seller undertakes to transfer possession and ownership of the goods and the buyer undertakes to pay the price (Code of Obligations art. 207). Therefore, when an invoice claim is disputed, the court examines whether the contract was concluded and whether the seller performed its own obligation, that is, whether the goods were delivered or the service was performed.

The eight-day objection rule (Commercial Code art. 21/2) is covered in our article The Legal Effect of an Invoice in Commercial Matters; in the upheld decision below it was emphasised that this rule does not mean that the goods in the invoice were delivered. For fake invoices and the tax dimension, see our article The Fraudulent Return Invoice.

Who Bears the Burden of Proof?

The general rule is that the party relying on a fact must prove it. For an invoice claim this means that the seller (contractor, service provider) proves the contract and its own performance, i.e. delivery or performance, while the buyer proves payment of the price. In an upheld decision this allocation was expressed as follows: in a sale, the burden of proving delivery lies with the seller and the burden of proving payment lies with the buyer. In a contract for work, too, the contractor must prove that the work was done and the employer must prove that the price was paid (Court of Cassation 15th Civil Chamber, E. 2017/23, K. 2018/2735).

Statutory Provision — Turkish Civil Code (Law No. 4721) art. 6 (Burden of proof) (unofficial translation)

“Unless the law provides otherwise, each party is obliged to prove the existence of the facts on which it bases its right.”

Statutory Provision — Code of Civil Procedure (Law No. 6100) art. 190/1 (Burden of proof) (unofficial translation)

“(1) Unless there is a special provision in the law, the burden of proof lies with the party deriving a right in its favour from the legal consequence attached to the alleged fact.”

How Is Delivery or Performance Proven?

The clearest evidence of delivery is a delivery note (sevk irsaliyesi) and a delivery report signed by the buyer’s authorised person. In an upheld decision, delivery was held proven by assessing together delivery notes whose delivery sections were signed and the buyer’s notification of the invoices on the BA form. By contrast, in a case where the buyer argued that its officers had not signed the delivery notes and the invoices appeared neither in the buyer’s books nor on its BA form, delivery was not proven and the action was dismissed. For invoices for works and services, the contractor must prove that the work was done.

What Do the Buyer’s Books, BA-BS Forms and e-Invoices Mean?

In disputes between merchants, the counterparty’s own records are often decisive. The BA form is the form by which the buyer reports its purchases, and the BS form the form by which the seller reports its sales, to the tax administration. In upheld decisions:

  • Where the invoice was recorded in the buyer’s commercial books or notified by the buyer on the BA form, delivery of the goods was accepted and the BA notification was treated as a presumption of purchase; the buyer must prove the contrary or payment.
  • A buyer that entered the invoice in its books and then argues that it was recorded “as an accommodation” and that it never received the goods must prove that defence itself.
  • The mere recording of the invoice in the seller’s books and its notification by the seller on the BS form do not by themselves prove the receivable.

The evidential value of commercial books is governed by Code of Civil Procedure art. 222; for the rules on evidence for and against the owner, see our article The Evidential Nature of Commercial Books.

VAT deduction: Sellers also argue that the buyer’s use of the invoice for VAT deduction proves delivery; in the decisions below, however, the decisive point was whether the invoice appeared in the buyer’s books and BA notification.

e-Invoice and e-archive: Issuing and transmitting an invoice electronically does not remove the need to prove delivery separately. In an upheld decision the seller argued that the invoices had been sent as e-invoices and had not been objected to; since the invoices appeared neither in the buyer’s books nor on its BA form, delivery was not proven.

Current account statement: A statement drawn from the seller’s own records essentially reflects the seller’s book entries. In an upheld decision concerning enforcement proceedings for an “invoiced current account receivable”, the claim was not proven as regards an invoice that was not recorded in the buyer’s books and whose acceptance was not proven.

What Must Be Proven in an Action for Annulment of Objection Based on Invoices?

If an objection is raised against enforcement proceedings without a judgment based on invoices, the creditor may file an action for annulment of the objection within one year of notification of the objection and must prove the existence of the receivable under the general rules. In the action, too, the invoice alone is not enough; delivery or performance and the buyer’s book and BA records are decisive. For the general framework of the action, see our article Action for Annulment of Objection.

Statutory Provision — Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law (Law No. 2004) art. 67 (Annulment of objection) (unofficial translation)

“A creditor whose enforcement request has been objected to may, within one year from the date of notification of the objection, apply to the court and sue for the annulment of the objection by proving the existence of its receivable under the general provisions. In this action, if the debtor’s objection is found unjustified, the debtor, or, if found unjustified and in bad faith in its proceedings, the creditor, shall, upon the request of the other party, be ordered to pay appropriate compensation of not less than twenty per cent of the amount rejected or adjudged, depending on the situation of both parties, the case and the burden of the adjudged matter.”

Enforcement denial compensation and liquidity: For this compensation the Court of Cassation requires, in addition to the debtor’s objection being unjustified, that the receivable be liquid: the debtor must be in a position to determine how much it owes; if determining the amount requires adjudication, the receivable is not liquid (Court of Cassation 4th Civil Chamber, E. 2017/198, K. 2019/4931). Even if the invoice states an amount, disputes over the quantity delivered, defects or set-off may require this condition to be assessed separately.

When Does Interest Start to Run?

Under the general rule, the debtor of a due debt is in default upon the creditor’s notice or, if the performance date was jointly fixed, upon the expiry of that date (Code of Obligations art. 117). In the upheld decision below, since no definite maturity date had been fixed between the parties, interest ran not from the invoice date but from the default date at the end of the period granted by notarial notice.

For the supply of goods and services between commercial enterprises, Commercial Code art. 1530 provides a special regime. That regime requires the creditor to have performed its supply obligation; here too, delivery or performance comes first. If the contract sets no payment date or period, the debtor is, as a rule, in default without notice at the end of thirty days following receipt of the invoice. As interest rates vary, no rate is given here; under Commercial Code art. 1530/7, where the contract sets no rate, the rate applicable under this article is announced by the Central Bank of the Republic of Türkiye every January.

Statutory Provision — Turkish Commercial Code (Law No. 6102) art. 1530/2-4 (Late payment in the supply of goods and services) (unofficial translation)

“(2) In transactions between commercial enterprises for the supply of goods and services, if the debtor fails to pay its debt on the date provided in the contract or within the stated payment period, even though the creditor has performed its supply obligation arising from law or contract, the debtor shall be in default without the need for notice, except in cases where it cannot be held liable for the delay. … (4) If no payment date or period is specified in the contract, or the specified period is contrary to the fifth paragraph, the debtor shall be deemed in default without the need for notice, and the creditor shall become entitled to interest, at the end of the following periods: a) At the end of thirty days following receipt by the debtor of the invoice or an equivalent payment request. …”

Court of Cassation Decisions

An invoice alone does not show a receivable; the seller’s book entry and BS notification are not enough

Court of Cassation Decision
6th Civil Chamber, E. 2022/1693, K. 2023/2515, 22.06.2023 (finding of the Regional Court of Appeal; upheld by the Chamber)

“that an invoice alone does not show the existence of a receivable, that the entry of the unaccepted invoice in the claimant’s commercial books and its notification to the relevant tax office on the BS form are not by themselves capable of proving the existence of the receivable, and that the claimant is obliged to prove that the goods stated in the invoice were delivered or the service was performed” (translation)

Failure to object within eight days does not mean that the goods were delivered

Court of Cassation Decision
11th Civil Chamber, E. 2024/6090, K. 2025/3953, 03.06.2025 (finding of the Regional Court of Appeal; upheld by the Chamber)

“that the mere absence of an objection to the invoice within eight days of its notification does not mean that the goods stated in that invoice had been delivered to the buyer, and that the seller must separately prove that it delivered the goods stated in the invoice to the buyer or its legal representative … that the invoices were not declared to the tax office on the BA form either, and that the recording of the invoices in the claimant’s books is not by itself sufficient to prove the receivable” (translation)

The buyer’s book entry or BA notification creates a presumption of delivery

Court of Cassation Decision
11th Civil Chamber, E. 2022/7519, K. 2024/1622, 29.02.2024 (finding of the Regional Court of Appeal; upheld by the Chamber)

“that where the invoices subject to the enforcement proceedings and the action are recorded in the defendant’s commercial books, or have been notified on BA forms to the tax office to which the defendant is attached, it must be accepted that the goods in the invoices were delivered to the defendant; that in a contract of sale the burden of proving delivery of the goods lies with the seller and the burden of proving payment of the price lies with the buyer … that the BA notification constitutes a presumption of the purchase of goods, and that the defendant could not prove the contrary of this presumption or that the invoice amounts had been paid” (translation)

A buyer that booked the invoice must prove an “accommodation invoice” defence; interest requires default

Court of Cassation Decision
11th Civil Chamber, E. 2024/6925, K. 2025/4700, 01.07.2025 (reasoning of the first-instance court; upheld on appeal and cassation)

“that the defendant is therefore obliged to prove its defence that it entered the invoices in its commercial books as an accommodation although it had not received the goods in return for them … that for default interest to run on receivables arising from a contractual relationship it is not sufficient for the receivable to be due; the debtor must be put in default by notice, or the payment date must have been definitely fixed by the parties under the second paragraph” (translation)

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an invoice evidence on its own?

An invoice is evidence, but on its own it does not prove the receivable. According to decisions upheld by the Court of Cassation, the seller must separately prove that the goods in the invoice were delivered or the service was performed.

Does an invoice without a delivery note prove the claim?

Without a signed delivery note, delivery must be proven by other evidence. In upheld decisions, delivery was accepted where the invoice was recorded in the buyer’s commercial books or notified by the buyer on the BA form.

Does recording an invoice in one’s books mean accepting the debt?

An entry in the buyer’s own commercial books is evidence against the buyer; in upheld decisions, delivery was accepted for invoices recorded in the books. A buyer claiming that it did not receive the goods or that the invoice was recorded as an accommodation must prove this itself; the burden of proving payment also lies with the buyer.

Are BA-BS forms evidence?

Yes, but their weight depends on who made the notification. An invoice notified by the buyer on the BA form was treated in upheld decisions as a presumption of purchase; the seller’s own BS notification alone does not prove the receivable.

If no objection is made to an invoice, does the debt become final?

No, not automatically. Failure to object within eight days has a consequence regarding the content of the invoice (Commercial Code art. 21/2), but, as emphasised in an upheld decision, it does not mean that the goods were delivered; the seller must prove delivery separately.
