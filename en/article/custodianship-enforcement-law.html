Custodianship in Turkish Enforcement Law: Safekeeping, Liability and Liquidation (EBL Art. 88, TCK Art. 289)

Short answer: A custodian (yediemin) is the person to whom the enforcement office formally hands over an attached movable to keep it until sale or release. The custodian may be the debtor, the creditor, a third party holding the asset, or a custodian depot licensed by the Ministry of Justice. The custodian must keep the asset with care and return it in its previous condition whenever the enforcement office requests. Breach of this duty triggers both civil liability (EBL art. 358) and criminal liability (Law No. 5237 art. 289).

This article explains Turkish law. It is an English translation of the Turkish original; in case of any discrepancy, the Turkish text prevails.

What Is a Custodian? Concept and Legal Nature

“Yed-i emin” literally means “trusted hand”. In enforcement law the custodian answers the question of whose hands the attached asset physically stays in. Attachment removes the asset legally from the debtor’s power of disposal; safekeeping measures ensure that, until the sale, the asset is not lost, does not lose value and is available on the sale date. Because the enforcement office cannot keep every asset itself, it shares this task with a custodian within the limits drawn by the law.

The Court of Cassation’s Criminal General Assembly characterises custodianship as a type of the contract of deposit (safekeeping) known to private law: the depositary undertakes to keep the asset in a safe place and to return it on request. Enforcement custodianship, however, arises not from a contract but from an official act of the enforcement office; the custodian takes on a public safekeeping function and is bound to it by the delivery record. A contractual “deposit with a stakeholder” agreed between private parties must therefore be distinguished from an official delivery by the enforcement office. The Criminal General Assembly has stressed that the offence of abuse of the duty of safekeeping can only arise from an official delivery.

The custodian is not the owner of the attached asset; ownership remains with the debtor until the sale. The custodian may not use, lease, transfer or relocate the asset. These prohibitions are not listed one by one in the statute; they follow from the purpose of safekeeping and from the prohibition of “disposal contrary to the purpose of delivery” in Law No. 5237 art. 289.

Safekeeping of Attached Movables

The core of the safekeeping regime is EBL art. 88. Cash, banknotes, bearer instruments, bills of exchange and other endorsable instruments, as well as gold, silver and other valuables, are kept by the enforcement office; enforcement and bankruptcy offices keep them in their own safes and, where necessary, in rented bank safes (EBL art. 9). Other movables are taken into safekeeping with the cost collected in advance from the creditor. If the creditor consents, the asset may be left temporarily with the debtor or a third party on condition that it is delivered on request.

Statutory Provision — Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law (Law No. 2004) art. 88 (Movables) (unofficial translation)

“Other movable assets are taken into safekeeping with the cost collected in advance from the creditor. If the creditor consents, they may be left temporarily with the debtor or a third party, on condition that they are delivered whenever requested. Where movables held by a third party are attached, they are left with the third party as custodian if the third party accepts. Assets that have been attached but not taken into safekeeping are taken into safekeeping, or made ready for delivery to the purchaser at auction, upon a request for sale; otherwise no sale may be made. … Attached assets are kept in licensed custodian depots belonging to natural or legal persons authorised by the Ministry of Justice. … The fees for the safekeeping stage of attached assets are determined by a tariff issued by the Ministry of Justice.”

The sentence of the article amended by Law No. 7343 (adopted 24.11.2021) is important in practice: an attached asset not yet taken into safekeeping must, upon a request for sale, be either taken into safekeeping or made ready for delivery to the auction purchaser; otherwise no sale can be made. For registered motor vehicles EBL art. 106 is reserved. The enforcement office may also take into safekeeping assets pledged to a third party; assets covered by a commercial enterprise pledge may be taken into safekeeping only after their sale has been ordered.

How an attachment is physically carried out and the procedure to follow where no custodian can be found are outside the scope of this article; that subject is explained in detail in our separate article on carrying out an actual attachment where no custodian is available.

Who Can Be a Custodian?

The debtor as custodian

The most common situation in practice is leaving the attached goods with the debtor as custodian. EBL art. 88 makes this subject to the creditor’s consent. This saves the creditor transport and storage costs but increases the risk of loss. A debtor-custodian benefits from a reduction under criminal law because he or she owns the asset (Law No. 5237 art. 289/1); ownership, however, gives no freedom of disposal.

A third party as custodian

Where a movable held by a third party is attached, it is left with that third party as custodian if the third party accepts (EBL art. 88). An asset held by a third party claiming ownership or another right in rem is likewise not taken into safekeeping if that person accepts custodianship (EBL art. 99). Where the debtor and a third party hold the asset jointly, the asset is not taken into safekeeping if the third party accepts custodianship; if the enforcement court orders the proceedings to continue, it may be taken into safekeeping (EBL art. 97/A). These rules are closely linked to third-party ownership claims, which are examined in our article on third-party ownership claims in enforcement proceedings.

The creditor as custodian

The law does not prohibit the creditor from acting as custodian, but given the conflict of interest between creditor and debtor this option should be used with caution. A creditor-custodian who uses the asset for purposes other than the sale is liable like any other custodian.

A licensed custodian depot

The principal statutory rule is that assets taken into safekeeping are kept in licensed custodian depots belonging to natural or legal persons authorised by the Ministry of Justice (EBL art. 88). Authorised persons may, with the Ministry’s approval and provided the same standards are met, delegate their authority to sub-operators; such delegation does not remove the authorised person’s liability.

Licensed Custodian Depots and the Regulation

The establishment, minimum standards, security, insurance, licensing, supervision and administrative sanctions of licensed depots are governed by the Regulation on Licensed Custodian Depots issued by the Ministry of Justice (Official Gazette: 23.08.2016). The Regulation has been amended several times since publication, including an amendment to the fee provisions published in the Official Gazette of 21.12.2023. Under the Regulation the licence holder is liable for damage arising while services are performed; if damage occurs to goods in the depot, the licence holder must immediately notify the enforcement office of the situation and the estimated amount of loss.

Statutory Provision — Regulation on Licensed Custodian Depots art. 17 (Liability for damage) (unofficial translation)

“(1) The licence holder is liable for damage arising during the performance of the services within the scope of the licence. (2) Where damage occurs to goods in the depot, the licence holder shall immediately notify the relevant enforcement office of the situation and the estimated amount of the loss.”

The Regulation requires the licence holder to ensure compensation for damage to goods inside the depot or during transport to the depot or to the sale area, to monitor whether the estimated value of the goods exceeds the insured amount and to insure the excess. The depot operator must insure goods in safekeeping against risks such as fire, earthquake, flood, theft and breach of trust. When goods are admitted, their condition is documented by photographs or video; conformity with the attachment record, whether vehicles and machines are in working order and any missing parts are recorded. Goods may leave the depot only on the written instruction or decision of the enforcement office, against an exit receipt.

Among the conditions for a licence is the absence of a final conviction for offences such as abuse of the duty of safekeeping. Where goods must be transferred to a new depot operator, the fee accrued up to delivery is paid by the enforcement office to the existing depot owner out of the safekeeping advance.

Custodian Fees and Safekeeping Costs

In the first stage the creditor bears the safekeeping cost: the creditor must, if requested, pay in advance the costs of safekeeping, administering and operating the attached assets (EBL art. 95).

Statutory Provision — Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law (Law No. 2004) art. 95 (Safekeeping costs of attached assets) (unofficial translation)

“The creditor is obliged, if requested, to pay in advance the costs of safekeeping, administering and operating the attached assets.”

Under the Regulation on Licensed Custodian Depots the officer carrying out the attachment collects the safekeeping advance from the creditor in advance; the advance is calculated on at least six months’ safekeeping cost and, if insufficient, the creditor may be given a reasonable time to top it up. Depot fees are set by the Ministry’s Evaluation and Fee Determination Commission, having regard to market conditions; the tariff is published in the Official Gazette every December and enters into force on 1 January. As at the date of this article, the latest tariff was published in the Official Gazette of 20.12.2025. The depot operator may not demand any fee from the parties beyond that determined by the enforcement office; towing fees and car-park fees until delivery to the depot officer for vehicles seized by the police on an enforcement office’s attachment annotation are treated in the same way.

Who ultimately bears the cost is a separate question. Unless the law provides otherwise, all fees and costs are borne by the debtor and are collected from the debtor without any further judgment (EBL art. 15); safekeeping costs advanced by the creditor are therefore charged to the debtor as enforcement costs. An important exception is EBL art. 110: if the sale is not requested within the statutory period, or a request for sale is withdrawn and not renewed in time, the attachment lapses, and the creditor who caused the attachment to lapse is liable for all costs relating to that asset, including attachment and safekeeping.

Statutory Provision — Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law (Law No. 2004) art. 110 (Lapse of attachment) (unofficial translation)

“If the sale of an asset is not requested within the statutory period, or … the request is withdrawn and not renewed within the statutory period, the attachment over that asset lapses. … The creditor who caused the attachment to lapse under the first paragraph is liable for all costs relating to that asset, such as the imposition of the attachment and safekeeping.”

Following the amendment by Law No. 7343, the period for requesting a sale is one year from attachment; for an asset whose sale was requested within the year but which could not be sold at auction, the period is extended by one more year for the creditor who requested the sale (EBL art. 106). For assets whose safekeeping is costly or whose value falls quickly, the enforcement officer may order a sale at any time (EBL art. 113); this power should be used for goods waiting a long time with a custodian and losing value.

Where the custodian is the debtor or a third party, the fee should be set in the attachment record. Under the Enforcement and Bankruptcy Regulation, the record must state the custodian’s name, capacity and address and the fee set for the custodian or, if none was set, the reason. Failure to set a fee causes disputes later: in a case where no fee was agreed at delivery, the 4th Civil Chamber held that the custodian fee was not liquidated and that no enforcement-denial compensation could therefore be awarded in an action for annulment of objection; the same decision recounts the Civil General Assembly’s view that the civil court of first instance hears such a claim (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2023/490, K. 2023/6865, 23.05.2023).

Seizure of Vehicles and Custodian Car Parks

For registered vehicles the attachment is first entered in the register; to obtain physical possession the enforcement office asks the police for seizure. Vehicles seized by the police are delivered to the nearest enforcement office within three working days at the latest, and the receiving office notifies the office that requested the seizure (EBL art. 88). In practice the vehicle is towed to a licensed custodian car park; under the Regulation all features of the seized vehicle and any missing parts are recorded on delivery to the depot officer and a copy of the record is sent to the enforcement office.

For vehicles the law links safekeeping to the sale stage: safekeeping, valuation and the request for sale must be made together and all related costs paid together and in advance; otherwise the request for sale is deemed not to have been made.

This rule responds to the problem of vehicles waiting for years in custodian car parks without a sale being requested. For the creditor it means that having a vehicle seized and towed is not a way of holding it without requesting a sale: if the sale period is missed the attachment lapses and the accumulated costs may remain with the creditor under EBL art. 110.

Liquidation of Goods No Longer Requiring Safekeeping

If goods remain with the custodian after the attachment has lapsed, depots fill with ownerless goods. Law No. 7445 (Official Gazette: 05.04.2023) introduced EBL art. 88/A, which subjects this problem to a systematic liquidation procedure: goods held by a custodian whose underlying attachment has lapsed are liquidated of the office’s own motion by the enforcement office where the proceedings were brought, and the liquidation details are published on UYAP.

Statutory Provision — Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law (Law No. 2004) art. 88/a (Liquidation of goods no longer requiring safekeeping) (unofficial translation)

“Goods held by a custodian whose underlying attachment has lapsed are liquidated of its own motion under this article by the enforcement office of the place where the proceedings were brought. … The enforcement office serves notice on the debtor that, within ten days of service, the debtor may take delivery of the goods by paying the custodian fee determined under the tariff, failing which the goods will be liquidated under the following paragraphs. … No right of retention may be exercised against the transfer on the basis of the custodian fee claim. The existence of a custodian fee does not prevent the transfer of ownership or the related procedures.”

Liquidation follows a staged sequence. If the goods are attached under Law No. 6183, the tax collection office is given one month before notice is served on the debtor; if the goods are not in free circulation for customs purposes, the customs administration is given the same period. The debtor is then given ten days to take delivery by paying the custodian fee; failing this, the pledgee is given ten days; registered goods not previously offered for sale are sold by electronic auction of the office’s own motion.

If the goods cannot be liquidated in these ways, the custodian’s turn comes: if within ten days the custodian pays the balance remaining after deducting the custodian fee from forty per cent of the value determined in the last two years (or, failing that, set by the enforcement office), the file is sent to the enforcement court for transfer of ownership to the custodian. If the custodian does not take registered goods, a transfer to the state-owned Machinery and Chemical Industry company (MKE A.Ş.) at scrap value is pursued; failing that, the goods are transferred free of charge to the Turkish Red Crescent. The enforcement court decides within ten days on the file, finally; the decision passes ownership and removes all attachments and pledges. Tax, fine and premium debts of the goods remain with the debtor. Liquidation costs are covered first from the advance on file and otherwise from the Ministry of Justice budget; any shortfall is notified to the tax collection office for collection from the debtor.

The Regulation on the Liquidation of Goods No Longer Requiring Safekeeping under EBL art. 88/A was published in the Official Gazette of 27.12.2025.

EBL provisional art. 20, also introduced by Law No. 7445, had created a faster liquidation procedure without notice to the interested parties for goods whose attachment had lapsed before that Law entered into force, with the custodian paying thirty per cent. The Constitutional Court annulled that provisional article in its entirety (Constitutional Court, 2024/205 E., 2025/88 K., 27.03.2025; Official Gazette: 04.06.2025). It held that the absence of any mechanism allowing the debtor, the pledgee and other attaching creditors to take part in an ex officio liquidation that can end in a transfer of ownership was incompatible with the right of property and the State’s positive obligations to protect it, and that publication on UYAP alone did not secure such participation; it also noted that EBL art. 88/A provides for notice to the interested parties. Strict compliance with the notice stages is therefore part of the constitutional safeguard when that article is applied.

The Custodian’s Duty to Return and Civil Liability

The custodian’s fundamental obligation is to return the goods: a third party must return attached goods left with it, in their previous condition, whenever the enforcement office requests. A custodian who cannot prove that the goods perished or were lost for a reason not attributable to it is made to pay, of the office’s own motion and without any judgment, the value previously determined; this is a sanction separate from criminal proceedings. A complaint to the enforcement court against the compensation order lies within the period in EBL art. 16.

Statutory Provision — Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law (Law No. 2004) art. 358 (Goods left with a third party) (unofficial translation)

“A third party is obliged to return the goods attached in enforcement and left with it, in their previous condition, at the moment they are requested by the enforcement office. Where a third party cannot prove that the goods so left perished or were lost for a reason not attributable to it, the values previously determined are, in addition to criminal proceedings, recovered of the enforcement office’s own motion without any need for a judgment. Those liable to pay these values may complain to the enforcement court against the enforcement office’s request and decision within the period set out in article 16.”

Because this power is exceptional, it is construed narrowly. The 12th Civil Chamber annulled a notice served on a custodian who had not brought a vehicle to the sale on the sale date, stating that the custodian was liable for its value and that enforcement would continue if not paid: the vehicle was still with the custodian, it had neither perished nor been lost, and the enforcement office had set neither a custodian fee nor the cost of bringing the goods to the sale, so the conditions of EBL art. 358 were not met (see the principle box below).

Losses outside the narrow scope of EBL art. 358 are pursued by an action for damages under the general rules: the custodian may be liable to the owner and the creditor for damage, loss of value or delayed return caused by its fault. The liability of a licensed depot operator is additionally regulated by the Regulation. As to the division of work at the Court of Cassation, the 4th Civil Chamber referred a dispute concerning a custodian’s civil liability to the 3rd Civil Chamber, relying on the Grand General Assembly’s division-of-work decision of 26.06.2025 No. 2025/1 (4th Civil Chamber, E. 2026/9533, K. 2026/7999, 02.07.2026).

State Liability

Where enforcement officers are at fault in the loss of or damage to an attached asset, the injured person sues the State rather than the officer directly; the State’s right of recourse against the officer at fault is reserved, and the ordinary courts hear such actions.

Statutory Provision — Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law (Law No. 2004) art. 5 (Liability) (unofficial translation)

“Actions for damages arising from the fault of officers of enforcement and bankruptcy offices may be brought only against the administration. The State’s right of recourse against officers at fault in causing the damage is reserved. These actions are heard by the ordinary courts.”

This is fault-based liability: an unlawful and faulty act of the officer, damage and an adequate causal link are required cumulatively. The 3rd Civil Chamber applied these conditions to a bus that could not be returned from a custodian car park although the debt had been paid: an enforcement officer who duly delivers a vehicle to a custodian has no duty thereafter to supervise the custodian, so the Chamber reversed the regional court decision holding the Ministry liable. The decision was by majority; the dissent argued that failing to act despite knowing of the transfer of the depot business and of the investigation into the disappearance of attached goods was negligence. The practical line is this: where the enforcement office has concrete information that a custodian is unreliable, its inaction opens the question of fault.

The action for damages becomes time-barred one year after the injured party learns of the damage and in any event ten years after the act; where the act is also an offence and criminal law provides a longer period, that period applies (EBL art. 7).

Criminal Liability: Abuse of the Duty of Safekeeping

The custodian’s criminal liability is the offence of abuse of the duty of safekeeping, regulated among the “offences against the administration of justice” in the Penal Code. Its object is pledged, attached or otherwise seized property formally delivered to the person for safekeeping.

Statutory Provision — Turkish Penal Code (Law No. 5237) art. 289 (Abuse of the duty of safekeeping) (unofficial translation)

“(1) A person who disposes, contrary to the purpose of delivery, of pledged or attached or otherwise seized property formally delivered to him or her for safekeeping is punished with imprisonment from three months to two years and a judicial fine of up to three thousand days. If the person is the owner of the property, the penalty is reduced by half. (2) The penalties of a person who returns the property forming the object of the offence defined in the first paragraph before prosecution begins or, if this is not possible, pays its value, are reduced by four fifths. (3) A person who causes the loss or deterioration of pledged or attached or otherwise seized property formally delivered to him or her for safekeeping by acting contrary to the duty of care and diligence is punished with a judicial fine.”

The elements of the offence have been clarified by case law:

  • Formal delivery is required. The Criminal General Assembly defines formal delivery as the transfer of possession of the property to the offender by the competent public authority in accordance with the legislation; where delivery is made within a private-law relationship such as a bank’s pledge annotation under a vehicle loan, the offence does not arise and the matter is a civil dispute.
  • Not bringing the goods to the sale is not in itself an offence. According to the Criminal General Assembly the custodian has no obligation to take the goods to the sale venue fixed by the enforcement office; return is requested at the place of safekeeping, so failure to bring the goods to the sale cannot be regarded as “disposal contrary to the purpose of delivery”. Conversely, failing to return the goods when the enforcement office requests them at the custodian’s address may constitute the offence; the 2nd Criminal Chamber likewise assessed the non-return of attached goods left with a custodian under Law No. 5237 art. 289/1 (2nd Criminal Chamber, E. 2025/8593, K. 2026/6300, 08.04.2026; the case was discontinued as time-barred).
  • Where the custodian owns the property, the penalty is halved. Failure to apply this reduction where the debtor is custodian of his or her own property is made a ground for reversal in the interest of the law.
  • The negligent form is punished separately. A custodian who causes the loss or deterioration of the property by breaching the duty of care receives a judicial fine (Law No. 5237 art. 289/3).
  • Effective repentance: the penalty of a person who returns the property before prosecution begins, or pays its value if return is impossible, is reduced by four fifths (Law No. 5237 art. 289/2).

Complaints and Time Limits

The choice of custodian, rejection of a request for safekeeping, failure to act on a request to change the custodian, the setting of fees and advances and a compensation order under EBL art. 358 are acts of the enforcement office; the remedy against them is a complaint to the enforcement court.

Statutory Provision — Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law (Law No. 2004) art. 16 (Complaint and conditions) (unofficial translation)

“Except for matters the law leaves to the courts, a complaint may be made to the enforcement court against acts of enforcement and bankruptcy offices on the ground that they are contrary to law or unsuited to the facts. The complaint is made within seven days of learning of those acts. A complaint may be made at any time for failure to perform a right or for leaving it pending without reason.”

The complaint period is seven days from learning of the act; in cases such as a request for safekeeping never being acted upon or being left pending, a complaint may be made at any time. If the complaint is upheld the act is set aside or corrected, and performance is ordered of a task the officer failed or delayed to perform without reason (EBL art. 17). The enforcement court applies summary procedure and, where no hearing is held, decides within ten days at the latest (EBL art. 18). A complaint does not stay enforcement unless the court so orders (EBL art. 22). In files sent under EBL art. 88/A for transfer of ownership, the enforcement court decides within ten days on the file and finally; objections in the liquidation process should therefore be raised at the enforcement office stage, within the periods given in the notices.

Practical Points

For the creditor: the attachment record should clearly state the custodian’s identity, capacity and address and the fee set, and record that the custodian was warned of civil and criminal liability. Before consenting to leave goods with the debtor, the risk of loss should be weighed; for valuable, portable goods a depot is preferable. The sale period must be monitored, and for vehicles the safekeeping, valuation and sale costs paid together; otherwise the attachment lapses and the safekeeping costs may remain with the creditor (EBL art. 110). If the goods are to be produced on the sale date, the transport cost and the custodian fee should be paid into the file and notified to the custodian.

For the debtor: a debtor who is custodian of his or her own goods may not sell, pledge, lease or relocate them; if relocation is unavoidable, the enforcement office should be informed in advance and a change of custodian requested. Once the attachment has lapsed, the goods should be collected by paying the custodian fee before they enter liquidation under EBL art. 88/A, and the ten-day period in the liquidation notice should not be missed.

For third parties and depots: a third party who accepts custodianship must return the goods in their previous condition whenever requested; asking for the fee to be set in the record prevents later disputes. No right of retention may be exercised on the basis of the custodian fee in the liquidation process.

Court of Cassation Decisions

The decisions below concern the limits of the offence, the reduction for an owner-custodian, the conditions for ex officio recovery under EBL art. 358 and State liability under EBL art. 5. The full texts were read from the official case-law database.

Not taking attached goods to the sale venue is not abuse of the duty of safekeeping

Court of Cassation Decision
Criminal General Assembly, E. 2018/299, K. 2018/394, 02.10.2018 (General Assembly’s reasoning on a judgment of resistance; reversal)

“since it may be said that the custodian acts in breach of its obligation if it does not return the attached property although the person who delivered it applies to the custodian’s address and requests its return … the custodian has no statutory obligation to take the attached goods to the sale venue, and the failure to take the attached property to the sale venue cannot be regarded as a disposal within the material element of the offence defined in article 289/1 of the Penal Code” (translation)

The offence requires “formal delivery” of the property by the competent public authority

Court of Cassation Decision
Criminal General Assembly, E. 2022/363, K. 2024/238, 12.09.2024 (General Assembly’s reasoning; vehicle subject to a bank’s pledge annotation)

“The attached, pledged or otherwise seized property forming the object of the offence must have been formally delivered to the offender. Formal delivery means, by the competent public authority in accordance with the legislation … since formal delivery cannot be spoken of where the property was delivered within a private-law relationship (by a pledge annotation in favour of the participating bank on the registration of the vehicle under the vehicle loan used)” (translation)

The penalty of a debtor who is custodian of his or her own property must be halved

Court of Cassation Decision
2nd Criminal Chamber, E. 2023/19480, K. 2023/10062, 27.12.2023 (reversal in the interest of the law)

“in the specific case, rendering judgment as written without considering that, because the convicted person was the owner of the property delivered to him as custodian, the penalty imposed had to be reduced under the second sentence of article 289/1 of Law No. 5237, is contrary to the Law, and the request for reversal in the interest of the law has been found well-founded.” (translation)

Ex officio recovery under EBL art. 358 requires perishing or loss; a notice is unlawful where no fee or transport cost was set

Court of Cassation Decision
12th Civil Chamber, E. 2017/1637, K. 2018/5912, 06.06.2018 (the Chamber’s reversal reasoning)

“Accordingly, since it is understood that the attached vehicle is in the custodian’s hands, that there is no perishing or loss, and that the enforcement office set neither a custodian fee nor money and costs for bringing the movables to the sale venue, the conditions of article 358 have not been met; the notice served on the custodian under that article is unlawful and, instead of upholding the complaint and annulling the notice, rendering judgment as written is erroneous.” (translation)

The enforcement officer who delivers goods to a custodian has no duty to supervise the custodian (EBL art. 5)

Court of Cassation Decision
3rd Civil Chamber, E. 2023/1608, K. 2023/2407, 26.09.2023 (the Chamber’s reversal reasoning; by majority)

“For the administration to be held liable for damage caused by officers of enforcement and bankruptcy offices, the officer causing the damage must have acted unlawfully in performing his duty, the officers must have acted with fault, the person concerned must have suffered damage through the faulty act, and there must be an adequate causal link between the faulty act and the damage. … By delivering the vehicle to the custodian, responsibility thereafter lies with the custodian, and the enforcement officer has no duty such as supervising the custodian.” (translation)

Frequently Asked Questions

My attached goods were left with me as custodian; may I use them?

No. Even an owner-custodian may not sell, pledge, lease or relocate the goods; any disposal outside the purpose of safekeeping may be an offence under Law No. 5237 art. 289. For an owner-custodian the penalty is halved.

What happens if I do not bring the goods to the sale venue on the sale date?

According to the Criminal General Assembly, the custodian has no obligation to take the goods to the sale venue; this alone is not abuse of the duty of safekeeping. However, failing to return the goods when the enforcement office requests them where they are kept may be an offence and may trigger civil liability under EBL art. 358.

Who pays the custodian fee?

Safekeeping costs and advances are first collected from the creditor (EBL art. 95) and are, as a rule, charged to the debtor as enforcement costs (EBL art. 15). But if the attachment lapses because the sale was not requested in time, the creditor who caused this is liable for all costs including safekeeping (EBL art. 110). Licensed depot fees follow the Ministry tariff.

The attachment has lapsed but my vehicle is still in a custodian car park; what should I do?

The debtor may collect the vehicle by paying the custodian fee under the tariff; otherwise ex officio liquidation under EBL art. 88/A begins. The debtor is given ten days from service of notice. The goods may ultimately be sold, pass to the custodian, be transferred as scrap to MKE A.Ş., or be transferred free of charge to the Red Crescent.

Whom do I sue if goods are lost in a custodian depot?

An action for damages lies under the general rules against the custodian and the licensed depot operator; the enforcement office may also recover the value, of its own motion, from a custodian who cannot prove perishing or loss (EBL art. 358). If enforcement officers were at fault, the action is brought against the State, not the officer (EBL art. 5).

How can I challenge enforcement office acts concerning the custodian?

By a complaint to the enforcement court within seven days of learning of the act (EBL art. 16); where a right is not performed or is left pending without reason, a complaint may be made at any time. A compensation order under EBL art. 358 may also be challenged by complaint within the same seven days.
